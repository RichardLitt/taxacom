At 12:43 PM 9/3/01 -0400, you wrote:
(...)
>Here I think the difference in perspective concerns what is a 'phylogeny.'
>In my perspective a phylogeny is the evolutionary history of lineages.
>Lineages have a biological and spatial history. A phylogeny that is
>rendered simply as a set of biological relationships without incorporation
>of spatial relationships is, to my mind, only a partial representation of
>the phylogeny. A natural classification to my mind is one which is
>cognizant of both biological and spatial relationships.

One of the reasons why I find Panbiogeography difficult to understand may
be that it uses a lot of terms in a sense that is distinctly different from
common usage. Thus,  Panbiogeography builds a "parallel universe'  of
terms, which is hardly accessible from this one.
Thus, "dispersal" does not mean dispersal, and "tracks" are not tracks,
"homology" is not homology (not quite, at least), "center of origin" is not
center of origin (or is it?), "main massing" need not be main nor massing
(see p. 79, where a single occurrence in the Comoros is treated as a "main
massing"),  and now "phylogeny" turns out to be not phylogeny (but note how
in the examples of Ch. 4 the term phylogeny seems to be used in its
conventional meaning). I realize that such terminological confusion is
inherent in the establishment of any new research program, but I would have
expected a more coherent account of these terms in a book of this kind,
summarizing 40 years of research after the first establishment of its
research program.

Again - how different must two patterns be to be distinguishable? My
earlier question about this was ignored by John Grehan. I see a lot of
light between Mayr's statement that different dispersal abilities result in
totally different distribution patterns (did he really make such a
catergorical statement? I do not have the reference at hand) and Grehan's
position which seems to be that there can never be an objective criterion
to distinguish such patterns.
