>That is curious, considering the content of chapter 4, which starts with:
>"An understanding of geographical evidence is *basic* to interpreting the
>phylogeny, systematics and taxonomy of species and higher taxa", and in
>the same vein on the following page:

I do not see that as to mean that biogeographic analysis is not to be
informed by phylogenetic analysis based on other [non spatial] characters.

>"The panbiogeographic project (...) advocates that biogeographic analysis
>is a necessary *prerequisite*  for a coherent presentation of phylogeny
>and constructioin of a natural classification (...)" (p. 89). (*emphasis*
>mine).

Here I think the difference in perspective concerns what is a 'phylogeny.'
In my perspective a phylogeny is the evolutionary history of lineages.
Lineages have a biological and spatial history. A phylogeny that is
rendered simply as a set of biological relationships without incorporation
of spatial relationships is, to my mind, only a partial representation of
the phylogeny. A natural classification to my mind is one which is
cognizant of both biological and spatial relationships.

>The chapter then continues with defending the position that heritable
>characters and geographical occurrence can be treated with equal standing
>("Geographical distributions as a Systematic Character") and concludes
>with "(...)a natural classification can only be established after, not
>before, a biogeographic analysis." (p. 113).

Treating heritable [presume biological] characters and geographical
occurrence with equal standing in my view does allow for each to
potentially inform the other.


>Moreover, and pehaps more telling, Craw, Grehan and Heads give a great
>many examples and illustrations, but only a very small minority of these
>refer to explicit hypotheses about phylogeny. Most of this minority is
>taken up by examples in Ch. 4, and deal with the prediction of
>phylogenetic relations from biogeographic patterns. Phylogenetic data as
>input for biogeographic analysis is obviously not what the book is about.

Agreed, phylogenetic (biological) relationships is not the central theme of
the book.


>Would not that be enough to dismiss the claim that "their distiributions

>Perhaps this illustrates the rather arbitrary way in which Panbiogeography

Since similarity of distribution range is not part of panbiogeographic
method the illustration of similar in the examples does not characterize
the way 'panbiogeography' uses similarity. Perhaps I should have asked Mayr
to illustrate his original assertions of 'totally' different distributions
(assuming he would have bothered to respond) and used his criteria for
illustrative purposes. Problems of similarity aside, the point remains that
dispersal ability is not a predictor of biogeographic homology.
