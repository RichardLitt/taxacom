The point of view expressed by Loius is extremely rare nowadays (and in the
world of higher primate systematics there would be a call for the straight
jacket). There was a posting for a PhD project in the last week that did
indeed give an 'and/or' possibility for the systematic project being
morphological or molecular, but this is rare and one wonders if any go for
morphological given the career opportunities. In US I suspect that the only
places where morphological systematics has survived to any degree at all is
in museums, but in many of those institutions the support for morphological
research has declined or has been eliminated altogether.

The ignorance of morphology is perhaps nowhere better illustrated than for
higher primates where comparative morphology remains largely stuck in the
1960's when the progress made over the preceding decades, much of it from
German researchers, came to a screaming halt. Since then there have been
various morphological studies, but often superficial and taxon limited and
often poorer quality than the earlier works. Even for human anatomy there
is little known about the precise morphology of many anatomic features (you
would think it would be otherwise), especially for variability. As for the
apes the situation is a catastrophic disgrace. Now we are supposed to
believe in the truth that chimpanzees are our nearest relative even thought
here is virtually nothing in morphology to support that. And we are
supposed to believe the orangutan is our most distant great ape relative
even though it shares specialized, and often unique (within primates)
similarities in anatomy, behavior, and physiology - including aspects of
reproductive biology that most people think of as almost defining what is
to be human. The state of comparative morphology in higher primates is so
pathetic that it was an entomologist (my wife), who first discovered that
our external ear is uniquely most like that of an orangutan. Imagine all
those primate biologists being so blind as not to be able to see the
obvious.

I would again repeat my caution about contrasting morphology and genetics.
DNA comparisons for systematics analysis is not genetics in the sense
of studying inheritance and development, but a morphological study of the
positional distribution of DNA base pairs (either singly or in groups).

John Grehan
