     We've been down this road before, so I'll just summarize.  I agree that
after a complete speciation event has occurred, there are two relatively
independent lineages then exist (although some limited hybridization is
often still possible).
     However, one lineage is the original mother species and the one with
the new character(s), i.e. apomorphy(ies), is the daughter species.  If both
lineages continue to speciate, separate groups (higher taxa) are created,
which can be arbitrarily grouped into species groups, subgenera, genera, and
so forth as they continue to diverge and speciate.
      The likelihood of a true sister group split (mother and daughter
simultaneously evolving separate apomorphies) is very low, and the
probability that two such true sisters would both evolve into higher taxa
gets even more remote.  Mother-daughter relationships are the norm, and
sister-sister groups are the rare exception.  We only pretend (by Hennigian
convention) that mother-daughters are really sisters because it greatly
simplifies cladistic analysis, and it works well because our data sets are
so incomplete (especially for fossil forms).  In my opinion, sister groups
should be taught as a convention, not as a reality-----and it is especially
misleading to use such a convention to then argue against the reality or
"naturalness" of paraphyletic taxa.  That kind of reasoning is dangerously
circular, with false premises leading to false conclusions.  Or as Larry
Martin might say "garbage in, garbage out" (which can be just as true for
human brains as it is for computers).
                ----- Ken Kinman
