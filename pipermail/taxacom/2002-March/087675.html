>From the regulatory point of view the use of such designation as rare
or common causes problems.  Early in the history of attempts to
protect local Southern California flora the opinion of local botanists
regarding the rarity of species became entrenched in the thinking of
planning agencies.  This led to the designation of several species as
rare and requiring special treatment under CEQA. As biologists started
looking for the plants several were found to much more common that
originally thought. This caused expensive and unnecessary restrictions
to be placed on some development activities.

Errors also were made with species considered to be extirpated.  The
Quino checkerspot butterfly is an example.  When the company  work for
was developing a NCCP Subregional Plan the U.S. Fish and Wildlife
Service (Service) considered the Quino to be extirpated in the area
and refused to give it Covered status.  Now that numerous population
have been located it has become a big problem for us that could have
been avoided.  Since the Service must consider the best available
scientific evidence in listing decisions it's very important that an
adequate assessment of a species actual distribution be made prior to
opinions on rarity being published. Any statement on rarity made on
the basis of limited collection efforts does not constitute a sound
basis for regulatory decision making but may used by some groups to
petition listing as Threatened or Endangered. This may result in law
suites against the Service which waste time and money that could be
better spent on their real mission.

There are good biological reasons to err on the side of the species in
regulatory decision making but the cost of making the wrong decision
can be very high.

Phil Bunch

> then others "psych" the location and continue to look in other

> have a similar habitat.  In reality, the individual specimens caught
