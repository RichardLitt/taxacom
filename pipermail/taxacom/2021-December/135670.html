Scott - I was using 'tectonics' in a very broad sense (otherwise I would
end up listing a whole lot of geological processes every time). And this
also encompasses other geological changes involving sea level
transgressions and regressions. And there can be climatic causes as well
that result in local isolation and vicariance. Heads (2014) illustrates the
impact of sea level changes for Australia where various distributions (and
ecologies) seem to match very well with former inland seas. So what you are
saying seems to fit quite well with that.

As for fossil ages. Yes, one could take a series of fossils and use that to
predict a 'soft' maximum, although if it is soft then it does not seem to
me to really be a maximum. And then we have the problem of why should an
existing fossil record be a predictor of older origins without assuming
somehow that the known fossil record is not an artifact of what happens to
be preserved or found (i.e. that the fossil record can be taken literally).
Interesting about your observations of fossils predating molecular ages -
more or less makes my point (that molecular ages do not preclude earlier
origins or somehow make earlier origins somehow 'unreasonable').

Cheers, John

> I would like to cautiously buy into this a little.

> First up I agree with what Rich said that the a-priori assumption is, if I
> get his point, a risky assumption. I would also add there is more than
> tectinics involved as with turtles in Australia for example a significant
> part of allopatry is explained by changing sea levels and not tectonics as
> gene flow follows the freshwater and some rivers connect below modern sea
> level.
>
> What I want to comment on is the way fossils are used in calibration of
> molecular data.
>
> I have spoken out on this in the past stating it is often poorly done, but
> the issue in it is often one of assumptions. Too often I see molecular
> papers on turtles using taxa they have clearly obtained from fossilworks or
> similar being used without any evidence of where in the tree they actually
> fall, they are taking the websites word for it. Not good. So evidential
> errors compound and for example in Australian chelids across three papers I
> saw a lineage age of 23mya, 36mya and 52mya.... Which is interesting when
> there are published fossils of chelids that are 106mya from Lightning
> Ridge. So much for molecular clocks. More recent papers are better with
> dates around 135 mya though there is a fossil chelid in the QM that is
> 145mya. So its a mix and with each subsequent paper it seems to improve.
>
> Ok onto the issue a fissil is a hard minimum and a soft maximum, you can
> probably model the soft maximum based on average species durations, but
> this is group specific. In mammals its often said average species lasts 2
> million years. Well, maybe but I doubt that spplies anywhere else. There
> are Emydura macquarii fossils at Bluff Downs Qld that are radiometrically
> dated to 2.7 million years, so yes the modern species. There are Chodina
> fossils there too indistinguishable from modern fauna.
>
> What I think can actually help here is to stop using single fossils where
> possible and have a chain of fossils of increasing age to narrow down the
> soft maximum. Of course this assumes such material is available,
> fortunately turtles are hard and preserve well, their just understudied.
>
> So I do not think the soft maximum is as big an issue that it should be
> ignored, so long as it is recognised as variable and modelled accordingly.
>

>> It sounds like a lot of the “debate” boils down to semantics.  For
>> example, suppose a major climate event (e.g., glacial cycle causing
>> substantial shift in global sea level) altered patterns of oceanic currents
>> in such a way that it dramatically increased the probability that
>> planktonic larvae could disperse from one geographic region to another.
>> Climate conditions then reverted to the prior state with much lower
>> probability for larvae dispersal between the two geographic regions.
>> Organisms that happened to colonize new regions during the anomalous
>> conditions and were subsequently prevented from exchanging genes with their
>> source populations eventually evolved into distinct allopatric species
>> before the next time the rare climate event once again established
>> conditions for high probability larval dispersal between the two regions.
>>
>>
>>
>> Such a scenario could be “claimed” both by proponents of vicariance modes
>> of allopatry, and proponents of chance dispersal modes of allopatry.  The
>> former would point to the “vicariant” climate event as the “cause” of the
>> allopatry, which is observed in multiple species that all followed the same
>> pattern of colonization and subsequent allopatry.  The latter would retort
>> that all the climate event did was alter average probabilities for
>> long-distance dispersal – each individual founder event for each species
>> represents a unique lottery (=chance) establishment of allopatric
>> distribution.
>>
>>
>>
>> I’m not saying the hypothetical example outlined above is typical or
>> atypical in marine organisms (though my money would be on the former), I’m
>> just saying that it’s not always clear whether the cause of allopatry can
>> be scored as vicariance or as chance dispersal.  But in any case, the only
>> “imaginary” solution in this scenario is one that invokes unsubstantiated
>> tectonic events simply because one has an a-priori assumption that
>> populations cannot become allopatric as a result of chance dispersal.
