Dear Colleagues,

For your information, the term "cladonomy" was coined independently in the
same year (1997) by a botanist (Brummitt) and a zoologist (Dubois).

See A. DUBOIS, A. OHLER & R. A. PYRON (2021) New concepts and methods for
phylogenetic taxonomy and nomenclature in zoology, exemplified by a new
ranked cladonomy of recent amphibians (Lissamphibia).
Megataxa, 5: 1-738.

“La culture ce n’est pas avoir le cerveau farci de dates, de noms ou de
chiffres, c’est la qualité du jugement, l’exigence logique, l’appétit de la
preuve, la notion de la complexité des choses et de l’arduité des
problèmes. C’est l’habitude du doute, le discernement dans la méfiance, la
modestie d’opinion, la patience d’ignorer, la certitude qu’on n’a jamais
tout le vrai en partage; c’est avoir l’esprit ferme sans l’avoir rigide,
c’est être armé contre le flou et aussi contre la fausse précision, c’est
refuser tous les fanatismes et jusqu’à ceux qui s’autorisent de la raison;
c’est suspecter les dogmatismes officiels mais sans profit pour les
charlatans, c’est révérer le génie mais sans en faire une idole, c’est
toujours préférer ce qui est à ce qu’on préférerait qui fût.”
(Jean Rostand, Le droit d’être naturaliste, 1963).

>          I agree that listing Eophytidae as a subclass is a good idea.
> However, not because it would be consistent with Chase and Reveal's 2009
> classification (which I never liked).  Chase and Reveal's abstract reads in
> part: "A formal classification of the land plants that is compatible with
> the APG III classification is proposed. Previous classifications inflated
> taxonomic ranks, particularly of the angiosperms. If the major clades of
> green algae are recognized as classes, then all land plants, the
> embryophytes, should be included in a single class, here recognized as
> Equisitopsida."
>         That argument that all embryophytes should be only recognized as a
> Class makes little sense unless you can't stand paraphyletic taxa.  And
> instead of inflated taxonomic ranks, you either end up with an inflated
> number of subranks or long lists of subordinate taxa that show little or no
> structure, such as Chase and Reveal's list of 16 Subclasses of Class
> "Equisitopsida" (which is a poor name for all embryophytes and thus rarely
> used for such a major taxon).
>         Therefore they would see Ruggerio et al.'s (2015) taxonomic ranks

> The following paper came to my notice this morning:
> Dianne Edwards et al., 2021 (or 2022?): "Piecing together the eophytes ? a
> new group of ancient plants containing cryptospores". New Phytologist 233:
> 1440-1455, doi: 10.1111/nph.17703
>
> It is not often that a completely new plant group is described, although
> its existence had been suspected for some time as the producers of
> "crytospores", apparently land-produced spores that did not belong to
> either bryophytes or other known land plants. Edwards et al. erect their
> new group "Eophytidae" at subclass level, so as to be consistent with the
> treatment of Chase & Reveal, 2009 in which groups such as bryophytes,
> liverworts, etc. are recognised only at subclass level, although in other
> treatments (for example Ruggiero et al., 2015) such groups are recognised
> as phyla (=Divisions im botanical-speak).
>
> Just mentioning this since in the general world of biodiversity I see it as
> pretty significant... you can find a commentary on the Edwards et al.
> paper, written by Alexandru Tomescu, also in New Phytologist at
