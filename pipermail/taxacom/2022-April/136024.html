If you want to speed up taxonomy and have it done well, well then, maybe
consider PAYING people to do taxonomic work. Most of the taxonomists I know
are laboring away in university settings, teaching courses, trying to get
some grant money, doing committee work. I am just about finished my nearly
50 year university career and I have had only a few grants to actually
describe some species. One of those was one of the first PEET grants from
the US NSF. Otherwise, what paid the bills and employed my grad students
was ecological work. In the end I have managed to describe a bunch of
crustacean and deep-sea octocoral species, and a few worms and sponges,
etc., and do some major revisionary work, but most of that was not paid for
by anyone but me.

I remember one meeting long ago where a bunch of ecologists were
complaining that the species they worked with were STILL undescribed and
wondered why we taxonomists didn't get the descriptions done. My response:
learn the essentials of taxonomy for the group you are messing with in your
ecological work and describe the species yourself. That would also speed up
the taxonomic impediment. Ecologists for some reason divorce the taxonomic
from the ecological.

So that's my rant. Nothing new there but I think occasionally needs saying.

> I have long been critical of DNA only approaches, particularly if its using
> the mitochondrial DNA and CO1 is particularly problematic if its the sole
> gene relied on and this seems to be the case with barcoding. At least in
> vertebrates this gene and the mitochondria in general is affected by
> introgression. Species boundaries are unfortunately not as clean as we
> would hope. I would imagine the same may be true of many invertebrate taxa
> and even plants. Hence I do favor less rapid production approaches.
>
> Sure DNA has several advantages, one it requires less specialisation in the
> species in question, it just requires skill in DNA extraction and
> sequencing, the ability to punch data into an algorithm of some type. The
> perfect scenario for rapid turnover budgets of Universities. However, on
> its own what it produces is not useful. It produces a result, but I think
> taxonomy, if we want it respected as a base science, must produce useful
> results. By itself in many ways DNA and particularly barcodes, are similar
> to numerical taxonomy, it takes objectivity to the level of the absurd.
> Biota is not divided into nice discreet equal packets we can call species
> and even objective methodologies rely on assumptions. In numerical taxonomy
> and I think this applies to barcodes as well the assumption is that all
> data is equal. But species are not equal.
>
> I do accept that in megadiverse groups there is an issue of expediency
> against the biodiversity crisis. I think to deal with that requires more
> taxonomists, and more specialisations and to actually turn taxonomy into a
> viable career path that is not done just by the lucky few to score a museum
> position. Many agencies should be encouraged to fund taxonomy and employ
> taxonomists.

> Molecular analysis has fatal problems.
>
> It relies on the assumption that one species has one molecular lineage;
> there are an average of 3.5 molecular races in the group I study, and each
> can generate new morphospecies on its own, generating molecular paraphyly
> and polyphyly. No, the extra races don't go away with time.
>
> Ever wonder why ecologists tout the 30 samples rule? This is because the
> bell-shaped curve of a simple distribution contains 95% of variation at the
> inflection where the curve bends outwards (2 sigma), which is 19/20 of the
> variation. How do you ensure that you are sampling the rare variation
> occurring in 1/20 of the distribution:  sample a goodly bit more than 20,
> say maybe 30 samples. How many samples do molecular people make of their
> testing? Are the results replicable?
>
> For progenitors with multiple descendants, each descendant is paired with
> a molecular race of the progenitor with exactly the same tracking traits.
> Where are they in a molecular cladogram? Extinct? Unsampled?
> Epistemologically cancelled?
>
> Cladistics cannot detail ancestors, yet ancestor species can be quite
> obvious when you polarize morphospecies with an outgroup. New species that
> are proposed based on faulty premises, poor sampling, and lacking an
> evolutionary context are exercises in futility. Scientists stand on the
> work of others, and molecular analysis, except for the most egregious
> problems in taxonomy, is a rickety platform for prediction of
> evolution-powered changes during the sixth extinction event.

>
> Introgression certainly occurs in aquatic groundwater-dependent isopod
> crustaceans from our own data. I studied a flock of 30 species of
> Phreatoicidean isopods from Australia's northern territory (Kakadu and
> Arnhemland) and was able to separate them using morphological analysis.
> We also collected mtDNA from practically all species but I was confused
> by one of the species showing up as with the same genetics as another
> species (we completed the molecular analysis before I finished the
> monograph). This finding was not an accident because we collected data
> on as many as 5-6 specimens from each putative species. These isopods
> subsist during the dry season in highly compact populations and
> sequences for individuals varied only by 0-3 bases (panmixis assumed).
> The two species are readily identifiable morphologically (I provide a
> key to the species) but they have identical COI and 16S sequences, which
> is evidence that their entire mtGenome may also be identical (hopefully
> someday someone will check this). If you want to see our results, please
> see our recent monograph: Wilson GDF, Humphrey CL. 2020. The
> /Eophreatoicus/ Nicholls, 1926 species flock from Kakadu and Arnhem
> Land, with a description of a new genus of Amphisopidae (Crustacea :
> Isopoda : Phreatoicidea) Zootaxa 4854: 1-303. Zootaxa made a figure
> errata but I can provide a revised (unofficial) version if anyone is
> interested. The earlier molecular analysis: Wilson, G.D.F., Humphrey,
> C.L., Colgan, D.J., Gray, K.-A. & Johnson, R.N. (2009)
> Monsoon-influenced speciation patterns in a species flock
> of/Eophreatoicus/ Nicholls (Isopoda; Crustacea). Molecular Phylogenetics
> and Evolution, 51, 349?364.
