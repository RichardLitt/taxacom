Dear All,
     In my opinion, the key phrase in Dave's post is "direct creationism".
When we use the phrase "creationists", we are usually referring to "direct
creationists".
     However, many biologists who believe in evolutionary theory also
believe in the possibility that a "supreme being" initially set the laws of
evolution (and physics, chemistry, etc.) into motion.  Perhaps such
"indirect creationism" is our best argument against the more extreme "direct
creationists".
      I have many times suggested indirect creationism as an alternative to
those find evolution as an assault on their religious beliefs, and this
alternative actually strikes a chord with many.  Just another one of those
middle-ground approaches I prefer to take that can generate cooperation and
discussion where there would have otherwise been no cooperation and a
divisive attitude of "us versus them".  Therefore to me, the warfare between
theology vs. science can be ameliorated (if we work at it), even though it
is perhaps a hundred times more intense than the warfare between scientists
over cladism vs. eclecticism.  Indirect creationism is an alternative (more
moderate) choice that is FAR FAR less threatening to science education.
Moderation in all things.
        ------ Cheers,
                 Ken Kinman
P.S.  I might add that the "xenophobia" that many strict cladists have been
taught to feel toward paraphyletic taxa has a rather puritanical streak to
it that often manifests itself as "scientific discrimation and intolerance".
  Although this biological warfare is less extreme than that which has been
historically demonstrated between different religions and different races,
it a form of puritanical xenophobia nevertheless.
     In all these cases, the solutions lie in mutual understanding and
moderating extreme viewpoints.  A learned intolerance of paraphyly is an
interesting parallel to those greater problems humanity has long confronted,
especially when you hear some cladists are just waiting for all the
eclecticists "to die off".  I'm sure Quakers and many other strict religious
sects have had similar visions of eventual triumph, but extremism always
becomes marginalized in the long run.  In the short run it just creates a
lot of unnecessary confusion, intolerance, and sometimes even hatred.
