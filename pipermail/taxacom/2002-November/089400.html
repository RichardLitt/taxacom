The apparent contradiction may be resolved for me if the term 'can' or
'may' were used instead of 'will'. In the historical evolutionary context
the question becomes one of understanding how an organism comes to occur in
a particular set of locations that are not necessarily the entire range of
locations that are theoretically possible for a given set of environmental
conditions.

In evolutionary time it may well be possible for a rainforest epiphyte to
be introduced into a desert if it is able to 'adapt' within the constraints
of its current or future variation. In a less 'extreme' classic there are
plants that are disjunct between the seashore and inland localities in New
Zealand where the inland plants occur only around salt pans. A traditional
explanation has been chance dispersal from the coast. A panbiogeographic
model is for the coast to have moved away leaving some plants stranded
inland that could only continue to survive under a high salt environment.
They would have become extinct as predicted by Mathieu, but certain areas
maintained the necessary conditions through ground salt seepage.

Other organisms along the former coast did survive under the new
environmental parameters and are widespread although their distributions
today continue to track the former coastline. Classic evolutionary ecology
(not that one would generally find this in ecology textbooks, although if
anyone has seen this sort of thing cited I would be very interested to know).
