Dear Stephen,

one doesn't have to cast ones mind back very far to recall the  
essentially anarchic situation when all this was published on paper.   
Most outside the taxonomic field were impressed by size and just used  
the most comprehensive treatment around, although all the ones I use  
still contain a number of significant errors.  I suspect, but can't  
demonstrate, that these days there are more of us, we're working in  
bigger teams and are consequently a little less anarchic.

Personally I do not think that there is every going to be "one true  
authoritative source".  Its a holy grail.  Its virtue lies in the  
search not in the finding.

I also offer the comparison of a musical night in the pub.  Sometimes  
the participants click and you get good performance satisfying all  
present (a good craic).  At other times, its discordant or prone to  
melancholy.  There is never a conductor.  If you're there its not hard  
to tell the difference, although probably impossible to define exactly  
why one night was good and another was not.

At a simple, practical level the first step is make the data  
accessible so that those that know can take it to the second step by  
filtering and assembling it efficiently into reviews and compendia.  I  
do think that this is achievable in little steps each one of which  
benefits the contributor.  For the foreseeable future it is a case of  
caveat emptor: readers should make active value judgement and not be  
passive consumers.

Cheers,  Dave

> The quote to which Stephen refers is simply an observation on the
> frequency of descriptive taxonomists either presenting or defending
> the plethora of initiatives in the field of biodiversity informatics.
> Here we draw a distinction with bioinformatics, dominated by molecular
> biologists, precisely because biodiversity informatics cares about
> morphology and more particularly about nomenclature.
>
> The article was published in the Bulletin of the British Ecological
> Society and was basically an appeal that sprang from a coffee-time
> conversation at eBiosphere with an ecologist lamenting the rather low
> representation of ecologists and the other essential disciplines that
> Fabian mentions in his response.  EDIT is very keen to see taxonomy
> integrated into other activities and not to see it become a cul-de-sac
> discipline.  Our view is that we need to mobilise the data that
> taxonomists use and, wherever possible, integrate those resources into
> forms that other disciplines need. Taxonomic output is often of  
> limited
> use outside taxonomy itself (= cul-de-sac).
>
> So we want to encourage use of use wikispecies, wikipedia, EoL,
> Scratchpads, GBIF and any of the other projects that are making
> taxonomic products accessible.  We particularly want to see them

> change,
