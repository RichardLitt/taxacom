No -- I'm using "candidate" species to represent cases where we lack a
minimum threshold of confidence in our understanding of the biology of the
organisms in question to warrant recognition as a full species.  Which, as I
understand it, is how the authors of the article intended to define it. And,
so it seems, you do as well.
 
To even consider recognition as a "candidate species", we have to assume
there is some minimal level of "consistent morphological [or genotypical]
differences".  When these differences are "too small to indicate
reproductive isolation" with sufficient confidence to justify a full-species
name, then a candidate species may be warranted.  In my mind, this is
exactly the sort of situation when we ought to consider subspecies
designators.  
 
Like you, I believe that we are always open to learn more about the biology
of organisms.  That includes an understanding of the extent of reproductive
isolation.

No, alas, I fear that our time of agreement is as fleeting as a marriage

term "candidate" species to mean populations that are on the evolutionary
road to full species, but haven't got there yet. This is not AT ALL how I
was understanding the term! Surely we were talking about cases where the
evidence for full speciation IN THE PRESENT is incomplete??
 
Entomologists and arachnologists (some mites) have had to deal for centuries
with organisms that have distinct life-stage morphotypes. Very few beetles,
for example, ever get described as new species from larvae. This doesn't
seem to be a problem. With some mites, there are names for larvae as well as
adults, and a big part of the taxonomy of these groups is to eventually
synonymize those names appropriately by association of life stages. Again,
it isn't a big deal. I just don't see how inventing a new class of names
(Candidati) helps???

The people I know of who routinely need to deal with "morphospecies" fall
into the former category, for the most part.  Except, it usually has little
or nothing to do with conservation management, and more to do with large
numbers of morphotypes, for groups that tend to have more than one life
stage, and whose biology is not well understood.  It's usually the case that
insufficient information and/or expertise is available to make a confident
judgement as to whether a new species-level name is needed.
