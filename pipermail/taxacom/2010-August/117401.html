Whilst at the BMNH I remember at least 2 sepparate projects to automate species identification. Both used morphology. The dream to automate identification is pervasive and not the domain of one or another group. Whilst it might be entertaining to identify organisms it is a practical matter of workload management. "Never have so many asked so few to identify so much"

> (2) IN THEORY this is true, but:
> (2a) this is no argument for not also describing the morphology; and
> (2b) "cryptic species" seem a bit dodgy to me - maybe you can find as many of 
> them as you look for? Maybe these genetic lineages are not what we mean by 
> species, anyway? I bet most good morphospecies are a single species, and that 
> problem cases are relatively few and far between...

Species do not originate to please taxonomists. What for us might be cryptic for the organisms may be chalk and cheese. Which also brings me to the issue of the nature of cryptic species. Whereas it is possible that some cases are the result of incipient or recent speciation, character suits do not necessarily evolve in tandem and their rates often differ dramatically. So two taxa which are morphologically identical may in fact be behaviourally, biochemically, phenologically, etc. different. But when all you have is the corpses there is not much else to compare is there? So although I believe that a proportion of cases are in fact "soft" cryptics (sloppy morphologicaly work, lack of material to assess variability, ...) others will be "hard" cryptics. No wanting to sound repetitive, the Anopheles gambiae group proves that bags of money and research can come up empty-handed. Most taxa will never be scrutinised like this.
