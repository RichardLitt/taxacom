Hi John,
      Sorry I couldn't get back to this earlier.  Reading your post on frog phylogeny absolutely baffles (even shocks) me, coming from someone who lectures primatologists for putting too much emphasis on molecular (vs. morphological) data regarding humans and great apes (chimpanzees in particular).                     
       Now we have a case where two frog families apparently diverged a hell of a lot longer ago, and you pick this as a case where molecular data convinces you, even though there are NO morphological synapomorphies AT ALL shared by these frogs.  The consensus actually seems to be that their shared morphological characters are primitive (symplesiomorphies), so these "DEEP" shared molecular characters are very likely to be symplesiomorphies as well.   If there is a case where the molecular data should be questioned, it is these frog families (not chimps and humans).  
        Therefore, I would side with Green and Cannatella (1993) and Cannatella and Ford (1993) who actually found several morphological synapomorphies uniting Leiopelmatidae with more recent frogs (to the EXCLUSION of Ascaphidae, which lacks those morphological characters).  Thus they named a new clade Leiopelmatanura as sister group to the most primitive living family of frogs (Ascaphidae).    
       Not surprisingly, it is noted that one of the co-authors in Ford et al., 2006 (namely Green), disagreed with their decision to lump Ascaphidae and Leiopelmatidae together.  Keeping them as separate families would have made perfect sense, given the conflict between morphological and molecular data.  However, Green was overruled by the consensus among the authors of that particular paper.    
       So John, I would suggest that if you are so concerned about molecular data being questionable, this is clearly a case where you should be focusing your attention.  If Ascaphidae split off first, and then Leiopelmatidae split off next (before the rest of the extant frogs families), one would expect those two primitive families to not only share morphological  (primitive) symplesiomorphies, but that their shared molecular sequences would be symplesiomorphies as well.  Especially as these cladistic splits happened so long ago. 
       Frankly, it is this type of ancient divergence which has made it so difficult to use molecular data to establish the relationships of invertebrate phyla, and the relationships within some those phyla (such as Arthropoda).  If you don't pick just the right molecules to sequence, you are likely to end up confusing molecular symplesiomorphies as molecular synapomorphies (and this has no doubt happened often). 
        Looks to me like the cladistic relationships between the earliest frogs is just another case where we haven't found the right molecular sequences to discriminate between symplesiomorphy and synapomorphy to explain the similarities.  CONCLUSION: Although morphology can obviously be confusing when it comes to convergence and parallelism,  the further back in time one goes, we are just as likely (sometimes even more likely) to be confused by convergence and parallelism in molecular sequences if we don't pick the right molecules.                                                                    ---------------------------------Ken Kinman
