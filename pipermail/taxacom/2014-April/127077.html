Yes, I'm not going to quibble with Neal over minor details, but just a few final general comments: 
?
(1) It is interesting that, as far as I can tell, Hoser has never been guilty of trashing other people's names for taxa and then renaming them himself. Yet others are doing that to him;
?
(2) If there is already a name for a taxon, then, unless it is 110% certain that it is unavailable, just treat it as being available (though not necessarily as valid, of course!);
?

OK. I've lurked enough.

I've had a look at all the relevant material in Hoser's case and have
concluded that his names are indeed available. His claim that a database
was changed is actually irrelevant, since he was basing his taxa on
specimen numbers, which do indeed refer to actual specimens (see further
below on this). Someone in this thread (maybe Hoser) obfuscated the word
"occurrence" into "observation" and things got blown out of proportion
after that.

Here's how I see the situation and relevant Code compliance:

Hoser in April 2013 published a paper that described two new taxa. The
names in that paper were described in such a way so as to fulfill all of
the criteria in the Code to meet availability: he gives characters to
differentiate, he said the new taxa were "new", he based his description
on a type series, he gave individual specimen numbers for those specimens,
and he specified the type depository.

The Code Glossary says that a "type series" is whatever specimens the
author indicates as included in it. Hoser gave type numbers indicating
exactly which specimens were in his type series.

He selected one of them as a holotype.

He gave the location of the type depository.

As for actual specimens, here is where Hoser screwed things up. No. Not in
making his names available. He screwed up in his video, which has got
everyone on this thread all hot and bothered. He claims someone changed
the database and points to the HerpNet database showing the word
"occurrence" and a blank for "Preparation type".

Based on that, he concluded that someone changed the database from actual
specimens existing to "observations". There is NO word "observation" in
that database. The word is "occurrence". Which in database parlance may
have a totally different meaning -- I have seen it mean a specimen
"occurs" in the specified collection, but more data is needed to properly
fill out the form (but again -- this is IRRELEVANT, so don't focus on
this).

However, where he screwed up is by not looking at the Florida Museum
database itself, where his types are stored. In that database, there is a
column "Specimens" and the number "1" for each specimen number entry for
the numbers he used in his type series. Thus, specimens do indeed exist
and Hoser was within the definition of the Code in specifying exactly
which of these specimens was in his type series.

There is nothing in the Code that requires an author to actually handle,
see, or have in front of him actual specimens. Bibliographic reference is
allowed (which means an author is not actually "seeing" specimens), and
that has been abused in the past to make available names in a quick and
dirty fashion. By using specimens from a database, Hoser was essentially
doing the same thing -- vicariously slecting actual specimens that were
identified as X from a certain locality and he knew these to be different
from other taxa and described them as new giving the defining characters.
But he did not actually "see" the specimens.

Surely we do not condone this type of method of making available names of
new taxa, but despite the abhorrence we may have to such methods, Hoser's
names are available and his foes unfortunately knee-jerked on this one and
created unnecessary synonyms.

my 2 cents,

-Neal
