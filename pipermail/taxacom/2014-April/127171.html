Hi Wolfgang,

Like Michael, I promised myself not to get dragged in to this; but his post
(which represents my views as well) have emboldened me a bit.

The answer to your question, I think, has nothing to do with "because that's
how it's always been"; and much more to do with putting the interests of
what I think of as "long term" stability ahead of "short term" stability in
nomenclature.

A solution to whatever controversial nomenclatural issue du jour happens to
be (in this case, there seems to be an epicenter of noise in the
herpetological domain), while placating frustrated workers caught up in the
cross-fire, would necessarily involve some assertive force that operates
somewhat heavily on subjective interpretation (see Michael's post below).
It may well be that an overwhelming majority of current workers maintain a
common consensus view, to the point where such a solution would appear to
fall into the realm of "common sense"; but nevertheless the tyranny of the
(even vast) majority is still tyranny.

OK, "tyranny" is too strong of a word. But subjectivity is nevertheless a
detriment to long-term stability, even when it can serve the needs of
short-term stability. The Code is not devoid of subjectivity, of course.
This is why there are provisions to allow the Commission to exercise plenary
powers.  But the Commission has tended to maintain a very conservative
approach on such matters (witness the Drosophila example); and in my
opinion, this is the way it should be, with the priority being on long-term
stability.  In this context, "long-term" stability is served by maximizing
objectivity, and minimizing the introduction of subjectivity.  The messiest
parts of the Code (i.e., the ones that have caused the greatest problems)
are the ones that require elevated levels of subjective interpretation
(e.g., prevailing usage clauses).

It has never been, and never will be, a perfect system.  It will always be a
case of the lesser of evils.  So I don't think it's "resignation" that
Michael, and I, and others share towards the plight of the [insert group
suffering from some controversy or another]ologists; it's more a recognition
that the costs of solving the short-term stability issues via subjective
approaches, are too large in terms of their threat to long-term stability.
And quite frankly, I see a few hundred (or even a few tens of thousands of)
synonymous names as the lesser of evils against the context of a system
designed to promote long-term stability among MILLIONS of names.

Also, as others have stated or alluded to, the job of the Code is not to
eliminate bad taxonomy.  There always have been, currently are, and always
will be bad taxonomists.  It is the job of the scientific community to
establish mechanism that help promote good taxonomy and reduce bad taxonomy.
This is outside the scope of the Code.

Aloha,
Rich
