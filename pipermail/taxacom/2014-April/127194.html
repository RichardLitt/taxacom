As for sympatric speciation:

"On Rhagoletis, Feder et al. (2003) wrote: ?we infer that an
ancestral, hawthorn-infesting R. pomonella population
became geographically subdivided into Mexican and North American
isolates ?1.57 million years ago. Episodes of gene
flow from Mexico subsequently infused the North American population'.
Michel et al. (2007 Mol Ecol) suggested that
?volcanic activity in the eastern EVTM [Eje Volcanico Trans Mexicano]
may have been responsible for the initial
geographical isolation of the Mexican and northern hawthorn-fly
populations'. This is a common pattern, with allopatric
differentiation, followed by subsequent overlap and local ecological sorting."

Feder et al are clear that it is introgression and not lineage
sorting. Further, they provide a mtDNA tree so you can see that it is
not
Mexican flies moving into apples (i.e. there is only one
metapopulation in the US and not two cryptic, sympatric populations)
but Northern
flies which had some genes from a different population.
But once infused into the Northern population it was sorted and
selected along a latitudinal cline and they were an exaption (probably
amongst
others, as the authors concede) that allowed R. pomonella to exploit
apples when introduced much later.
The actual speciation only started afterwards as a consequence of the
different fruiting times, assortative mating, etc. All sympatric
processes.

In regards to the range overlap of sister species, sympatric
speciation doesn?t have to result in broad sympatry. In fact it
probably will lead to the opposite
result due to local adaptation. Focusing on the R. pomonella complex,
host races? ranges will be contained within that of their host and not
of the original R. pomonella population.
Furthermore, chromosomal inversions, although they do not preclude
mating they can facilitate local adaptation (reduced recombination)
and in
the process kickstart speciation (genetic isolation). For a recent
example check Lowry D. B, Willis J. H (2010) A widespread chromosomal
inversion polymorphism contributes to a major
life-history transition, local adaptation, and reproductive isolation.
