Hi Pierre,

You write: 'I wonder what are exactly the differences between vicariance
biogeography and panbiogeography maybe the rejection of geological
arguments by the latter...'.

In fact, panbiogeography engages much more with geology than traditional
vicariance biogeography does. E.g. Nelson & Platnick's (1981) standard
text on vicariance biogeography has long discussions about the possible
cladistic relationships among Borneo, Java and Sumatra. In contrast,
panbiogeography (like geology) does not use geographic areas as a priori
entities and instead looks at distributions  and tectonics, which turn out
to deconstruct Borneo, Sumatra and Java.

Panbiogeography has introduced many geological concepts to biogeography.
For example, it was the first to look at terranes in relationship to
distributions, in New Zealand in the 1980s. This is now a standard approach
in work on the Mediterranean. In another example, panbiogeography has
studied disjunctions along transform (strike-slip) faults. In another,
recent example, I stressed the significance of the Musicians Seamounts off
Hawaii. These are now known to be flat-topped (eroded to sea-level) and so
were former islands. I don't know of any other biogeographic work that has
mentioned these.

Other examples of geological structures where the potential biogeographic
effects were first examined by panbiogeography include the accreted
terrane/craton boundary in western Colombia, the accreted terrane/craton
boundary in New Guinea, the central African rift system (not the Great
Rift), and the Lebombo monocline in southern Africa.
