Hi Richard,

Speciation by 'chance dispersal' may well occur, but by definition would
not result in a standard distribution pattern. I'd be interested in any
possible cases.
   As for groups other than higher plants and animals, here are two
examples. Both show the same thing that has been found in many marine
groups since sequencing began - high levels of phylogeographic structure
not predicted in the dispersal paradigm.


   Molecular studies of soil rotifers found 'surprising' and 'unexpected'
structure (Robeson et al., 2011):



Local communities show *strong spatial structuring*, whereas more distant
communities are very different from one another, even in similar
environments... This world view directly contradicts the idea of EisE
[everything is everywhere] and suggests that the diversity of microbial
eukaryotes such as rotifers may be vast beyond our imagining, especially
given that endemic microbes may have *species ranges of about 100 m*...
Bdelloids are known to produce small, resistant resting stages... that
should disperse easily by wind. The fact that our results show that
bdelloids are not widely distributed implies that other microbial
eukaryotes with less resistant stages... should have even more
geographically restricted distributions...  the ability to form resistant
stages probably evolved not for dispersal but to survive periods of
unfavorable environmental conditions (i.e., dry and cold conditions)...
thereby maintaining unique local communities. [Italics added].



   Allopatry even exists in bacteria and a survey *of *the global
genus *Chroococcidiopsis
**concluded: *



Temporally scaled phylogenetic analyses showed no evidence of recent
inter-regional gene flow, indicating populations have not shared common
ancestry since before the formation of modern continents... although a
desert cyanobacterium such as *Chroococcidiopsis* may notionally possess
the characteristics for ubiquitous dispersal due to aeolian transport of
desert particulates and its desiccation/radiation tolerance..., this is not
reflected in contemporary colonization [i.e. distribution] patterns. The
existence of regionally isolated gene pools of hot and cold
*Chroococcidiopsis* variants strongly supports the concept that widespread
contemporary dispersal is not common, and that relationships reflect
ancient historical legacies. Although strong selection for hot and cold
variants has occurred, this pre-dates contemporary climatic selective
pressures... (Bahl et al., 2011).

>
> 1) Does everyone agree that there is ample evidence that some populations
> of
> organisms have been fractioned via rare vicariant events, leading to
> reproductive isolation and subsequent divergence and, ultimately,
> speciation?
>
> 2) Does everyone agree that there is ample evidence that some populations
> of
> organisms have been fractioned via rare dispersal events, leading to
> reproductive isolation and subsequent divergence and, ultimately,
> speciation?
>
> If anyone's answer to either question is "No", I would like to hear an
> explicit statement of such, accompanied by explanations that counter the
> aforementioned ample evidence (which has been, and could easily be,
> provided
> by members of this list) in specific, individual situations.
>
> If we all agree that the answer to both questions is "Yes", then can
> someone
> explain what this seemingly never-ending debate is really about?  Is it
> simply about the relative frequency of each mechanism, across broader
> biodiversity, that has historically resulted in reproductively isolated
> populations and subsequent speciation?  If so, then the next question is:
>
> 3) Are we arguing about 95/5 one way or the other, or more like 60/40 one
> way or the other?
>
> Some additional questions:
>
> 4) Is anyone willing to step up to the plate to discuss cases where
> speciation seems to have occurred in the absence of geographic isolation
> (i.e., no dispersal or vicariant events required)?
>
> 5) Does someone want to take a stab at defining the scope of "broader
> biodiversity" alluded to in the preamble to question #3 above? (There seems
> to be a lot of focus on terrestrial vertebrates and plants -- does the
> debate also include marine organisms?  What about insects?  Microbes? Can
> vicariance or dispersal explain patterns of bacteria diversity?)
>
> 6) Imagine a hypothetical(?) situation where infrequent events like global
> glaciation cycles cause substantial sea-level changes, which have dramatic
> impact to ocean currents and thereby shifts in patterns of dispersal
> potential/frequency among marine organisms with planktonic larvae -- such
> that geographically disjoint populations that have high potential gene flow
> during long periods of time, undergo long periods of time with very low
> potential gene flow.  In such cases, where speciation results during the
> low-gene-flow periods, do we score a point for vicariance, or for
> dispersal?
