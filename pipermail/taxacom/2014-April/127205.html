Hi Michael,

thank you for your comment

for me this makes just more similarity
between panbiogeography and vicariance biogeography
(I mean similarity in methodological principles,
not the practice of such or such biogeographer).

As for pre-defined biogeographical areas,
recent developments make use of grids of different spatial grains
so that the areas of endemism emerge from and objective analysis
of distributional data -
And again, this can make no methodological difference
(both methods can use such an approach).

As for "orthogenesis", for me it's a completely different matter -
hardly connected with historical biogeography
("Croizat dixit" is not sufficient for me);
mutation is the primary evolutionary process
(it must logically preceed any possible selection) -
but orthogenesis can't be invoked /a la legere/,
and certainly not as way to discard standard phylogenetic patterns
when they would contradict some panbiogeographical scenario
(hence tending to make it unfalsifiable)

just trying to make up my methodological mind,
nothing personal

Best,
Pierre


Le 21/04/2014 00:00, Michael Heads a ?crit :
> Hi Pierre,
> You write: 'I wonder what are exactly the differences between 
> vicariance biogeography and panbiogeography maybe the rejection of 
> geological arguments by the latter...'.
> In fact, panbiogeography engages much more 
> with geology than traditional vicariance biogeography does. E.g. 
> Nelson & Platnick's (1981) standard text on vicariance biogeography 
> has long discussions about the possible cladistic relationships 
> among Borneo, Java and Sumatra. In contrast, panbiogeography (like 
> geology) does not use geographic areas as a priori entities and 
> instead looks at distributions  and tectonics, which turn out to 
> deconstruct Borneo, Sumatra and Java.
> Panbiogeography has introduced many geological concepts to 
> biogeography. For example, it was the first to look at terranes in 
> relationship to distributions, in New Zealand in the 1980s. This is 
> now a standard approach in work on the Mediterranean. In another 
> example, panbiogeography has studied disjunctions along transform 
> (strike-slip) faults. In another, recent example, I stressed the 
> significance of the Musicians Seamounts off Hawaii. These are now 
> known to be flat-topped (eroded to sea-level) and so were former 
> islands. I don't know of any other biogeographic work that has 
> mentioned these.
> Other examples of geological structures where the potential 
> biogeographic effects were first examined by panbiogeography include 
> the accreted terrane/craton boundary in western Colombia, the accreted 
> terrane/craton boundary in New Guinea, the central African rift system 
> (not the Great Rift), and the Lebombo monocline in southern Africa.
