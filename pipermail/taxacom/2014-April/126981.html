Hi Jason,

You say you'd have preferred a definition, but I don't find definitions as
interesting as facts and ideas. Anyone can make up a definition. So I gave
you some data.

Sample size: the authors could have studied the plants for 20 years and
found a seed that dispersed 2 m rather than 1 m, but this would
hardly affect the argument. You say they'd need seeds large enough that
they could track, but they did track them - this is how they found how far
they dispersed.

You say 'taking the 1m from the article and multiplying it to get "1,5
million times" is, at best, inaccurate'. I'd be happy to change the number
- so how much wider do you calculate the distribution gap (across the
Tasman) is than the greatest dispersal distance in the plants?
You say that I 'accuse' Meudt and Bayly. All I said was: they 'concluded
that the mechanisms of the process in the two species are 'unknown' and
'hard to envisage''. That's not an accusation, and I think their point is
nicely made.

You wrote: 'you are perfectly happy (in general I mean, not
discussing the specifics Veronica sp) with accepting the existence of as
yet unknown tectonic events that can explain all biogeographical events'.
I've never said anything like that!  For example, if you read what I've
written, you'll see that I attribute great significance to major climatic
events and associated sea level changes.

Michael
