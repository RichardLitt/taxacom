Keep in mind that hydrolysis is not the only DNA killing process. Nucleases are a major problem as well and even 96% Ethanol, although displacing most of the water, does not shut down nuclease activity. This particularly applies to enzyme-rich tissue such as liver or spleen (or most internal organs, for that matter). For this reason, many vertebrate samples collected in the 'good old times' by fixing chunks of liver in ETOH cannot be used for high throughput analysis of mitochondrial DNA. I would discourage the assumption that if you simply fix your specimen in Ethanol, you are guaranteed to retain its DNA intact for ages to come. It will still degrade over time, even quicker than in a dry specimen. For arthropods that can be preserved dry for morphology, it is best not to use ethanol at all, unless there is high ambient humidity. For specimens that have to be preserved in Ethanol, it is a good idea to store them at low temperatures until they are sampled for molecular analyses.

I think that the actual culprit for trashing DNA is water. The problem 
with isopropanol is that it is not as effective for replacing the water as 
ethanol is, allowing the DNA to degrade. Similarly a low concentration of 
ethanol won't preserve the DNA. Depending on the size and composition of 
the taxon, the water from the tissues may significantly dilute the 
preservative.
