Dear John,

I am not interested in getting too much into this discussion, but will briefly add a few comments and then I will be happy to leave the readers to conclude on their own.

You used the example of one "mysterious" situation (the collection and researchers are not mentioned) to justify your point. Apparently that is enough support -and cannot be argued because we are left without any details to look at. 

I provided one example of an institution (CNC) where DNA barcoding has been going on for many years (probably since around 2004; personally I have been involved with those efforts here since 2006 and have personally sampled over 30,000 specimens for DNA barcoding). I am naming institution, insect groups (all major orders), and sample size (several dozen thousands of specimens), so you are free to judge my points as you wish.

And, respectfully, I do not agree with the statement of DNA barcoding being contentious. It might have been at the beginning -when perhaps some early proponents were too enthusiastic and others thought it would be the silver bullet for pretty much anything... over the years thing have settled down, and now is a very useful and powerful tool in many taxonomic works (among many other tools a taxonomist can use, be it morphology, ecology, ethology, etc). The OCCASIONAL specimens being lost (I capitalized the word 'occasional' to emphasize that the number lost is very low) is nothing compared with what one gains. Again, I only speak from my "experience" of describing around 250 new species of braconid wasps using a combination of morphology, biology and DNA barocoding.

By the way, one also looses specimens (and creates mess) during the process of sorting, mounting, labeling and incorporating specimens. Same happens when loaning specimens (or when borrowing specimens from other collections). DNA barcoding per se does not mess collections more than any other process related to collection manipulation for anything else. 

You are very welcome and free to disagree with DNA barcoding (and barcoding proponents). But it would help if you present your case with actual names and actual examples. I have just mentioned my side of the story and my experience with DNA barcoding. But I am open to hear yours (or that from anyone else).

Cheers,
Jose

On Mon, Apr 21, 2014 at 12:25 PM, Alex Borisenko < aborisen at uoguelph.ca > wrote: 


Hi David, 

Your critical comment below relates to two separate issues: 
1. The lack of convenient feedback tools for reporting identification errors in online data portals for DNA barcoding information; 
2. The poor quality of data submitted to such portals. 

With regards to #2, I can only say that the data are as good as we (collectively) make them. E.g., many providers of specimen provenance information do not indicate (and often do not know) the name of the identifier at the time of submission. 

With regards to #1, I must note that such tools are available. For example, the Barcode of Life Data System has an attribution section for each specimen record that denotes the name of the identifier (when available) and the name of the project manager who could be asked to address taxonomic problems within a particular dataset. E.g., see public BOLD record below (bottom right corner): 
http://v3.boldsystems.org/index.php/Public_RecordView?processid«SMS268-06 

Because of privacy/spam concerns, it is not possible to email the identifier or project manager directly from a public specimen record - this functionality is only open to registered BOLD users who are logged in. 
However, if users wish to provide instant feedback, they have the option to annotate the data record by clicking the Add Tags&Comments button just above the taxonomy - this will flag the record for future users. 
They can also add tags on the corresponding BIN page which would annotate all specimens putatively referred to a given species: 
http://v3.boldsystems.org/index.php/Public_BarcodeCluster?clusteruri=BOLD:ACF2193 

I am not sure if I fully understand your remarks about 'proper' or 'correct' identifiers. If you imply that some barcode data records are contributed by people without adequate taxonomic expertise or accreditation, I personally think that it is a strength of such large-scale data projects. 
In most other real world situations, the 'proper' and 'correct' identifiers are those who get acknowledged in specialized taxonomic revisions, whereas many of the the 'incorrect' and 'improper' ones write volumes of ecological assessment and biodiversity survey reports whose baseline data never get digitally annotated, let alone cross-referenced against museum collections or taxonomy databases. Ironically, such reports probably have more weight in environmental decision-making. I think that having information from different sources in one digital system where taxonomic inconsistencies become glaringly visible is a step in the right direction. Thank you for your efforts in trying to clean up the resulting taxonomic issues. 

Best wishes 
Alex

Quality information is critical, but it's difficult to formulate a standard 
when different taxa vary greatly in their key features. For many animals, 
dissection showing reproductive features is critical, and often challenging 
*(e.g*., microscopic). Others have key differences in a specific part. 
Barcodes have great potential for facilitating identification, but only if 
the validity of the barcode is tested for the group in question and 
specimens are carefully identified. 
As with any online compilation, content must be accompanied by appropriate 
curation. There needs to be a quick way to communicate information (such 
as corrected identification) to a responsible party. In the case of 
barcoding, where often large numbers of specimens are quickly processed, 
it's particularly important to have a clearly designated identifier for a 
particular specimen. It's frustrating to notice an error in a barcoding 
project, search through several pages to finally find some contact 
information, and then be told that the submitted identifier (the one used 
in GenBank) was not the proper identifier to enable them to tell which 
specimen had the problem. At least they could have given some sort of clue 
as to what the correct identifier would have been ("the one where you've 
got a photo of something from a different order" wouldn't do, either).

> impossibility. Specimens are always physical, or they would be no 
> specimens. 
> 
> - What comes closest in the ICZN to what some seem to call "physical 
> specimen" is "extant specimen" ("sp?cimens qui existent toujours" in the 
> French text). 
> 
> - For names published after 1999, Art.16.4.2 gives explicit additional 
> requirements for availability in the special case where the holotype or 
> syntypes are extant specimens. As this is clearly treated as a special 
> case, it is obvious that having extant specimens as types cannot be 
> understood as a general requirement for all species-group names 
> published after 1999. 
> 
> - Again, it is a complete misreading to pretend that: 
> "73.1.4. Designation of an illustration of a single specimen as a 
> holotype is to be treated as designation of the specimen illustrated; 
> the fact that the specimen no longer exists or cannot be traced does not 
> of itself invalidate the designation." 
> ...introduces an exception to a general "rule" that would say that a 
> holotype must be an extant specimen. Should such a "rule" exist, this 
> article would actually not allow any exception to it: the designation of 
> the illustrated specimen would be allowed only if the specimen is 
> extant; the designation of the illustration would have to be treated as 
> the designation of the specimen; thus, should the specimen not be 
> extant, the designation of the illustration would not be allowed. 
> 
> The truth is that there is NO such rule in the Code.
