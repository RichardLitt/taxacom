Hi All,

I've only been partially paying attention to this thread, but Jason's recent
post (which captured my own general perspective nicely) has encouraged me to
ask some questions:

1) Does everyone agree that there is ample evidence that some populations of
organisms have been fractioned via rare vicariant events, leading to
reproductive isolation and subsequent divergence and, ultimately,
speciation?

2) Does everyone agree that there is ample evidence that some populations of
organisms have been fractioned via rare dispersal events, leading to
reproductive isolation and subsequent divergence and, ultimately,
speciation?

If anyone's answer to either question is "No", I would like to hear an
explicit statement of such, accompanied by explanations that counter the
aforementioned ample evidence (which has been, and could easily be, provided
by members of this list) in specific, individual situations.

If we all agree that the answer to both questions is "Yes", then can someone
explain what this seemingly never-ending debate is really about?  Is it
simply about the relative frequency of each mechanism, across broader
biodiversity, that has historically resulted in reproductively isolated
populations and subsequent speciation?  If so, then the next question is:

3) Are we arguing about 95/5 one way or the other, or more like 60/40 one
way or the other?

Some additional questions:

4) Is anyone willing to step up to the plate to discuss cases where
speciation seems to have occurred in the absence of geographic isolation
(i.e., no dispersal or vicariant events required)?

5) Does someone want to take a stab at defining the scope of "broader
biodiversity" alluded to in the preamble to question #3 above? (There seems
to be a lot of focus on terrestrial vertebrates and plants -- does the
debate also include marine organisms?  What about insects?  Microbes? Can
vicariance or dispersal explain patterns of bacteria diversity?)

6) Imagine a hypothetical(?) situation where infrequent events like global
glaciation cycles cause substantial sea-level changes, which have dramatic
impact to ocean currents and thereby shifts in patterns of dispersal
potential/frequency among marine organisms with planktonic larvae -- such
that geographically disjoint populations that have high potential gene flow
during long periods of time, undergo long periods of time with very low
potential gene flow.  In such cases, where speciation results during the
low-gene-flow periods, do we score a point for vicariance, or for dispersal?

Aloha,
Rich
