Subject: Re: [Taxacom] Linz International Zoologucal Nomenclature Workshop


Dear all,

There indeed* is* a detailed program of the meeting. It is quite long, so I
did not post it on this forum, but I stated clearly that we would send it
to any colleague interested to attend, or just to know more about it.

Our paper on electronic publications and related matters, by 19 authors
including several Zootaxa editors, is available as open access on the
Zootaxa website. It is not difficult to see that many points raised in this
paper were not addressed in the Commission's rebuttal which only tackles a
few of them. There are other problems of several kinds with this rebuttal,
that we will discuss later.

Once again, the purpose of the Linz meeting is not to make a complete
discussion of the questions raised, but to prepare a roadmap for a special
issue of Bionomina where these questions will be addressed, and where
different, and sometimes certainly contradictory, points of view, will be
freely expressed. So interested colleagues are invited to contact us if
they think they have something to say about these questions.

We think the preparation of a new edition of the Code is a very important
issue for all taxonomists and even biologists, that should be discussed
freely, without censorship, in *published* contributions, that will remain
available in the future (as was the  case at the time of Hemming in the
BZN) and not only online. This is why we created Bionomina, as an open
uncensored forum, without page limitations, to discuss these matters fully.
We think nobody should be afraid of discussions, and that science often
progresses through confrontation of different points of view, not through
having a dogm, edicted by a few, that everybody should follow blindly.

Best wishes,

Alain

> ask some questions:
>
> 1) Does everyone agree that there is ample evidence that some populations
> of
> organisms have been fractioned via rare vicariant events, leading to
> reproductive isolation and subsequent divergence and, ultimately,
> speciation?
>
> 2) Does everyone agree that there is ample evidence that some populations
> of
> organisms have been fractioned via rare dispersal events, leading to
> reproductive isolation and subsequent divergence and, ultimately,
> speciation?
>
> If anyone's answer to either question is "No", I would like to hear an
> explicit statement of such, accompanied by explanations that counter the
> aforementioned ample evidence (which has been, and could easily be,
> provided
> by members of this list) in specific, individual situations.
>
> If we all agree that the answer to both questions is "Yes", then can
> someone
> explain what this seemingly never-ending debate is really about?  Is it
> simply about the relative frequency of each mechanism, across broader
> biodiversity, that has historically resulted in reproductively isolated
> populations and subsequent speciation?  If so, then the next question is:
>
> 3) Are we arguing about 95/5 one way or the other, or more like 60/40 one
> way or the other?
>
> Some additional questions:
>
> 4) Is anyone willing to step up to the plate to discuss cases where
> speciation seems to have occurred in the absence of geographic isolation
> (i.e., no dispersal or vicariant events required)?
>
> 5) Does someone want to take a stab at defining the scope of "broader
> biodiversity" alluded to in the preamble to question #3 above? (There seems
> to be a lot of focus on terrestrial vertebrates and plants -- does the
> debate also include marine organisms?  What about insects?  Microbes? Can
> vicariance or dispersal explain patterns of bacteria diversity?)
>
> 6) Imagine a hypothetical(?) situation where infrequent events like global
> glaciation cycles cause substantial sea-level changes, which have dramatic
> impact to ocean currents and thereby shifts in patterns of dispersal
> potential/frequency among marine organisms with planktonic larvae -- such
> that geographically disjoint populations that have high potential gene flow
> during long periods of time, undergo long periods of time with very low
> potential gene flow.  In such cases, where speciation results during the
> low-gene-flow periods, do we score a point for vicariance, or for
> dispersal?

> Hi All,               I've been reading various research papers on the
> Aplocheiloid killifishes, and in a recently published paper by Costa, 2013
> [Vertebrate Zoology, 63(2): 139?154], I was very surprised to see his
> results showing that genus Kryptolebias was sister group to ALL of the
> other genera of the New World family Rivulidae.
>
>           Is it merely coincidence that the primitive American genus
> Kryptolebias just happens to be a great candidate for a fish that could
> disperse on a raft of vegetation from Africa to South America?  (1) It's
> not only very tolerant of salt water, but more importantly (2) it can
> survive out of water for weeks at a time (66 days in one case).  (3) And
> guess where Kryptolebias marmoratus spends much of its time while out of
> the water----in logs and cavities of trees (which would be the main
> structural components of a large transoceanic raft).
>          Those are the three things I thought of offhand which pointed to
> possible trans-Atlantic dispersal.  But then I began wondering about yet
> another strange thing about Kryptolebias fish.  They often exhibit various
> types of hermaphroditism, and even its most extreme form
> (self-fertilization, which is apparently absent in vertebrates except for
> Kryptolebias).  So just a few of these fish (or even a single individual)
> could have established a population on another continent.  And the
> proximity of northern South America to the end-Cretaceous bolide impact off
> southern Mexico (and the resulting mass extinction) would have meant lots
> of empty niches that needed to be refilled in the early Tertiary.
>          I'm not saying that this is definitely how the first Rivulid got
> to the New World, but it is certainly worth serious consideration.  As for
> possible dispersal among the clades and subclades back in the Old World
> (Families Aplocheilidae and Nothobranchidae) versus the proposed vicariance
> explanations, I am definitely still on the fence and hoping to find more
> evidence either way.   It could be a case where vicariance best explains
> the Old World pattern, but then followed by Cenozoic dispersal to the New
> World.  But if there were no Aplocheiloids in existence when Madagascar
> (and India) split off of Africa, dispersal would trump vicariance there as
> well.
>        Anyway, I can certainly understand why there has been a backlash in
> recent years among so-called "dispersalists" who believe that others have
> sometimes been too quick to assume vicariance where the evidence is frankly
> equivocal and some of them seem too quick to dismiss (or to just ignore)
> possible dispersal just because it appears unlikely.  If a
> proto-Kryptolebias genus in Africa did disperse to South America by rafting
> sometime in the early Tertiary, hopefully a closely related genus might
> still exist in western Africa.  But we won't know if we don't look for
> evidence (molecular or morphological) for that relationship in particular.
>  Sometimes more intensive sampling (especially molecular) in a particular
> area of interest can reveal important information that limited sampling
> have simply missed.  A sister group relationship between Kryptolebias and
> some West African aplocheiloids might be found, even if it is in molecular
> trees which aren't the shortest (and thus overlooked).
