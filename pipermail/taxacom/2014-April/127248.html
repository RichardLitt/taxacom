I also received a copy of the paper. Rather curious in that the paper
asserted  that ?most? of the non volant, non aquatic forms must have
arrived on rafts or floated there and invoked Simpson?s sweekpstakes model
for the Galapagos biota. I have no problem with Ali and Aitchison choosing
the dispersalist model since they have the right to their opinion. What was
curious was to be sent a paper that omitted any reference to an alternative
island arc model published for the Galapagos biota, particularly as I have
been involved as an author in that model. This is not the first time I have
been sent a paper that made this kind of omission (i.e. omitting reference
to my work). Not sure of the psychology involved in doing that. I have
asked Jason for his explanation for the omission.

>
>
> Thanks for the useful paper. The classic group for biogeography on the
> Galapagos are the mockingbirds, Nesomimus (now a clade in Mimus), that
> Darwin (1859) gave as the 'type' example of allopatric species (the more
> famous 'finches' are not allopatric, although many people assume they
> are!):
>
>
>
> 'In the Galapagos Archipelago, many even of the birds, though so well
> adapted for flying from island to island, are distinct on each; thus there
> are three closely-allied species of mocking-thrush, each confined to its
> own island...'. [7 distinct forms are now recognised].
>
>
>
> Arbogast et al. (2006) showed that the sister group of the Galapagos clade
> is M. gundlachii of Jamaica/Bahamas, the standard Galapagos - Caribbean
> connection. Within the Galapagos, the clade on the outer eastern 'arc':
> Espanola/Genovesa/San Cristobal is sister to (and allopatric with) the
> other Galapagos clade.
>
>
>
> Michael

> > Hi Jason,
> >
> > You should be able to find a free version of the O'Malley paper using
> > 'google scholar' ('apps' button on google). A similar paradigm shift to
> > that seen in microbiology has also happened in marine biology - huge
> > amounts of allopatric structure, even down to small scales,  have been
> > discovered in what were thought to be widespread taxa dispersed by
> > currents. I wrote a review in 2005, 'towards a panbiogeography of the
> > seas', which is also freely available at google scholar.
> >
> > Here's another example of what I'm talking about with sympatric
> > speciation. Imagine a species of liverwort found in an area of hill
> forest
> > 50 km across, that diverges by ecological, sympatric speciation into one
> > species found on the tops of leaves, and its sister on the underside.
> This
> > process would give groups with different ecology but very similar
> > geographic ranges.  (Of course, one or both species may subsequently
> expand
> > or contract their geographic range).  Such a pattern would be
> > solid evidence for sympatric speciation, but most studies claiming
> > sympatric speciation don't provide anything like this. If there is the
> > slightest overlap between two sister species, many studies now claim
> > sympatric speciation. The process may occur, but it seems to be very
> rare.
> >
> > Michael

> >> Dear Michael:

> >> "Another colleague sent a good paper on the 'everything is everywhere'
> >> debate - it's:
> >> O'Malley, M. A. (2007). The nineteenth century roots of'everything is
> >> everywhere'. Nature Reviews Microbiology, 5(8), 647-651. 'The
> >> identification of geographical patterns in microbial distributions has
> >> begun to challenge purely ecological explanations of biogeography and
> >> the underlying principle of 'everything is everywhere: but the

> >> I don?t have access to this paper so I cannot add anything to this.
> >> Nevertheless, what are you using to substantiate your next comment?:
> >>  "Of course, no-one is suggesting that dispersal does not occur, but
> >> chance dispersal/ecology does not seem to be the explanation for these
> >> patterns."
> >> What is chance ecology? Which patterns do you refer to? Pantaneto and
> >> colleagues are careful to note that, although certain lineages in
> >> their phyla show the effects of vicariance, a large proportion of
> >> their taxa are indeed widely dispersed (they use the term themselves).
> >> So how does this negate dispersal? Again, nobody denies vicariance, or
> >> that it may be more important than dispersal in many situations or at
> >> particular scales, but the evidence of dispersal is rife so unless
> >> there is a testable hypothesis to distinguish your two dispersals, we
> >> will be arguing forever and not make any progress,
> >>
> >> As for sympatric speciation and range overlap, range overlap of the
> >> hosts/habitats may or may not be fully concordant. So why would your
> >> ridge species simply stay on that ridge? Why would adaptation to a
> >> ridge not preadapt it to living on cliffs? We call it a ridge but for
> >> your species it may be an inaccessible area due to dryness, exposure,
> >> temperature oscillations, etc. Once the problem is solved, there are
> >> other ridges to move into or other "ridge-like" environments that are
> >> suddenly accessible. Once a new niche is broken into the range
> >> limitations of the ancestral population/species will no longer fully
> >> apply. In addition, consider that, it is likely that most of these
> >> jumps will occur at the edges of a range, where a species? survival
> >> chances are at their limit (high selective pressures); where gene-flow
> >> may also be limited (distance to bulk of population gene-pool,
> >> suitable habitat is more fragmented hindering migration); and where
> >> the population may be small (drift more likely) the resulting
> >> ditribution will show little overlap.
