 I would agree with Stephen if this
 were an isolated case. However, bear in mind that in the
 case of this particular author we are dealing with
 approximately 600 new names by now. Of the species-group
 names he has coined, we can rest assured that for the
 majority of type designations, the procedure used was
 exactly as for Macrochelys (some others were based on
 literature descriptions of the types, only a very few of his
 earliest descriptions involved types he had actually seen).
 Under those circumstances, if the herpetological community
 wishes to retain any degree of sanity in herpetological
 taxonomy and nomenclature, it has little option but to
 choose what Frank Krell very aptly called a "reaction of the
 community [that] might not be Code-compliant, but ethically
 and scientifically understandable and preferable". For that
 reason, I would bet good money on the next editions of the
 various US field guides and standard species lists making
 reference to Macrochelys apalachicolae and M. suwanniensis,
 not M. maxhoseri or M. muscati, irrespective of what
 ultimately transpires about the availability of the two
 latter names under the Code.
 
 We need to remember that, to most taxonomists, the Code is a
 tool with the function of? assisting communication and
 information retrieval, not an end in itself.
 
 Best regards,
 
 Wolfgang

 Just to reiterate what I consider to be the most important
 point in all of this mess:
 
 Proper procedure would have been for Thomas et al. to have
 petitioned the ICZN for an official ruling on the
 availability of Hoser's names, and to have maintained
 currect usage (i.e. kept using Hoser's names) until such
 time, if ever, that the ICZN ruled them to be unavailable.
 The ruling has to be in the form of a citable official
 publication in the BZN. It is no good just asking a
 commissioner or two what their personal view(s) is/are (it
 is a commission for a reason).

 I hesitate to wade in, and I am a botanist, so This Code is
 not My Code.? ;-)? But.? It appears to me
 that what Mr. Hoser did was to read the literature,
 determine from it that there were 3 clades that plausibly
 warranted separate taxonomic recognition, that they were
 geographically separate, and then cite as a holotype a sight
 record by someone else based strictly on its geographic
 location.? If it was in the Suwannee River drainage, it
 had to be Clade B.? If it was in the Apalachicola River
 drainage, it had to be Clade C.? Maybe (or maybe not)
 what he did was okay by the current code (I'll not try to
 research that), but it seems that it is a bad idea for a
 holotype to be allowed to be a second-hand sight record, in
 which the naming author is basing it strictly on
 geography.? Isn't the fundamental reason for a holotype
 (and various inferior sorts of types) to FIX (meaning make
 stationary and concrete) the meaning of a name in a way that
 a mere
  description cannot?? [I'll leave aside for the moment
 the other large issue of the circumscription of the taxon
 around the point represented by the holotype].? If a
 holotype has no physical existence, and even no graphical
 existence, but is a second hand sight record, how can that
 be gone back to in the future by our successors and
 consulted and evaluated in a manner which FIXES the meaning
 of the name?? This essentially amounts to the holotype
 being a lat-long?? but distributions change for
 natural and anthropogenic reasons?

 specimen, n.
 An example of an animal, or a fossil or work of an animal,
 or of a part of these. See Article 72.5 for the kinds of
 specimen eligible to be name-bearing types of nominal
 species-group nominal taxa.
 
 72.5. Eligibility as name-bearing types. Only the following
 are eligible to be a name-bearing type, or part of a
 name-bearing type, of a nominal species-group taxon:
 72.5.1. an animal, or any part of an animal, or an example
 of the fossilized work of an animal, or of the work of an
 extant animal if the name based on it was established before
 1931; 72.5.2. a colony of animals that exists in nature as a
 single entity, derived by asexual or vegetative
 multiplication from a single individual (e.g. a colony of
 cnidarians, such as corals), or part of such a colony;
 72.5.3. in the case of fossils, a natural replacement,
 natural impression, natural mould, or natural cast of an
 animal or colony, or part of either; 72.5.4. in extant
 species of protistans, one or more preparations of directly
 related individuals representing differing stages in the
 life cycle (a hapantotype) [Art. 73.3]; 72.5.5. a
 preparation for microscope examination (e.g. a "type slide")
 containing one or more individual organisms, in which the
 name-bearing types are clearly indicated and identifiable.
 Recommendation 72C. Marking of important individuals.
 Whenever possible, authors establishing new nominal
 species-group taxa based upon microscope preparations
 containing more than one specimen (a "type slide") should
 mark distinctly the locations of specimens which are
 considered to be of crucial importance in demonstrating the
 taxonomic characters.
 72.5.6. In the case of a nominal species-group taxon based
 on an illustration or description, or on a bibliographic
 reference to an illustration or description, the
 name-bearing type is the specimen or specimens illustrated
 or described (and not the illustration or description
 itself).
 
 Note that I am not saying that the observational record is
 the holotype. I am saying that the individual turtle that
 was observed is the holotype. It is analogous to 72.5.6.,
 though the Code does not explicitly mention a case of a
 nominal species-group taxon based on an observational
 record. The Code doesn't explicitly rule this out. The
 individual turtle has simply been designated (specified) by
 way of the observational record. The Code is silent on what
 modes of specification are allowable, so all are ..
