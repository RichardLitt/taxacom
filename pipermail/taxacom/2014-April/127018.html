Linz International Zoological Nomenclature Workshop

Dear Doug,

Yes, we did invite personally several members of the Commission to the
meeting, and anyway all its members are welcome to attend it if they wish
to contribute to the special volume of Bionomina that will result from this
workshop. Two days is a short period and we will not be able to discuss all
questions in detail, let alone use Skype for discussions. The meeting will
fix a roadmap for the preparation of this volume. People not present at the
meeting who will express interest to contribute to this volume will be
invited to submit manuscripts once this roadmap is fixed.

Yes, we (not me alone) read the "rebuttal" of the Commission to our work,
which leaves many questions unanswered and shows that our work was indeed
useful. This also will be discussed in Linz and in the special volume.

Sincerely,

Alain Dubois

> > > Hi Ken,
> > >
> > > The aplocheiloid killifish illustrate a standard pattern, with two main
> > > clades (Fig. 2-3 in my 2012 book). One is in America, and (another
> >
> > > subclade) mainland Africa. It's not in Madagascar, and you might
> > > assume that this was the result of extinction there. But the other main
> > > clade is in Madagascar and the Seychelles, and also (another
> >
> > > subclade) from India to Java.
> > >
> > > You could explain the pattern by chance dispersal from anywhere within
> > the
> > > whole range, e.g. Guatemala or Gabon or Goa. Chance dispersal is not
> > > related to any other factor, and so this wouldn't make any particular
> >
> > > predictions.
> > >
> > > As an alternative, both groups could have been everywhere to begin
> with,
> > > and you could explain the pattern by the chance extinction of the first
> > > group from Madagascar - Java, and the chance extinction of the second
> > group
> >
> > > from America and Africa. This would predict fossils in the area of
> > absence,
> > > but none have ever been found. It's also difficult to imagine why one
> > group
> > > would go extinct in an area that is exactly allopatric with the area
> > where
> >
> > > the other group went extinct. This explanation also leaves the original
> > > differentiation of the groups unaccounted for.
> > >
> > > Or you could explain the pattern by simple vicariance, with the first
> > break
> >
> > > located between Africa and Madagascar, at the Mozambique Channel. This
> > > would make the general predictions that other groups would show similar
> > > patterns, and that there would be a simple correlation between the
> > pattern
> >
> > > and tectonics, climate or some other environmental factor. Note that
> the
> > > first break, between Africa and Madagascar, comes before the secondary
> > > breaks (between America and Africa, and between Madagascar-Seychelles,
> > and
> >
> > > India - Java). This is congruent with the continental breakup sequence.
> > >

>
>
>

> nomenclature, questioning the pertinence of some recent evolutions of the
> *Code*, and worrying about other possible evolutions;
>
>

> of zoological nomenclature;
>
>
>
> (3) to prepare a special issue of the journal *Bionomina* (Magnolia Press)

> problems identified.

> mysterious etc. But back to the observed events. The indeed occur. The
> question that they raise is to what extent they are responsible for the
> observed vicariant patterns of distribution? They are often invoked as
> evidence that overwater dispersal was responsible for these distributions
> in relation to current ocean geography, but are they?  Panbiogeography (as
> I understand it) in some ways reverses the argument to look at patterns and
> from analysis of those patterns make inferences about the role of dispersal
> capability and observed dispersal events in the present.
>
>
>
> There is nothing wrong with dispersal hypotheses being generated, but it is
> how they are generated that is often problematic. In most cases where
> molecular similarity is used to construct phylogeny the authors also
> misrepresent minimal divergence estimates as actual or maximal and from
> that misrepresentation (which is sometimes deliberate as the authors begin
> by acknowledging that the estimates are minimal but 'forget' about that
> when constructing the historical events) they generate fictional evidence
> of dispersal. I understand that Ken and perhaps others consider the gap
> between the oldest fossil and the age of a vicariance divergence event is
> too great to be acceptable, but such judgment seems to rest largely on a
> personal belief rather than any empirical requirement. Some, for example,
> may consider it too incredible for Old and New World monkeys to have
> already diverged in the Cretaceous or earlier in the absence of fossils.
> And maybe it is too incredible, but whether the fossil record is accurate
> about these groups or not is another matter - given that so many taxa lack
> fossils at all, or purportedly old taxa have a relatively recent fossil
> record (I think tuatara only go back a few million years for example).
>
>
>
> When it comes to considering dispersal or vicariance, it is not whether one
> stands for either or both in general, but how one reaches those conclusions
> for particular cases. In the killfish example one has a geographic pattern
> that is consistent with a vicariant history (and is congruent with other
> groups of different dispersal capabilities). On the other hand, Briggs just
> says that the fish are too young and take his word for it. Other approaches
> might use divergence estimates to predict overwater dispersal based on
> misrepresenting minimal divergences as maximal. In some cases of freshwater
> patterns it has been argued that some members or some related group shows
> saltwater tolerance and so marine migration is possible. In theory yes, but
> again what might constitute evidence as to whether or not that was
> significant in the origin of particular patterns?
>
>
>
> When a taxon located either side of an ocean basin comprises one sub clade
> that is also on both sides, but vicariates with another subclade on the
> other side (this is a very common biogeographic pattern as I presume Jason,
> Ken and most others on this list interested in biogeography are aware so
> apologies in advance for stating the obvious), one has a primary divergence
> that is not between the ocean 'barrier'. A vicariance explanation would be
> that the divergence predated the 'barrier' and so the 'barrier' was not a
> 'barrier' as such in the context of the origin of the taxon. I suppose a
> dispersal hypothesis could be generated for this pattern in some way
> allowed the taxon to be so highly dispersible as to cross an ocean, but
> also not sufficiently dispersible to prevent vicariant divergence in the
> first place, or maintain the subsequent vicariism.
>
>
>
> Overall, for modern life, spatial analysis of differentiated taxa indicates
> that dispersal is critical for the origin of ancestral ranges within a
> former geography predating the origin of modern ocean basins and many other
> modern geomorphological features both on land and in the sea.
>
>
>
> I look forward to reading a specific analysis of Heads' book by Jason and
> Ken.
>
>
