Thanks, Rod -- good answers.

It matters because GUIDs of all sorts (including DOIs) are completely
useless (and, in fact, exacerbate the problems more than solve problems) if
people are not clear on what is actually being identified by the identifier.
If I mint an identifier for a taxon name, then someone misinterprets it as
being for a taxon concept, that's bad.  If I mint an identifier for a
location, and someone else misinterprets it as an event (yes, this does
happen), that's bad.  If I mint an identifier for a dynamic data source, and
someone misinterprets it as representing a static data source, that's bad.
I know you like to think the world would be a better place if we just stuck
DOIs on everything; but sloppy application of identifiers is like sloppy
taxonomy -- it makes things harder for everyone else.

Aloha,
Rich

> I have two standard responses to this question:
> 
> 1. Who cares about versions? No, really - we keep worrying about this, but
> does anybody actually take versions into account? For example, GenBank has
> versioned DNA sequences and I have never seen anyone cite a sequence
> accession number AND version. So, to a first approximation, I don't think
this
> is the issue people make it out to be. Anyone every seen a versioned link
to a
> Wikipedia page?
> 
> 2.OK, so you really, really, care about versions. Then I think one answer
is to
> provide a DOI for the data e.g., 10.1234/data (which you can think of as
either
> all the versions, or the latest version), and a DOI for each version
(e.g.,
> 10.1234/data.v1, 10.1234/data.v2). If, like most folks, you couldn't care
less
> ("just give me the latest version please") then you'd use the DOI for the
data
> (10.1234/data) that resolves to the latest version. If you care about the
> version (e.g., reproducibility is your thing) then use the DOI for the
particular
> version (e.g., 10.1234/data.v1).
> 
> There are examples of versioned DOIs in the wild. For example,
> http://dx.doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.2-92.v1 is a versioned DOI.
> CrossRef supports versions through Crossmark
> http://www.crossref.org/crossmark/i to handle things like errata,
> retractions, etc.
>

> > However, this raises another issue that I have not seen adequately
> > addressed in any of these discussions about identifiers for datasets
> (including DOIs):
> >
> > Is there an expectation that the object represented by an identifier
> > remains static?  In the case of traditional publications, this seems to
be the
> case.
> > When I dereference a DOI, I get a digital document (or a landing page
> > for a digital document).  In my mind, that DOI identifies the
> > conceptual object (i.e., the published work as an abstract entity, not
> > any particular digital manifestation/instantiation of the published
> > work, such as a particular PDF file on a particular hard drive
> > somewhere).  But in the case of "traditional" publications (including
> > electronic publications, and as opposed to datasets), the content of
> > the object is expected to remain static over time.  A hundred years
> > from now, when I (more likely my grandchildren) dereference a DOI,
> > they will get the same information content (at least at a
human-perception
> level, if not a bit-identical data file) that I get today.
> >
> > Now, I know lots of people (particularly Rod) like to say we should
> > assign DOIs to "datasets".  That's fine, and totally within scope of
> > what DOIs were intended for.  But is it implicit that a DOI assigned
> > to a dataset is actually assigned to a *snapshot* of that dataset at a
> > particular moment in time?  Or is the DOI representing the conceptual
> > notion of the dataset, which changes in content over time?
> >
> > So, I could assign a single DOI to the Bishop Museum fish collection
> > dataset, representing the "conceptual" notion of the Bishop Museum
> > fish collection dataset.  Tomorrow the content is likely to be
> > different from what it is now. To me, this seems to border on useless.
> > One of the critical issues of citing databases that comes up again and
> > again is to include the date on which the dataset was accessed (see my
> > previous post).  Is that date part of the permanent DOI metadata?  If
> > so, there may be hundreds of DOIs issued for the Bishop Museum fish
> > collection dataset, each one representing a particular moment in time
> > for which someone wishes to be able to cite the dataset content.
> >
> > Glancing over through the DOI specification, it seems to me that there
> > is an implicit assumption that the document identified by a DOI is
> > static in content.  But I haven't read it thoroughly, and am by no means
an
> expert.
> >
> > So, my question to Rod (and anyone else) is:  if we assign an
> > identifier such as a DOI to a dataset, is there an expectation that
> > the dataset indicated by the identifier is static?  Or, might it be
> > dynamic?  In the parlance of LSID-speak, what is implicitly the "data",
vs.
> the "metadata"?

> >>> I have LONG supported this notion.  One of the problems, though, is
> >>> that very few databases allow future users to see what the database
> >>> contained on the date on which it was accessed.  In an ideal world,
> >>> all online databases would include a parameter "asOfDate" or
> >>> something similar, where users can pass in a particular date and see
> >>> what the database consisted of as of that date.  For some systems
> >>> (which maintain robust edit logs or "audit trails", or maintain
> >>> explicit versioning), it would be possible to do this; but for many
> >>> systems it
> > would
> >> not.
> >>>
> >>> Recognizing this issue, about ten years ago we conceived of a system
> >>> where users of our online databases could select an option to
> >>> "publish" a full snapshot of the query results they examined.  The
> >>> idea would be for a user to construct a query of the database and
> >>> get a result-set back, then upon deciding that this result-set is
> >>> the one to be cited, there would be a button that "published" the
> >>> result set as an XML file (which could be represented as a styled
> >>> PDF or converted to Excel, complete with its own title, authorship
> >>> [jointly the person constructing the query, and the database owner],
> >>> full data set, and its own "references" section that cited the
> >>> original sources of the data).  This XML file would then be
> >>> permanently archived for future access, and would be the digital
> >>> object cited in the work for
> > which it
> >> was generated.
> >>>
> >>> We never followed through with the development of that concept
> >>> (budget issues, different priorities, etc.); but there may yet be
> >>> some value in doing it.  For example, perhaps there could be an
> >>> automated system that allowed users of online databases to snap-shot
> >>> result-sets directly into a publication venue such as the Biodiversity
Data
> Journal.
> >>>
> >>> Speaking of that journal, we are planning to publish a regional
> >>> checklist (fishes of the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands) in BDJ that
> >>> changes the way such regional checklists are created.  Basically, we
> >>> build an evidence-based checklist of occurrence records for
> >>> within-scope taxa at within-scope region, and store this in a
> >>> database.  The checklist is annotated and curated by the authors,
> >>> who vet all the data, flag dubious records, confirm taxonomic
> >>> identifications, etc.  The checklist is then published in a highly
> >>> structured form (ala BDJ), with a link back to the database.  The
> >>> link would then go to a page that displays everything as it appeared
> >>> in the published version, as well as all data that were altered or
> >>> added since the moment of publication.  The "vetted" (published)
> >>> records would be formatted clearly to distinguish from all the
> >>> un-vetted, "raw" data that became available following the
> >>> publication.  That way people who come to that website could see the
> >>> full content of what was published, *AND* all of the new information
> >>> that has become available since the time it was published.
> >>> Eventually, when there is enough new information, it would be vetted
> >>> again and a new-snapshot generated and published (in the form of a
> >>> "revised" checklist, or new-records paper, or whatever).  The idea
> >>> is to be able to clearly show the full dataset as it was
> >>> snapshot-published, and also give acces to up-to-the-moment
> >>> additional data; thus avoiding the current problem with regional
> > checklists,
> >> which is that they become obsolete the moment they are published.
> >>>
> >>> In any case, I fully support the idea of more consistent and
> >>> deliberate citation of online data systems in the "references"
> >>> section of publications, and I'd like to see us move even beyond
> >>> that to a standard system that allows people to immediately compare
> >>> what was available at the time the dataset was cited to what is
> >>> currently in the
> >> dataset now.
