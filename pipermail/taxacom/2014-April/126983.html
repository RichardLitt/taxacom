Ken,



Your original assertion was that the frog relationship was out of date and
I asked for supporting citation. I then gave citations showing that there
was recent support for the clade from molecular data showing that your
assertion appeared to be incorrect and you have not introduced anything to
refute that conclusion.



Your subsequent introduction of another issue regarding molecules and
morphology is another matter. I did not assert that the leiopelmatid
relationship was necessarily correct because it was molecular. The
molecular clade did show a Pacific pattern that contrasted with other frog
taxa - which is interesting in its own right. So, am I 'convinced' that the
clade is correct? No, of course not.



Ironically, your argument for the molecular similarities being questioned
in frogs is just as applicable to humans and chimps even though you somehow
do not see the latter in this light despite the extensive documentation of
morphology from many authors). In the case of frogs I have not studied the
morphology of this group so I am not in a position to judge the veracity of
morphology vs molecules for frog phylogeny. It took me 10 years just to
work over the 7 or so species of large bodied hominoids.



"If Ascaphidae split off first, and then Leiopelmatidae split off next" one
would still have a basal split in the Pacific for the evolution of frogs.



"If you don't pick just the right molecules to sequence, you are likely to
end up confusing molecular symplesiomorphies as molecular synapomorphies
(and this has no doubt happened often)". Trouble is, how does one know one
has the "right" molecular sequences?



John Grehan

> Hi John,
>
>       Sorry I couldn't get back to this earlier.  Reading your post on
> frog phylogeny absolutely baffles (even shocks) me, coming from someone who
> lectures primatologists for putting too much emphasis on molecular (vs.
> morphological) data regarding humans and great apes (chimpanzees in
> particular).
>
>        Now we have a case where two frog families apparently diverged a
> hell of a lot longer ago, and you pick this as a case where molecular data
> convinces you, even though there are NO morphological synapomorphies AT ALL
> shared by these frogs.  The consensus actually seems to be that their
> shared morphological characters are primitive (symplesiomorphies), so these
> "DEEP" shared molecular characters are very likely to be symplesiomorphies
> as well.   If there is a case where the molecular data should be
> questioned, it is these frog families (not chimps and humans).
>
>         Therefore, I would side with Green and Cannatella (1993) and
> Cannatella and Ford (1993) who actually found several morphological
> synapomorphies uniting Leiopelmatidae with more recent frogs (to the
> EXCLUSION of Ascaphidae, which lacks those morphological characters).  Thus
> they named a new clade Leiopelmatanura as sister group to the most
> primitive living family of frogs (Ascaphidae).
>
>        Not surprisingly, it is noted that one of the co-authors in Ford et
> al., 2006 (namely Green), disagreed with their decision to lump Ascaphidae
> and Leiopelmatidae together.  Keeping them as separate families would have
> made perfect sense, given the conflict between morphological and molecular
> data.  However, Green was overruled by the consensus among the authors of
> that particular paper.
>
>        So John, I would suggest that if you are so concerned about
> molecular data being questionable, this is clearly a case where you should
> be focusing your attention.  If Ascaphidae split off first, and then
> Leiopelmatidae split off next (before the rest of the extant frogs
> families), one would expect those two primitive families to not only share
> morphological  (primitive) symplesiomorphies, but that their shared
> molecular sequences would be symplesiomorphies as well.  Especially as
> these cladistic splits happened so long ago.
>
>        Frankly, it is this type of ancient divergence which has made it so
> difficult to use molecular data to establish the relationships of
> invertebrate phyla, and the relationships within some those phyla (such as
> Arthropoda).  If you don't pick just the right molecules to sequence, you
> are likely to end up confusing molecular symplesiomorphies as molecular
> synapomorphies (and this has no doubt happened often).
>
>         Looks to me like the cladistic relationships between the earliest
> frogs is just another case where we haven't found the right molecular
> sequences to discriminate between symplesiomorphy and synapomorphy to
> explain the similarities.  CONCLUSION: Although morphology can obviously be
> confusing when it comes to convergence and parallelism,  the further back
> in time one goes, we are just as likely (sometimes even more likely) to be
> confused by convergence and parallelism in molecular sequences if we don't
> pick the right molecules.
