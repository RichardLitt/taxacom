Briggs asserts that the Cyprindondiformes are part of the Antherinomorpha
for which the oldest fossil dates are from the early to mid-Tertiary and
alognth older fossils are not precluded, their appearance becomes "less
probable" as time goes by. No explanation of what this means scientifically.



Then Briggs makes a huge leap in logic by saying "If, as the fossil
record *seems
to indicate*, the aplocheiloid fishes are too young to have been carried
about on tectonic plates, how did they achieve their cir-cumglobal range?"



This reads as a slight of hand, by claiming that the fossil records "seems
to indicate" that the fishes are too young and therefore they are to young,
and having established that claim (not evidence) they must have achieved
their range without being carried on tectonic plates. The rest of his
argument is the invention of centers of origin and dispersal routes. Pure
fiction.

> > The aplocheiloid killifish illustrate a standard pattern, with two main
> > clades (Fig. 2-3 in my 2012 book). One is in America, and (another
>
> > subclade) mainland Africa. It's not in Madagascar, and you might
> > assume that this was the result of extinction there. But the other main
> > clade is in Madagascar and the Seychelles, and also (another
>
> > subclade) from India to Java.
> >
> > You could explain the pattern by chance dispersal from anywhere within
> the
> > whole range, e.g. Guatemala or Gabon or Goa. Chance dispersal is not
> > related to any other factor, and so this wouldn't make any particular
>
> > predictions.
> >
> > As an alternative, both groups could have been everywhere to begin with,
> > and you could explain the pattern by the chance extinction of the first
> > group from Madagascar - Java, and the chance extinction of the second
> group
>
> > from America and Africa. This would predict fossils in the area of
> absence,
> > but none have ever been found. It's also difficult to imagine why one
> group
> > would go extinct in an area that is exactly allopatric with the area
> where
>
> > the other group went extinct. This explanation also leaves the original
> > differentiation of the groups unaccounted for.
> >
> > Or you could explain the pattern by simple vicariance, with the first
> break
>
> > located between Africa and Madagascar, at the Mozambique Channel. This
> > would make the general predictions that other groups would show similar
> > patterns, and that there would be a simple correlation between the
> pattern
>
> > and tectonics, climate or some other environmental factor. Note that the
> > first break, between Africa and Madagascar, comes before the secondary
> > breaks (between America and Africa, and between Madagascar-Seychelles,
> and
>
> > India - Java). This is congruent with the continental breakup sequence.
