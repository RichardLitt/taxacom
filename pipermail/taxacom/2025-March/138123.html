  They just say: 'Pleurodonta = Iguanidae sensu ( 16 ) (e.g., anoles,
horned lizards, and relatives), are also largely restricted to the western
hemisphere'.  They don't mention the sister group, let alone vicariance
with it, although it's a classic case of vicariance that's been cited many
times. 
      Pleurodonta: Madagascar + Americas + Fiji/Tonga. (i..e trans-Pacific
+ Atlantic).
      Acrodonta (sis group of Pleurodonta): Madagascar + Africa + Europe +
Asia to Australia and Solomons. (Trans-Indian).
The groups are allopatric except in Madagascar. The distributions are
determined by breaks (of a global ancestor) in the Atlantic, around
Madagascar, and between the Solomons and Fiji, all areas of major rifting.
There is no dispersal indicated except (perhaps) in Madagascar.

 I've read the PNAS article and it does seem to be a very thorough
even-handed assessment of all the possibilities in their discussion. 
 They say humans have observed iguanas making landfall on islands and quote
this presumably famous instance:
 "In 1995, a vegetation mat
 with at least 15 individual green iguanas (Iguana iguana) that likely
 originated on the islands of Guadeloupe was observed making landfall
 on Anguilla, an island 300 km away that was already inhabited by
 a different species of iguana, I. delicatissima"
 One quibble is that they work at a genus-level with little mention of
species.  The genus is an 'it' as though it is a physical entity. A
practice more familiar of palaeontologists rather than molecular
biologists.
 This quote illustrates the style:  "Brachylophus , which contains 4 extant
and 1 extinct species, is morphologically divergent from other iguanas and
occurs extremely far from all of its extant relatives"
 Substitute 'are' for 'is' and 'their' for 'its' and see how it reads. The
actual species is Brachylophus bulabula.  It gets just one mention (in a
figure), and there are relations on different Fijian islands, plus
apparently also one iguana human-transported.
