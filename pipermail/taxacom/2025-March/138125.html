        I agree.  This is a fine research paper.  And if iguanas can travel 300 km. overseas in recent times, much further voyages over millions of years are very probable.

I've read the PNAS article and it does seem to be a very thorough even-handed assessment of all the possibilities in their discussion.

They say humans have observed iguanas making landfall on islands and quote this presumably famous instance:

"In 1995, a vegetation mat
with at least 15 individual green iguanas (Iguana iguana) that likely
originated on the islands of Guadeloupe was observed making landfall
on Anguilla, an island 300 km away that was already inhabited by
a different species of iguana, I. delicatissima"

One quibble is that they work at a genus-level with little mention of species.  The genus is an 'it' as though it is a physical entity. A practice more familiar of palaeontologists rather than molecular biologists.

This quote illustrates the style:  "Brachylophus , which contains 4 extant and 1 extinct species, is morphologically divergent from other iguanas and occurs extremely far from all of its extant relatives"

Substitute 'are' for 'is' and 'their' for 'its' and see how it reads. The actual species is Brachylophus bulabula.  It gets just one mention (in a figure), and there are relations on different Fijian islands, plus apparently also one iguana human-transported.
