Nationality is nor really decisive. Article 32.5.2 says "based upon a German word".  Although I have to leave the etymology in this case to someone else, it may be relevant to note that Danish and Swedish are largely founded on Low German and the umlauts (mostly ö and ä in Swedish) mayindicate a Low German word..  Brünnich may be from the word Brunn (Swedish) Brunnen,  (German  and  brønd (Danish), or Brünne (German), brynja (Swedish), brynje (Danish)

According to Geni, there was a16th Century ancestor Brünnich in Rostock, Germany. Although nationality is not decisive, family origins may indicate the proper etymology.

So, I guess it is ue

BTW, The trema is used in several languages, not only In German umlauts

>> There is a Code Article for this specific type of change (below), and 
>> the same rule (33.3.1) applies; if the incorrect spelling is in 
>> prevailing usage, then it *becomes* correct, accordingly. If the only 
>> place these spider names are spelled "-i" is in the catalogs, then 
>> they might not qualify as being in prevailing usage (also below).
>>
>> "33.4.Use of -/i/for -/ii/and vice versa, and other alternative 
>> spellings, in subsequent spellings of species-group names.The use of 
>> the genitive ending -/i/in a subsequent spelling of a species-group 
>> name that is a genitive based upon a personal name in which the 
>> correct original spelling ends with -/ii/, or vice versa, is deemed 
>> to be an incorrect subsequent spelling, even if the change in 
>> spelling is deliberate; the same rule applies to the endings -/ae/and 
>> -/iae/, -/orum/and -/iorum/, and -/arum/and -/iarum/."
>>
>> "*usage, prevailing*,/n/.
>>
>> Of a name: that usage of the name which is adopted by *at least a 
>> substantial majority of the most recent authors* concerned with the 
>> relevant taxon, *irrespective of how long ago* their work was published."
>> (emphasis mine, to highlight that one calculates usage by counting 
>> AUTHORS, not works, and that one can go back as far as one deems 
>> necessary to assess the pattern).
>>
>> I'm not pointing this out to start a debate over the pros and cons of 
>> "prevailing usage", only to point out that these provisions do exist, 
>> and that the reason these provisions exist is so people won't keep 
>> changing spelling back and forth based on finding old forgotten 
>> references, and arguments over grammar. The "novel" principle that 
>> the 2000 Code edition introduced is that stability is best achieved 
>> through consensus (rather than incessant one-upmanship as to who can 
>> dredge up the oldest and most obscure references, or make the most 
>> scholarly foray into the nuances of Latin grammatical construction). 
>> It is unfortunately hard to come up with a purely objective criterion 
>> as to what, exactly, constitutes "consensus", so the definition of 
>> prevailing usage adopted is not perfect, but it's an approximation. 
>> Names can be rendered *permanently* unalterable via the List of 
>> Available Names (LAN: Article 79), but that's a special and difficult 
>> process.
