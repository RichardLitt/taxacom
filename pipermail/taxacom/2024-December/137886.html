My appreciation to all the responses, including a couple off list. My
understanding, if correct, is that the abstract was not in a 'published'
venue in the sense of Article 8 because that venue was an electronic
publication without ZooBank
registration number (if it were in hardcopy, does it still have to require
ZooBank number to be valid as an accepted form of publication?). In this
context the names are all unavailable. If it was decided for one reason or
another that the venue conformed to that of 'publication', the names would
appear to be nomen nude for various reasons as stated in responses. One
person looked up Magnijuxta purpuravena in Zoobank and found no entry. The
only puzzle here is how two seasoned taxonomists appear to have overlooked
this situation. Obviously a full paper was anticipated, but did not yet
eventuate. Anyway, grateful for the very helpful clarifications of the
context.

It's not immediately critical for me, although if I were to refer in the
future to these taxa, does that presumably mean that I could not refer to
these names, at least in a journal following the Code? Otherwise presumably
I could refer to the names even if unavailable in terms of the code? The
record is very significant biogeographically, being part of a trans-Pacific
pattern, and significant in terms of location being on a former island arc
remnant. These are very tiny moths and would not be surprising that they
are easily overlooked yet more widespread in Central and South America.

Cheers, John

>
> > As of the 2000 Edition of the Code (Code 4) there are more criteria
> > that need to be met for a new name to be available than was true in
> > earlier Code versions.
> >
> > (1) It must be *published* in the sense of the Code. With print pubs
> > this is generally easily met, for e-only publications the requirements
> > are very stringent and complicated.
> >
> > (2) Names for new families *must* designate a type genus, new genera
> > must designate a type species, new species must designate a type
> specimen.
> >
> > (3) There must be an *explicit statement* that the names are new, and
> > they must be proposed as available names of subspecies or higher rank,
> > not synonyms, morphs, or varieties, or as "conditional" names.
> >
> > (4) proposals of new taxa must include a *verbal description* of their
> > diagnostic feature(s).
> >
> > (5) for new species, the *name and location* of the depository of the
> > type specimen must be given.
> >
> > While these provisions are excellent at preventing "accidental"
> > introduction of new names (such as in a meeting abstract) it's pretty
> > remarkable how often authors trying to publish new taxa fail to comply
> > with all the requirements. In just the past year, I've seen examples
> > of works failing each one of the criteria above, sometimes more than one.
