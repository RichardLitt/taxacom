I'll echo the sentiments of both Thomas Pape and David Ride and suggest that our long tradition of standardized (codified) scientific nomenclature -- one of the longest-standing and most universally adopted standards in ALL OF SCIENCE -- may not survive much longer if it cannot accommodate modern mechanisms of information exchange more elegantly.This sentiment is NOT limited to Zoological nomenclature (ahem), but applies generally to all scientific nomenclature for all domains of life.  Botanists, Mycologists and Phycologists have an opportunity to amend their rules every 6 years.  The interval in zoology is much longer, but as luck would have it, the next (fifth) Edition of the ICZN Code is being drafted right now.  Sometime in the next few years, we'll have a once-in-a-quarter-century opportunity to simultaneously modernize the way scientific names of organisms are established (and maintained), and harmonize the rules across the different domains/codes.  If the critter/weed/shroom/limu gang can follow the lead of the prokaryotic folks in embracing a system of registration, and if all of them (prokaryote folk included) can anchor that registration system within a common (or, at least, very similar) electronic, online, and public-domain data resource, the likelihood that standardized scientific nomenclature (and, by extension, the field of biological taxonomy as we know it) will persist for decades (or centuries) to come, will increase dramatically.I've held out hope for this across several decades, and have crusaded for it during my 18-year tenure on the ICZN Commission (as well as a decade or so prior to that), but sadly it still feels (just barely) out of reach.The opportunity to move in this direction is right in front of us, and we still have a couple of years to make it happen.  Are we (taxonomists) up to the challenge?Aloha,RichRichard L.
