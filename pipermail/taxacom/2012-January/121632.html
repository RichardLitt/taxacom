Hi Stuart,
       I agree with most of what you said.  I avoided even discussing
the purported Euagrus species from Taiwan for the reasons you gave.  I
was less bothered by the South African species, since it would have been
adjacent to South America during the mid-Mesozoic.  However, if it is
indeed not Euagrus, then that makes it even more likely that Subfamilies
Euagrinae and Masteriinae BOTH arose within South America.  The question
which has arisen in my mind is whether they are sister taxa, or whether
Euagrinae might be paraphyletic with respect to Masteriinae.  But as you
suggested, a lot of revisionary work is needed in all such subfamilies
of Dipluridae.  

       Whether or not genus Masteria is holophyletic (strictly
monophyletic), I have no definite opinion.  But in any case, I have
little doubt that Masteriinae (assuming that its three genera form a
holophyletic taxon) arose in South America (although Michael Heads might
disagree).  In any case, I am increasingly doubtful about
panbiogeographic suggestions that any of these spider taxa spread from
the mid-Pacific to South America via floating terranes.     

       However, there is one point on which you and I might disagree.  I
increasingly suspect that Masteria probably does exist on Borneo and
perhaps other islands between it and New Guinea.  Granted, it could have
dispersed directly from New Guinea to the Philippines, but island
hopping in between would not surprise me in the least.  Therefore, my
question about whether anyone has done a serious search for Masteria on
Borneo (especially in caves or near caves).

Stuart longhorn wrote:       
Hi all., Re: Masteria mystery 
Having actually stared down a dissecting microscope at diplurid spiders
this week, i feel that rather than needing complex scenarios to explain
unusual biogeography, perhaps instead the combination of few
poorly-defined putative synapomorphies and series of poor taxonomic
judgements have simply converged to make yet another genus urgently in
need of serious revision with modern tools and techniques. As with any
taxonomic group, it's probably very valuable to first consider if the
genus is actually monophyletic, and whether the few characters alleged
to support the group (such as reduction from 8 to 6 eyes) are actually
robust synapomorphies, for example considering whether eye reduction
actually commonly appears convergently... as i think most of us know for
cave adapted invertebrates. As for Euagrus in Taiwan, it appears nobody
has looked much at E. formosanus Saito 1933 nor E. atropurpureus Purcell
1903 since their initial descriptions (see below* which probably
explains the ? in Platnick's catalog), nor at I.indicola Tikader 1969,
and as none of these original authors were exactly luminaries in the
world of mygalomorphs, nor flawless in other generic placements, i'm
just suggesting some revision of type specimens (if they even exist or
can be found - see *) and consideration of generic definitions might be
needed before anyone rushes off to the caves of Borneo in a desperate
bid to explain some alleged unusual biogeography. I think careful
reconsideration of old rogue species with exceptional localities might
actually solve such mysteries ** - I just can't believe this needs
saying to some on a mailing list full of taxonomists! Regardsstuart
longhorn 
*See Coyle (1988), on p223 he says: "MISPLACED SPECIES: As I have
previously demonstrated (Coyle, 1984), Evagrus atropurpureus Purcell
(1903) is neither a species of Euagrus nor of Allothele. Both Evagrus
caffer Pocock (1902) and Evagrus regnardi Benoit (1964) are species of
Allothele (Coyle, 1984). The holotype of Euagrus formosanus Saito
(1933), a female, was apparently "lost in Hokkaido during world War II"
(T.Yaginuma, personal commun.). The published description and
illustrations are not very informative (there is no information on the
spermathecae), but are sufficient to show that this spider is not a
species of Euagrus." In Coyle (1988). A Revision of the American
funnel-web mygalomorph spider genus Euagrus (Araneae, dipluridae). Bull.
Amer. Mus. Nat. Hist. 187(3):203-292** Why Coyle just didn't say nomen
dubium or at least species inquirenda at some point is beyond me...
