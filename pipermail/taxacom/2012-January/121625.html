Hi all., Re: Masteria mystery
Having actually stared down a dissecting microscope at diplurid spiders this week, i feel that rather than needing complex scenarios to explain unusual biogeography, perhaps instead the combination of few poorly-defined putative synapomorphies and series of poor taxonomic judgements have simply converged to make yet another genus urgently in need of serious revision with modern tools and techniques.
As with any taxonomic group, it's probably very valuable to first consider if the genus is actually monophyletic, and whether the few characters alleged to support the group (such as reduction from 8 to 6 eyes) are actually robust synapomorphies, for example considering whether eye reduction actually commonly appears convergently... as i think most of us know for cave adapted invertebrates. 
As for Euagrus in Taiwan, it appears nobody has looked much at E. formosanus Saito 1933 nor E. atropurpureus Purcell 1903 since their initial descriptions (see below* which probably explains the ? in Platnick's catalog), nor at I.indicola Tikader 1969, and as none of these original authors were exactly luminaries in the world of mygalomorphs, nor flawless in other generic placements, i'm just suggesting some revision of type specimens (if they even exist or can be found - see *) and consideration of generic definitions might be needed before anyone rushes off to the caves of Borneo in a desperate bid to explain some alleged unusual biogeography.
I think careful reconsideration of old rogue species with exceptional localities might actually solve such mysteries ** - I just can't believe this needs saying to some on a mailing list full of taxonomists!
Regardsstuart longhorn
*See Coyle (1988), on p223 he says: "MISPLACED SPECIES: As I have previously demonstrated (Coyle, 1984), Evagrus atropurpureus Purcell (1903) is neither a species of Euagrus nor of Allothele. Both Evagrus caffer Pocock (1902) and Evagrus regnardi Benoit (1964) are species of Allothele (Coyle, 1984). The holotype of Euagrus formosanus Saito (1933), a female, was apparently "lost in Hokkaido during world War II" (T.Yaginuma, personal commun.). The published description and illustrations are not very informative (there is no information on the spermathecae), but are sufficient to show that this spider is not a species of Euagrus." In Coyle (1988). A Revision of the American funnel-web mygalomorph spider genus Euagrus (Araneae, dipluridae). Bull. Amer. Mus. Nat. Hist. 187(3):203-292** Why Coyle just didn't say nomen dubium or at least species inquirenda at some point is beyond me...
