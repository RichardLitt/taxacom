Dear All,
      I guess this thread is getting nowhere.  Be careful what you ask
for.  I asked for a shred of evidence, and I am referred to one small
shred in an older paper (Macey et al., 2004) based on a very limited
sample of species and only on mitochondrial sequences.  Doesn't seem to
matter that Vidal et al (2007) and Muller et al. (2011) considerably
expanded the number of species sampled and more gene sequences----they
dared to challenge the primacy of vicariance, and that is apparently a
no-no in panbiogeography circles.  
       So I guess panbiogeographers (at least the ones that I am familar

with) prefer old data in older papers, as long as they have "nice maps"
and don't challenge some of their favored vicariance conclusions.
Anyway, I certainly don't think that "repeated patterns are
meaningless".  However, I don't discriminate against dispersal patterns
in favor of vicariance patterns.  The truth is almost certainly going to

be a mix of the two for many taxa (much to the discomfort of those who
are heavily invested in vicariance patterns and seem to have a visceral
reaction to dispersal patterns that might be challenging them).              
                ---------Ken Kinman                             
P.S.  I am not holding my breath for any fossil Amphisbaenians at all in

the uppermost Cretaceous, much less earlier (before South America and
Africa separated).  I just can't believe that Cretaceous amphisbaenians
(if they existed at all) were less fossilizable or less discoverable
than those throughout the Cenozoic.  These are not the kind of animals I

would expect to have undiscovered ghost lineages.  Those who think such
a ghost lineage exists in this case are probably indulging in wishful
thinking.  So in my view, I am siding with authorities with the widest
sampling of taxa and data, and those citing "authorities" basing
conclusions on less sampling and less data would do well to put up or
shut up.
