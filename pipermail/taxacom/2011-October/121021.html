In my opinion, Costello et al. ought to have claimed only that all existing estimates of global biodiversity are unreliable, without adding another unreliable estimate to the pot, particularly since?their new low estimate could be used and quoted by those who would chop down all the rainforests (i.e., "associate professor at respected university says that there aren't thousands of species going extinct before they?can be discovered?or documented")?. We should surely take the cautious approach and admit that we just don't know the extent of global biodiversity. I'm not even going to comment any further on the crazy notion that we could document all species within 100 years!
?
Surely, the obvious problem with using existing databases for biodiversity estimates is that the data is full of various biases, due in part to the fact that taxonomy has not been in proportion to greatest need, but has progressed very much by subjective selection of convenient groups and localities. Furthermore, I know for fact, for example, just how bad the fit with reality is between the NZ fauna on paper and both in the field and even in collections. It must be far worse for the megadiverse tropical regions...
?
Stephen

Yes.? Actually it seems all of the estimates from existing databases (as
opposed to field studies using extrapolatory methods) appear to
underestimate overall richness (sometimes severely).

As Rod says, why? the answers may provide some insights into how some
groups are systematically undersampled, others are clearly underdescribed
relative to others based on what we now know, etc.? A way forward might be
then clear. Of course, it could be that one or the other of these recent
studies is actually fairly accurate, in which case we have to re-think
other things! I am betting not, however.
