Hi All, 
?? ?????It seems that John Grehan is so excessively
vicariance-oriented that he wants to explain away rare dispersal events
in any way that he can, branding them as "far-fetched" or even
"fantasies" and even comparing the scientists proposing such events to
creationists. So I would bounce back his initial comment "Good Grief"
right back at him. ? ? ? ? 
?? ?????Anyway, flotsam from North America explaining some

Caribbean biological distributions seems a perfectly valid hypothesis,
on the scale of million years (or even less). And rare longer distance
flotsam events over tens of millions of years seems a reasonable
hypothesis as well. ? ? ?? ? ? ? 
?? ????Frankly I think that the power of two major tsunamis
in just the last decade (off Japan and earlier off Sumatra) is just a
hint of how devasatating those (or even larger forces over geological
time periods) can be in sweeping large masses of flotsam (perhaps even
thousand of trees on rare occasions) into the ocean as the water
retreat. Then it just takes favorable currents to occasionally sweep
some of them to another continent. ?? 
????????A large clump of hundreds of trees (or perhaps
even just a few dozen trees for that matter) carrying a colony of
hoatzins from Africa to South America in one rare event does not seem a
fantasy at all. As long as the trees had leaves to munch on (better yet
some fruits as well), such vegetarians could no doubt get by for weeks,
if not months, until they reached South America. It doesn't seem
far-fetched to me, and it only had to happen once. ? 
????????Seems just as likely to have happened once as
John's alternative explanation that hoatzins evolved extremely early
(Late Cretaceous??) in a connected Africa-South American region. So
where is the evidence that hoatzins have been around that long? And I
especially have a hard time imagining a specialized and tree-dwelling
bird like the hoatzin surviving the devastation of the end-Cretaceous
extinction (unless it was perhaps geographically much further removed,
as in Australia). Therefore, a Cenozoic tsunami-flotsam event seems a
lot more survivable, landing in South America with plenty of luxurious
Cenozoic vegetation to eat upon arrival, as opposed to the fire-storms
that must have devastated the forests of the Americas at the end of the
Cretaceous.? 
? ? ? Calling such a dispersal a fantasy or even far-fetched seems to
underestimate the potential of rare, massive, forces of nature to exceed

our expectations in dispersing taxa in ways that were previously totally

unexpected.? And one could easily argue that it is sometimes proposed
vicariance (especially in the crocodile case) which is far-fetched.
Vicariance has its place, but to invoke it excessively can be
counterproductive.? ? ? ? ? ?
