These broad genomic changes can be happening?throughout a population (e.g. broad shifts in CG composition), not because of random mutation in an individual followed by gene flow, adaptation and selection.?

In any case you are not explaining how the application of random genetic forces applies to your version of vicariance and neither do Lynch nor Stoltzfus. 

MH: The genetic forces are not random - that's the point.

In fact they point fingers at the non-mathematically inclined biologists that place too much stress on selection (i.e. most of us in TAXACOM). 

MH: There are non-random forces that are not selection.

Still I find their work to be similar to Wright?s shifting balance theorem, although I admit not goint over their papers carefully. Nevertheless, they stress not the organisms but the genes. 

MH: Well, they are geneticists. Actually their arguments stress?genomic evolution.

In this point they differ from Mayr, 

MH: who was not a geneticist.

for whom the genes were interdepent so the unit of selection was the genome, which was as well shielded from the outside by the phenotype. Notwithstanding this, he recognised the role that random genetic forces (drift, mutations, recombination) had in the evolution of populations and species.

MH: Yes, he thought everything was random.

You keep bringing THE quote from 1965 as it somehow defines his ideas, but his body of work speaks otherwise. 

MH: I think it?defines his ideas in a?clear and succinct way.?I've read most of what Mayr wrote, including dozens of?his early papers.

?...a widespread ancestor 'breaks down' into subordinate groups by repeated, non-random mutation.?
and Mayr?s 1976 quote: "Not all population discontinuities in nature originate by the colonization of islands. Sometimes they arise by the contraction of a species range and the separation of a previously continuous area into separate ranges." (Mayr 1976)?
It is obvious that the second part of the statement is perfectly compatible with vicariance of any sort. So I am not sure why you take issue with it. 

MH: I don't take issue with it. The point is he did not apply this principle in practice, to his analyses of actual groups. This was written in 1976, a couple of years after critique of his work had begun to appear in journals such as Syst. Zool.? 

Furthermore your own quote is parapatric speciation paraphrased.
Mayr, in the historical context and with the data of the 40?s, 50?s and 60?s was correct. You are judging him in hindsight, enjoying the benefits of the huge advances in population genetics and phylogenetics. 

MH: I'm?interested in Mayr only as the?source, the type specimen,?of the current?ideas - most people accept the modern synthesis as basically correct, give or take sympatric speciation, founder effect speciation?etc.?

Did Mayr make mistkaes? Yes, he did, from our present context. Not accepting recognising speciation was a mistake

MH: you seem to have missed out something here - 'sympatric'?? I think he was correct to dismiss sympatric speciation. Most supposedly sympatric groups?actually show high levels of allopatry and the area of sympatry probably represents secondary overlap. The limnetic and benthic forms of three-spined sticklebacks in the British Columbia lakes turned out not to form monophyletic pairs in lakes.
