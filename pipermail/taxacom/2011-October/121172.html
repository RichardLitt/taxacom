 Ha! I wondered whether or not to wait until someone else jumps to the wrong conclusion. My position is, and still is, that molecular results have no automatic precedence over morphology. That is the fundamental position I hold. 

All I have argued in the list and in the literature is that there are potential methodological and conceptual factors in molecular analysis that can result in erroneous results. 

In the orangutan case the range and extent of morphological similarities with humans make the rhetorical assertion that it must be wrong very problematic - in my opinion, and in this case I lean towards the morphological result.

As Peter is or should be aware, I have used molecular phylogenies in my biogeographic analyses. I agree with Mike that molecular studies render biogeographically informative results, which does lead credence to their having some validity. When working with biogeography I used phylogenies as they are presented - whether morphological or molecular, with the caveat that either may be problematic - but one cannot individually study to death every group.

As Mike points out, where incongruence occurs, it may be with one set of characters, but not another. In morphology one is in a position to individually re-evaluate proposed homologies, but there is not the same potential in molecular analysis.

In some cases the molecular-morphological incongruence may not matter so much biogeographically. Take the case of the tarsier. Some (majority maybe - I forget) molecular results support tarsiers being the sister group of anthropoids. The cladistic morphology supports tarsiers being either the sister group of lorises, or even perhaps a member of the Lorisidae. The morphology would tie the tarsiers in more directly with the African-Asian lorisids, but it would seem to change nothing much regarding the biogeographic position of the tarsiers overall.

John Grehan
