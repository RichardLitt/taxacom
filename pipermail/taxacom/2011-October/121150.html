Ken,

No doubt Mike will have his own response, but with respect to your comment that "If you or John can offer a shred of evidence to counter the views of these specialists in amphisbaenian evolution, I would like to hear it" I think you are being disingenuous to demand positive evidence when the molecular dispersalist do not. They only have minimal dates, from which they then impose a particular faith about their meaning, as you do also. 

The empirical point is that the minimal dates do not preclude an earlier origin, and when these individual instances match up to patterns in general for plants and animals of different means of dispersal, and show spatial correlations with Mesozoic tectonics, one does indeed have a basis to suggest another minimal estimate. 

There are plenty of examples of fossils turning up that are many millions of years older than the date of origin that was "previously thought" (to quote the often used clich?). Most extant species of plants and animals have no fossil record whatsoever. I guess they all evolved only yesterday!


As for "Otherwise, I have to go with the evidence published by these various herpetologists who actually study this group" there is a difference between being an expert on the molecules of some group and having expert knowledge and understanding of biogeography.

John Grehan

Hi Michael,
       Well, the clades "could" perhaps be older than they estimated,
but I doubt "much older" if you are suggesting that these families split

up during the Cretaceous.  Amphisbaenians have a pretty good fossil
record in the Cenozoic, and only the most basal family goes back to the
Middle Paleocene.  So where is the fossil record of all these families
in the Cretaceous?  I doubt that they exist, because the living families

increasingly appear to have split up during the early-to-mid Cenozoic.  

        And I'm not talking just about Vidal's paper.  Take a look at
the recent paper published by Muller et al., 2011, in Nature, describing

Cryptolacerta (an Eocene fossil which appears to be part of the sister
group to Amphisbaenia).  Their phylogeny and timeline (based on both
morphological and molecular data) indicates that Amphisbaenia originated

near the beginning of the Cenozoic, with the basal family Rhineuridae
splitting off in the early Paleocene from the clade containing the other

families.   

        Sure, this basal split could have occurred in the uppermost
Cretaceous, with Rhineuridae and the other clade both surviving the
end-Cretaceous extinction. However, neither the fossil record nor
molecular data supports your position that it happen even earlier than
that (before Africa and South America separated).  If you or John can
offer a shred of evidence to counter the views of these specialists in
amphisbaenian evolution, I would like to hear it.  Nitpicking at their
papers is easy, but let's here some solid evidence from you that
Amphisbaenia even existed (much less diversified) before Africa and
South America separated.  Otherwise, I have to go with the evidence
published by these various herpetologists who actually study this group,

and whose conclusions generally make sense to me and are backed up by a
variety of data.
          ------------Ken Kinman
