Hi Stephen,
       Logically, we must remember that Reptilia (before birds evolved from them in the Mesozoic) was once a holophyletic (strictly monophyletic) taxon.    But having so much success and given rise to so much diversity and numbers of taxa (especially after their dinosaur ancestors were wiped out),  birds were naturally regarded as distinct from reptiles and thus given their own Class.   Amphibians likewise are classified as distinct from their sarcopterygian fish ancestors.  And embryophytes (metaphytes) are classified as distinct from their charophyte ancestors, so much so that they are usually given their own Kingdom Metaphyta (or Plantae sensu stricto).           
        Therefore, it has always seemed to me logical not to give a new name to Reptilia, but to simply add an exgroup marker {{Aves}} to show where the birds branched off cladistically within the theropod dinosaurs.   Same with a {{Metaphyta}} marker within Charophyta, and a {{Tetrapoda}} exgroup marker within Class Sarcopterygii.   This is not only more informative, but it avoids most of the instability that "strict"  cladism has caused in its continued assault on perfectly good paraphyletic taxa.   The Linnean hierarchy has managed to hold on in spite of the increasing strain this assault has placed on it, but just barely.   Why bioinformatics so often gravitates to the strictly cladistic methodology, which is inherently unstable, continues to baffle me.   Exgroup markers are much more logical, and if you want to add a coding system to show cladistic relationships, all the better (but you certainly can't do that with an alphabetical listing).

>  Hi Tony and Robin,       
>                   I have to disagree.  I
>  don't think this was inevitable (or wise or brave).  We
>  have long known (since Archaeopteryx) that birds evolved
>  from reptiles, but that is not a good reason to subsume one
>  into the other.  By such reasoning, we could argue that
>  Amphibia and the other tetrapod classes should be subsumed
>  into Sarcopterygii.  Or subsuming all the higher plant
>  phyla into Phylum Charophyta.  Or how about dumping all the
>  insects into the Crustacea.  Such reasoning is just as
>  unwise with respect to Aves as it is with these and many
>  other examples.  So I don't think it is inevitable that
>  birds have to be dumped in with their reptile (dinosaur)
>  ancestors, any more than it is would be inevitable that we
>  dump tetrapods in with the sarcopterygian fish (or Insecta
>  into Crustacea).
