Hi Stephen,

A piece of string is as long as it needs to be to perform a specified task.
In this case, you still seem to be having trouble understanding what the
specific task of the CoL consensus taxonomy is.  Tony Rees answered much
more effectively than I could have; but the key point to understand is that
a consensus taxonomy of this sort is established explicitly to facilitate
harmony among disparate datasets, to more effectively allow cross-dataset
comparisons, and to dramatically simplify activities that benefit from a
general organization of biological names into a rough/approximate SHARED
classification.  In this context, the importance of stability and shared use
of the consensus classification across multiple data-providing constituents
VASTLY exceeds the importance of reflecting the evolutionary speculation du
jour.  We have plenty of information streams to provide dynamic/evolutionary
classifications as new information becomes available in real time.  What
we've been lacking (prior to the establishment of this CoL consensus
taxonomy), is a community-oriented classification "flag-pole" around which
data content providers can collectively rally.

Also, your reference to a "new species" further reveals your
misunderstanding of what this consensus classification is intended for. If
you had read the paper, and downloaded the classification, you would see
that it only goes down as far as the rank of Order.  More granular changes
to taxonomy (e.g., families, genera, species, etc.) are not included (those
are in the realm of the much-more dynamically updated CoL itself).
Personally, I think a consensus classification at the rank of Order and
higher probably does not need to be updated more than once a decade, but I
suppose that revisiting it every five years is a reasonable frequency.

In any case, I agree with Tony that the discussion has gotten a bit off
track.  

Aloha,
Rich
