Evolution is ongoing, and different features follow different patterns in
different species.  Both molecular and morphological characters may provide
clear differences or intergradation, and what works in one species may not
in another.  Freshwater mollusks are extremely subject to ecophenotypic
variation, and have also experienced major rounds of lumping and of
splitting, so there's little agreement on what is a valid species.  Thus,
molecular evidence is quite useful, though all of the already-mentioned
caveats apply.  However, molecular data turns up problems such as
contamination and paralogs.

A difficulty in calculating a percent is "out of what?"  The percent of
studies on freshwater mollusks that make some use of molecular data in
identification might be a bit higher (
http://ellipse.inhs.uiuc.edu:591/mollusk/biblio.html would allow the
generation of some statistics, remembering the possible biases of the
database).  But the percent of specimens identified by molecular means is
always going to be quite low, and there are still a lot of species not
analyzed.

Additionally, a molecular result does not automatically yield agreement on
the species status.  Lumpers and splitters remain.
