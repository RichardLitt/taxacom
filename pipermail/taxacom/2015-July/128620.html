Sure, one could argue indefinitely about how to contruct a "better"
consensus classification.  But these LITERALLY endless arguments are exactly
why no such comprehensive classification has previously been presented FOR
THE PURPOSES INTENDED BY THIS classification.  The value of this listing is
not the "classification" part, so much as it is the "consensus" part.
Consensus is not the same thing as unanimity.  You can certainly convey your
personal opinions (as listed below) to the architects of the list, and maybe
a future version will be altered to reflect them (assuming your opinions
resonate with the broader consensus).

 

In any case, sequencing of names in a list is an incredibly BAD way to
represent phylogenetic information; ESPECIALLY in the context of that this
particular list is intended for.  If you want to represent affinities of
taxa, there are three basic ways to do it:

1)      Come up with a mark-up scheme (like yours)

2)      Acknowledge names at ever finer units of intermediate ranks, so that
relationships can be represented within nested sets

3)      Draw a cladogram

 

For the purposes of this list, any sequence other than alphabetical
(particularly if some sort of information is intended to be conveyed by the
particular sequence) would have been a very silly thing to do.

 

Aloha,

Rich

Hi Rich,

        I think John is being overly harsh.  Overall, it's not a bad
classification, but I think it certainly could be made better.  The protist
classification is very good, thanks to the work of Thomas Cavalier-Smith.
It's too bad he couldn't get his co-authors to classify Posibacteria and
Archaebacteria together in one Kingdom (and Negibacteria in another).  I
still think alphabetizing the lists is still a sad loss of information.  It
may be a bit more convenient for some, but I'm sure many others will find
the loss of information more than just an inconvenience.     

          I don't understand why they didn't subdivide the list of orders of
eutherian mammals (and splitting Pilosa and Cingulata as separate orders
unfortunately eliminated the more familiar name Xenarthra, as well as being
separated alphabetically).  And of course, I would raise Aves back to Class
status, and add an {{Aves}} marker within Reptilia.  Anyway, even if they
insist on retaining the alphabetical "convention" for the convenience of
some, there is still plenty of room for improvement in my opinion.


                    ---------------Ken                    

P.S.  Hexapoda is probably polyphyletic.  Collembola almost certainly
evolved hexapody independently from one group of crustaceans, and the
insects from a different group of crustaceans

> Hmmm.... I've been deliberately quiet on this thread as well. I appreciate
the viewpoints of Ken and others in terms of holophyly, the value of
acknowledging paraphyletic taxa through Linnean nomenclature, etc. This is
all good philosophical grit, and I enjoy the intellectual stimulation as
much as anyone.
> 
> However, this thread began (and remains?) relevant to the PLoS ONE
publication of Ruggiero et al., which provides a generalized **CONSENSUS
CLASSIFICATION**. Perhaps there is some misunderstanding on what is meant by
this term (and the associated classification)? Anyone who read as far as the
abstract of this paper should at least know that:
> 
> "This classification is neither phylogenetic nor evolutionary but instead
represents a consensus view that accommodates taxonomic choices and
practical compromises among diverse expert opinions, public usages, and
conflicting evidence about the boundaries between taxa and the ranks of
major taxa, including kingdoms."
> 
> And:
> 
> "Beyond its immediate use as a management tool for the CoL and ITIS
(Integrated Taxonomic Information System), it is immediately valuable as a
reference for taxonomic and biodiversity research, as a tool for societal
communication, and as a classificatory "backbone" for biodiversity
databases, museum collections, libraries, and textbooks."
> 
> In this context, it is ABSOLUTELY CORRECT that the subtaxa within a taxon
are alphabetically arranged! It is absolutely NOT intended to reflect any
sort of "naturalness" in patterns of evolution. Yes, it probably does kinda-
sorta- happen to reflect modern views of evolutionary relationships
(otherwise there wouldn't be much consensus behind it). But its function is
to serve as a tool for informatics and generalized (i.e., highly simplified)
organizational representation of life on Earth. As such, it is
overwhelmingly valuable to the people for whom it was intended (see second
quote above).

requirement of
> > science. Alphabetization is repeatable.

> > Principles of phylogenetic classification are artificial. Some
principles include
> > strict monophyly (so long Aves), putting known taxa at the top of a tree
of
> > relationships ("tree-thinking") rather than in the tree when known
(stem-
> > thinking), ignoring the probability of extinct paraphyly that casts
doubt on
> > molecular trees, ignoring the evolutionary importance of autapomorphies,
> > asserting that only zero length branches imply a surviving ancestral
taxon,
> > pretending that a cladogram is "the evidence" (when it is only some
> > evidence) and evolutionary theory is mere narrative . . . you can
probably
> > think of more phylogenetic principles of classification that do violence
to
> > evolutionary theory developed only after long struggle with artificial
> > classifiers of the past.
> >
> > Classification by phylogenetic principles is actually quite
unprincipled, and

> > taxonomy.
