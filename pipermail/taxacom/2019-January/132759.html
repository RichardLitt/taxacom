Hi all,

I am "guilty" of being the lead author of the paper David mentioned -as well as many other papers on the fauna of Microgastrinae wasps of Area de Conservacion Guanacaste (ACG) in Costa Rica, which have been published over the past 7-8 years. I think we have described 350+ new species of Microgastrinae from ACG, and we still have 800 or more new species awaiting description from that subfamily of parasitoid wasps. And most of those ACG species are named after collectors (the "gusaneros" or parataxonomists that walk the area collecting caterpillars and rearing them until adults or parasitoids emerge), or local students, or local managers/politicians that help preserve that natural wonder that is ACG. I should also disclose that most of the credit for doing that goes to Daniel Janzen and Winnie Hallwachs, as they have made an effort (for the past 35+ years or so) to protect and research ACG. And the result (beyond microgastrines) have been hundreds of names after people that either collected specimens or contributed in one way or another towards the work done in the ACG.

Personally, I agree with Mary when she said that naming after collectors build good will, and there is nothing wrong with that. Giving credit to others (indirectly via naming species after them) feels great, helps to foster collaboration and makes many people smile (I have gotten back such feedback from people that have felt touched by species I named after them... and it also feels good to me ;-)). 

Plus, there is the frequently overlooked advantage of naming species after people: species names tend to be more unique (and thus potential homonyms are minimized... Unless your name is as common as mine: there are hundreds of Jose Fernandez in the world! ;-)  
[Of course, I now read the comment from John Grehan that long names increase the potential for misspellings... Although he may have a point there, the argument goes both ways, as there are also very long species names that have nothing to do with honoring people. So I see both advantages and disadvantages of naming species after people's names].

As others have mentioned before me, the point of who "discovered" something is really a subjective issue. Personally, I would have preferred to name the species after the local collector (I have nothing against Wallace! But he has been recognized in a number of other ways by scientists, including his own "Wallace line", ain't that cool?). But I also see the point of Wallace being the one that realized it was a new species. And the local cultures there probably knew that species long before the two men of this story got the specimen... So, my personally reply to the original description is that it really is not clear cut nor perfectly defined (who "discovered" the species) and it should not matter either.  

And, anyways, we, the taxonomists, should not feel that important because we "name" species (although that is certainly cool), and should focus more on the quality of the papers produced. But crediting as many people as possible is my preferred way to go.

There is a point for another discussion (but I just do not have the time now) on how naming new species could be used more (by the taxonomic community) to get support for taxonomic research (not talking here about for profit business), and how we have, as community, failed to seize that opportunity. Perhaps in another time I will change my ideas on that.

Cheers,
Jose
