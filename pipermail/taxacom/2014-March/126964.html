Hi Jason,

You asked: 'So the question is, can you accept the fact that stuff moves
and that this ability to move, a meter or a thousand kilometers, is
essentially the same even if the
probability of the latter to be successful is much, much smaller than
the former?'

People have been saying for decades that panbiogeography/vicariance denies
dispersal. Dispersal or movement of organisms is obvious, even to a child,
and claiming we deny it is simply an attempt to make us look foolish.  So,
for the 1000th time (once a week for the last 30 years): I accept the fact
that stuff moves.

Here's an example (adapted from a manuscript) of the two kinds of dispersal
I've been talking about, real and imaginary, and also the two kinds of
chance.


Normal, observed dispersal and inferred, chance dispersal have both been
examined in detail in five alpine species of *Veronica* from New Zealand
(Pufal and Garnock-Jones, 2010). The average seed dispersal distance was 13
cm and the greatest distance

covered by a single dispersal event was 1.1 m. This valuable data could be
used to calculate the chances of particular population processes occurring
('chance' being used here in the Fermat/Pascal sense of calculated
probability).

   Two of the studied species (*V. ciliolata* and *V. densifolia**) also
occur in SE Australia. B*ased on clock studies, the authors inferred that
these have both dispersed ~1700 km from New Zealand to Australia, against
the prevailing winds. This would represent a

leap of a magnitude 1.5 million times greater than the maximum observed
dispersal distance. Meudt and Bayly (2008) concluded that the mechanisms of
the process in the two species are 'unknown' and 'hard to envisage'. Pufal
and Garnock-Jones (2010)

argued that *the trans-Tasman migration is not the result of the normal,
observed mode *

*of dispersal. Instead, it '*is probably due to chance secondary dispersal,
as rare long-distance dispersal is more often not related to the primary
[mode of] dispersal'.  Note

that 'chance' as used here in 'chance dispersal' does not refer to a
calculated

probability, it just means 'it happened, but we have no idea how' (the
ancient, pre-Fermat/Pascal idea of chance as 'luck' or 'fortune').

Michael

> "The new, revolutionary idea of chance as a calculated probability
> began with Pascal and Fermat in the 17th century and became a founding
> principle of modern science. For example, a probability-cloud diagram
> of seed dispersal depicts how 'chance' (in the new, modern sense)
> determines aspects of normal ecological dispersal. On the other hand,
> 'chance dispersal' uses chance in the old sense of 'factors beyond our
> understanding' or 'given enough time anything can happen'; 'chance'
> here is a very different concept and is not a real explanation for
> anything."
> Chance or probability, as used by anybody outside of casino amateurs,
> is the likelihood of an event happening in a given period. So it may
> or may not happen and you have no way of knowing if it will for sure.
> So yes, I agree, it is beyond our understanding why one particular
> gravid fly in a million successfully crossed an ocean. But overall
> every fly had a "chance" which, with the appropriate data, can be
> determined. And since this is used in physics and they are doing
> nicely with it, I have no problems using it myself.
