Regarding Robert's observation that " Going back to the humans vs.
orang-utan vs. chimp problem: what about when one uses behaviour as
classification criterion? If you compare behaviour vs. morphology/anatomy,
then you obtain a conflict, as each set of characters results in a
different classification. There is no objective way to sort this out, other
than using an unrelated set of characters, DNA sequence data. These tell us
that behaviour is a better predictor of relationships in this case than
morphology/anatomy."

I would view behaviour as an expression of morphology/anatomy in the sense
that all of this represents characteristics of developmental pathways. I
contrast that to base pair alignment or base pair counting. I sometimes
wonder whether divergence is best estimated by unique divergence in
developmental pathways or some overall measure of base pair similarity. I
don't know whether that is resolvable. For the most part molecular trees
give credible results (in terms of morphology) but quite a few cases of
glaring inconsistency and not involving antiquated notions of the
morphology. I look at the human sister group question as the ultimate
example of the challenge. While I have my own opinion favoring the
orangutan, it is really just my opinioni. Interestingly for me, when I
approached varous molecular systematists about the issue I was met with
silence or outright hostility. If the morphology could be objectively
refuted they could have shown that. But declined. And the hominid
systematics world continues to do so, even to the point of ensuring no
reference to the orangutan evidence (especially by the paleontologists
involved).

On behaviour, when some human characteristic shows up in chimps it gets a
huge amount of publicity. When it is an orangutan, nothing. Just a 'chance'
anomaly. It is a possibility that the chimp-human clade is true (and
converse of course), and all the uniquely shared human-orangutan features
are plesiomorphies that were lost in the African apes (including reversion
to more primitive states such as external estrus changes, monkey external
ear etc.), along with pretty much any morphological evidence of derived
human-chimp features. It sometimes seems as if God is laughing at us. Make
it look like we have an orangutan ancestor, when we don't. The literature
is filled with an amazing amount of contortions trying to make a human out
of a chimpanzee. All very entertaining if nothing else.

I was informed by one orangutan biologist that when chimpanzee biologists
visited an orangutan field site, they would sometimes whisper to her that
'orangutans are smarter than chimps'. Whispered, because of course it did
not fit the evolutionary scheme. And what does not fit is never said aloud.

Cheers, John

> Under the pressure of an overwhelming number of problematic new taxa, we
> call for taxonomists to incorporate phylogenetic methods and reasoning into
> their taxonomic work. I understand that many may disagree, but please allow
> me to explain.
>
> This paper briefly summarizes why taxonomy is a science, how taxonomy can
> be a science, how the ideas and methods of Kant, Darwin, Hennig, and Popper
> have influenced modern taxonomy, and why contemporary taxonomic research
> should not be separated from phylogenetics, both from the perspectives of
> history and the philosophy of science.
>
> To reach readers who may be new to phylogenetics, we must emphasize that
> phylogenetics is not limited to molecular data. Although I belong to
> Generation Z, I am aware that phylogenetics was first widely applied to
> morphological characters long before molecular sequencing became common.
> While morphology-based phylogenetics may suffer from homoplasy, something
> is still better than nothing. Simply describing morphological patterns
> without an explicit phylogenetic framework is less informative and less
> scientific than conducting morphology-based phylogenetic analyses.
>
> I am aware of previous arguments presented in the paper "Should we
> describe genera without molecular phylogenies?", whose author strongly
> opposed our views and directly criticized our commentary during the review
> process. That paper lacked a clear understanding of phylogenetics based on
> morphological data, as also revealed during the review process of our own
> manuscript. The author wrote, "not to mention fossil taxa, which also need
> to be placed in the system obviously without molecular support." I believe
> most paleontologists would disagree with this statement, as fossil taxa can
> indeed be placed within a phylogenetic framework using morphological
> characters.
>
> In response to claims such as "we should invest more trust in the
> taxonomic evaluations of the decreasing number of taxonomists and allow
> more freedom for morphology-based grouping," I would say: "Taxonomy should
> not become theology, where people are expected to believe without
> evidence." Furthermore, I personally disagree with the statement "taxonomy
> is a science, and all new taxa that are proposed are hypotheses that can be
> refuted and falsified." A taxon described solely by a fixed morphological
> pattern, as the author often does, cannot truly be falsified, since any
> additional variation can easily be reinterpreted as representing a new
> taxon. Such direct assertions are not falsifiable, much like religious
> doctrines. When synonyms or new taxonomic acts are later proposed, it
> merely reflects a redefinition of original taxa using new assertions or
> occasionally falsifiable methods.
>
> Ultimately, this commentary only represents our perspective on taxonomic
