Kirk,

Zander may well have a different response, but for me the question of " how
do you know you have an erroneous result, from which you can decide not to
explain observations in the same inference" I would say (If I understand
the question correctly) that when it comes to molecular genetic and
developmental genetic inference there is no unambiguous answer - at least
not for now. In reference to the molecular and developmental genetic
contradiction for human origins (since this is the only example I have
worked with considering the two sources of data), my view is that there is
insufficient empirical justification for combining the data into one 'total
evidence' since the former does not provide individual unequivocal derived
states whereas the latter does, and it is my (admittedly minority) position
that only the latter (developmental genetic) data that
conforms adequately to cladistic requirements.

And just a reminder - please send me the two articles you cited.

John Grehan

> >> I see "total evidence" as the need to explain all relevant facts, facts
> >> being well-documented observations.
> >>
> >> This means first you get rid of facts that are not relevant. In
> >> evolution this means descent with modification of the particular taxon
> >> you are studying. Then you explain all relevant evidence in some
> >> inclusive theory, OR explain in some reasonable way why you can't. For
> >> instance, suppose you get observations of some process that are one
> >> "red" observation and 20 "green" observations. You can't just throw out
> >> the "red." You can't just throw out the "red" because it is rare or
> >> inconvenient. If "red" was generated by a different aspect of the
> >> process you are analyzing, then this is an explanation.
> >>
> >> If morphological analysis says "red" and molecular analysis says "green"
> >> with a ton of more data then you have to explain why morphological
> >> analysis says "red." "Red" is a fact and a probability distribution does
> >> not explain it.
