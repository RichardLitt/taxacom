Thanks, Francisco.

> Does "AGTC" allow to differentiate?

Here are a list of possible words that might appear in a morphology-based description:

1) "Green"

2) "Green head"

3) "Distinguished by its green head"

4) "Distinguished from other taxa in having a green head"

5) "Distinguished from taxon X, Y, and Z by having a green head"

6) "Distinguished in having a green head, unlike taxon X, Y and Z which all have a red head"

I could easily give other examples, but my question is:  which one of these represents the minimum threshold for "purport to differentiate".

What if each of the above sets of words was preceded by the word "Diagnosis:".  Would most people not interpret that word to mean "the words that follow express the characters that I purport to differentiate the taxon"?  If so, would something like:

"Diagnosis: Green" not fulfill the requirement, as current written in Code-4?

I used morphological characters, but you can insert the "AGTC..." equivalents in place of "green", and something like "COI gene" in place of "head".

> > I agree with your broader point, but the pedant in me needs to add a quick
> comment:  a reasonable case can be made that "A", "G", "T" and "C" as
> presented in a description are "words" in the sense that they are letters that
> represent words (Adenine, Guanine, Thymine and Cytosine) -- every bit as
> much as a morphological diagnosis containing "D-XIII,17" counts as an
> abbreviation for "Dorsal-fin with 13 spines and 17 soft rays".  If abbreviations
> are not, themselves, "words", then a whole lot of minimalist morphological
> descriptions over the past two and a half centuries for currently-available and
> Code-compliant names would be put in jeopardy.
> >
> > So, I think a strong case can be made that a DNA sequence, when
> presented in the form of "AGTC...etc.", counts as a "description...that states
> in words characters purported to differentiate the taxon".
