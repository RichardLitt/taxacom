> 
> This sort of discussion periodically flares on taxacom (and often
> with the same participants). Actually looking at the maligned
> molecular data might be a useful approach to the issue. I've included
> a small fasta file of hominidae sequences for cytochrome oxidase II.
> I downloaded it from phylota ( http://loco.biosci.arizona.edu/cgi-bin/
> sql_getcluster.cgi?ti=9604&cl=61&ntype=1&db=GB159 ), added taxon
> names to the annotations, and trimmed off the regions of DNA present
> in only a subset of the taxa (and if this is a controversial step,
> feel free to get the original data from the above URL and play with
> it directly). One can load this file in a favorite sequence alignment
> program that allows manual alignment (MacClade and Mesquite are
> popular), or even just print out the sequences and truly manually
> align them, and "go and see that" the reasonable alignment is one
> with no indels, even though the sequence data are rather divergent
> (up to 16% pairwise differences) and thus potentially difficult to
> align. For a mitochondrial protein-coding gene, the lack of indels
> and thus trivial alignment is what you'd expect. One can also try
> various "theoretical mathematical models" (Clustal, Muscle, etc.)
> using reasonable parameter values (such as the program default
> values) and verify that these algorithmic approaches return the same
> results as the gold standard eyeball analysis. Of course, one can set
> bizarre parameter values to get wacky results, just as one could
> assert that ears in elephants are homologous with fins in sharks, but
> barring this, in many cases molecular homology statements will be
> reasonable and uncontroversial.
> 
> Perhaps the morphological characters are giving the correct answer
> while the molecular data are consistently misleading about hominidae
> relationships, but if so, the explanation has to come from somewhere
> other than some innate arbitrariness of alignment. Sometimes
> alignment IS hard and leads to ambiguous or even erroneous results,
> but often, especially with exons of protein-coding genes, it's just
> not that bad. See for yourself.
