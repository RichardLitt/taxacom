Bureaucracy and barriers associated with scientific collecting permits cause species and biodiversity loss!
I was once approached by a student that wanted to save an endangered species that occurred in the construction site of a new residential condominium. I was deeply move by his plea and love for Nature, but I had to tell him that this was an endangered species and that by the time we obtained a collecting permit the area would be gone. He told me that he knew the endangered species was there and that we should collect the fish to document it presence and publically denounce the situation. I then told him that doing so would amount to an admission of guilt for collecting an endangered species that could lead to heavy fine and even jail, and could even result in a law suit for invasion of private property.
I nevertheless applied for the scientific collecting permits and several months later we finally had all the permits, but it was too late: the area had already been closed, scraped and construction had started. It looks like somebody else tried to legally challenge the environmental report (which obviously did not report the species), because I later received a letter from the public prosecutor office asking if we had vouchers specimens from that area in our collection. I replied that we had no documentation in our collection database, and never heard of them anymore.
End of the story: The endangered species was extirpated from the area and we don´t even have a record or genetic material documenting its previous existence.
During my career as a biodiversity scientist I had numerous of cases where licensing barriers to scientific collecting and shipping severely hampered scientific research and even stopped international collaborations, but this one was to me the most frustrating case.
Bureaucratic barriers to scientific collection and collaboration are a major cause of biodiversity loss!

Yes, we are largely in agreement I think.

 The real limitation in most cases is a combination of not enough resources/expertise in taxonomy to process the specimens already in museums, let alone new inventories. If I wanted to revise the Apanteles wasps of the world, there are several million specimens already waiting for me in collections (albeit maybe not sampled in the way I would have preferred). And there are only a handful of people like me to deal with those specimens. So in our short timescales for conservation, we need to be coming up with plans that do not depend (in the short run at least) on large inventories. Those inventories are really for us, the taxonomists, and rarely are effective in supplying timely actionable conservation data, because completing an inventory taxonomically almost never happens. 

More investment in taxonomy!  More expertise! More people caring about these organisms! Collecting is, I think, a relatively minor issue for conservation, and more of a logistical issue for scientists. In my experience it is complicated more by the regulations not being very well informed by science, and by countries having unstable political systems, than by disagreements among the scientists. I do appreciate your efforts to try and make things more transparent and reasonable.
