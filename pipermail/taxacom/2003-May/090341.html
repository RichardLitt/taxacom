The problem here may be failure to recognize how cladistic methods have
evolved.  In the "early" days of cladistics, a great deal of effort went
into hypothesizing, for each character, a character-state tree.  These
trees were often simple (in the case of two-state characters, 0 -> 1),
slightly more complex (e.g., 0 -> 1 -> 2 ), or worse (e.g., 1 <- 0 -> 2,
etc.).  Each character was represented by its own phylogenetic tree.
One prominent cladist, when asked how the plesiomorphic state was
determined, replied "choose the next most closely related group."  As a
consequence, common=primitive (common in the ingroup, if the "next most
closely related group" was unknown; later expanded to include common in
the "next most closely related group), was an approach that was
sometimes used to determine the plesiomorphic character state for
constructing character trees.
************************************************************************
*************************
