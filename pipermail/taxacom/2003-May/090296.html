> I already have.

No you have not.  You have declared from principle that all molecular
characters are invalid as a source of information on common ancestry,
regardless of what you do with them.  That does not qualify as "direct
appraisal" in my mind.

Assuming that you admit that any principle used to guide inquiry or
interpretation can (and should) be subject to testing, here is a test
of your logic:  if molecular characters can never be used for
evolutionary analysis, then why do they so frequently give phylogenies
that are congruent with "real cladistic analyses"?  Are you really
saying that morphological and molecular trees never agree (or agree as
or less often than expected by chance)?  If not, then you have accepted
that molecular analyses reveal some historical information.  At this
point, I suggest you ask yourself HOW can this be?

My point is that instead of dogmatically dismissing all molecular
characters, you might consider actually analysing them--in whatever way
tickles you--and seeing what they have to say.  Maybe it would give you
a better appreciation for their value.   ...that is what I consider
"direct appraisal".

> The difference is that the morphological synapomorphies can each be
> treated on their own merits whereas the molecular characters are an
> unknown mix of apomorphic and plesiomorphic states. In Ken's case he
> is positing one set of synapomorphies (human-chimp) over another
> (human-orangutan).

No, i don't think so.  The process of considering molecular characters
can be done in exactly the same way as with morphological characters.
What is the difference between A versus T and tail versus no tail (as a
discrete character)??   The only difference is in how you treat the
characters, and there is no restriction on this, whatsoever...
