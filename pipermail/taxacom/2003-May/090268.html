I've since had the opportunity to read the Nature article. Christian's
comments on the positive evidence is certainly warranted. The article
itself which is basically a traditional biogeographic approach
incorporating geohistorical narratives to provide historical context. Thus,
the presumption of 'Gondwnana' comes not from biogeographic analysis, but
the acceptance of a particular geohistorical narrative for a particular
time period.

I'm lost as to what the biogeographic 'ointment' is intended to represent.
Some additional clarity would also have come from mentioning why there was
a 'long-held belief that the continent was never inhabited by these
insects' in the first place.

On the more positive side, the article presents artifactual evidence
supporting a formerly diverse Antarctic biota that is also predictable from
the position of the continent within the trans-oceanic tracks of many groups.

John Grehan

>Willi Hennig once suggested on the basis of zoogeographic analysis that

>possibilities: 1) higher flies may be older and may have been part of
>the earlier (Mesozoic) Gondwana fauna (that is, Hennig was wrong) or 2)
>they colonized Antarctica more recently.
>
>So, we concluded that "dipterists need to re-examine the taxonomy of
>southern cylcyorrphaphan fly taxa to see whether any trans-Antarctic
>relationships among the more derived clades have been overlooked."
