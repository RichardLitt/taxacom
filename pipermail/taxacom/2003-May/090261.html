Without yet seeing the article, I would concur with Ken that, in general
principle, there would be nothing to suggest that the apparent absence of
the taxon in Antarctica represents a historical reality. For the ghost
moths (Hepialidae), for example, there are no fossil or living taxa in
Antarctica, yet one cannot say they were never there. Similarly, they are
absent from Madagascar, the Caribbean, much of West Africa, and the
lowlands of the southeastern United States but this does not mean they were
never there.

There is a common technique in the biogeographic and evolutionary
literature (and this may not apply to the Thompson article) to proclaim
that a new find (modern or fossil) as changing what was 'previously
thought' about the age or distribution of that taxon to make the discovery
more significant and noteworthy. One does see this kind of rhetoric quite
often in Nature and Science, but it is widespread in other journals as
well. It will be interesting to see what the empirical issue is in
Thompson's article.
