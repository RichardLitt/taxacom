Wolfgang,
?
Two quick points in response: 
?
(1) you say "quorum, two-thirds majority required etc.", but majority of whom?? Whoever has an opinion? It isn't going to be the majority of the entire taxonomic community, or even the entire herpetological community (which would INCLUDE Hoser, Wells, Wellington, etc.) Do you really think that every herpetologist is going to bother to vote, or just those with a strong opinion? Why should the vote be restricted to herpetologists (for Hoser's names)? Reptiles are animals, so why not all zoologists,; animals are living things, so why exclude the botanists? I have no doubt that the institutional herpetological community could whip up a consensus fairly easily against any "outsider", dodgy or not.
?
(2) what irks me about all this is the equally unethical behaviour that I sometimes see happening WITHIN the institutional taxonomic community, e.g. using public funding to bolster publication records by pushing out regurgitated content of little substance, descriptions and illustrations which are a poor match to the actual taxa, exclusion of "outsiders" from access to "public collections", etc., etc. So, to some extent, the Hoser issue reaks somewhat of hypocrisy..
?
Cheers,
?
Stephen

Stephen,?

Petitioning the ICZN regarding Hoser specifically is something that has been (and is continuing to be) discussed. Unfortunately, the history of such petitions is not encouraging. The Commission already has the powers to suppress such works and/or the names therein by placing them on one of the Official Indexes, but, perhaps understandably, has historically been reluctant to use them.

I fully understand your fears regarding the delegation such decisions to the Community. However, if done with sufficient safeguards (quorum, two-thirds majority required etc.), then I would argue that a system such as the taxon filter proposed by Kaiser could be far *more* open, transparent and democratic than a decision by the Commission based on a relatively small number of submission by those who feel strongest about the matter. Unethical mob-rule would be *more* difficult, not *less* difficult under such a scenario, but it would bring the collective wishes of the community to bear directly on the decision-making process.

As to the impact of pseudoscientific taxonomy in herpetology, the evidence suggests that a very large number of herpetologists do indeed feel that it is severe - otherwise, Kaiser et al. would not have received the support they did, nor would their recommendations have been implemented so rapidly by several independent sets of authors and as the editorial policy of the African Journal of Herpetology. That impact extends way beyond concerns about authorship and credit. It includes impeding communication about ongoing projects (see Oliver & Lee, Taxon, 2010), disincentivisation of research on afflicted groups, ?and it further damages the reputation of taxonomy as a science (I wish I had a penny for every time a non-taxonomist has asked me what kind of crazy system we have where we can't just ignore the nomenclatural output of pseudoscientific taxonomy). Sure, the vast majority of the new names will disappear into synonymy, but given the scale of the
 output, that leaves more than enough that won't.

One of the problems with this kind of thing is that it impacts a small number of taxonomists substantially, whereas the majority remains unaffected. There is therefore, perhaps understandably, a tendency for the unaffected majority to dismiss or ignore the problems of the affected minority. That is something herpetologists are hoping to change.

Cheers,

Wolfgang

Wolfgang,
I am not entirely unsympathetic with you in relation to Hoser, but the whole issue is being handled wrongly. If you can make a strong enough case to show that Hoser's work really is damaging to herpetology, then the ICZN should be petitioned to make a ruling on Hoser, and Hoser alone. But they appear reluctant to rule in your favour, on principle, so they are trying to find a "back door" way of doing it, i.e. by putting the judgement in the hands of the taxonomic community, by way of entirely new and general Code provisions. These provisions may be acceptable applied to Hoser, but, because of their generality, they will be abused to exclude others who are working independently (and constructively) in taxonomy, mark my words.
That said, I am not convinced that Hoser's work is really all that damaging. I think it is more about him "stealing" credit by naming things cheaply which may turn out to be valid, and therefore carry his name as taxon authority. But?I don't see this as a serious issue, and I certainly don't think that it is within the mandate of the ICZN to intervene on this. Note that most of Hoser's new names appear to be supraspecific, which means that they are very easy to deal with, as they are based entirely on a type species, rather than on a possibly difficult to interpret original description or problematic type specimen of Hoser's creation.
Cheers,
Stephen

Apologies for prolonging this thread, but for the sake of fairness, I believe that the misconception that the problem in herpetology is a small problem involving just a few individuals and their egos should not go unchallenged.I would like to cordially invite anyone subscribing to that notion to actually read the Kaiser et al. (2013) paper, in particular the closing paragraphs, which list the scholarly herpetological societies that officially support the Point of View (and which collectively represent thousands of academic and amateur herpetologists) and, in the acknowledgements, a list of herpetologists who also individually endorse the stand taken.The paper can be downloaded from https://dl.dropboxusercontent.com/u/35959689/2013_Herp_Review_PoV.pdfTable 1 provides an idea of the scale of the problem for herpetologists, but is in fact woefully out of date: since the table was finalised in early 2013, approximately 250 additional new taxon names have
 been proposed by one single author in his self-published journal.The question of what, if anything, the Commission or the Code could or should do about problems of this nature is clearly highly contentious, as has been very obvious in this thread. Fundamentally, it touches on the relationship between the Code and the science of taxonomy: should the role of the Code be seen primarily as? supporting the science of taxonomy through the provision of a means of communicating about taxa, or should it be seen as entirely independent of the science of taxonomy?? If the former is the case, then can or should the Code and the Commission really remain entirely above the fray when the strict application of the Code is damaging to the performance of the science of taxonomy itself? If the latter is the majority view, does this not raise the question of whether the Code should invariably be regarded as relevant or binding by taxonomists when it hinders them in the
 performance of their job? I don't wish to start a debate about this on Taxacom, but this question lies at the heart of the fundamental questions that Commissioners Doug Yanega and Mark Harvey asked biologists to address in their comments, and might be usefully considered by those submitting comments in response to that request.Best regards,Wolfgang W?ster________________________________________From: taxacom-bounces at mailman.nhm.ku.edu <taxacom-bounces at mailman.nhm.ku.edu> on behalf of Kim van der Linde <kim at kimvdlinde.com>Sent: 10 January 2014 23:23To: Stephen Thorpe; taxacom at mailman.nhm.ku.eduSubject: Re: [Taxacom] [iczn-list] Dealing with taxonomic vandalism without the need to alter the zoological Code, this being relegation of bogus taxa to synonymyWell, if ego is the reason, even more reason for the Commission to stayout of it.KimOn 1/10/2014 5:59 PM, Stephen Thorpe wrote:> But Kim, the issue is more that the names might end up valid, but with>
