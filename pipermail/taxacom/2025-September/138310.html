Leslie - interesting comments. The terms 'avoid' and 'competition' are
tricky as they can pertain to foresight (I avoid walking on a cliff edge to
avoid falling off) to circumstantial 'avoidence' (I live in suburbia that
does not have tigers roaming loose, so I 'avoid' being eaten by tigers).
The former is teleological, the latter is not. So as I see it, what the
issue comes down to is whether 'avoidance' of 'competition' and predation
(what portion of intertidal species do not endure 'competition' do not
get predated - and how does that differ from marine or fully terrestrial
situations?) has anything to do with the origins of those species. I am
arguing that there is no evidence to suggest that these ecological
characteristics have anything to do with the origin of terrestrial life.
It's not as if some one species managed to first survive on land, but a
whole ecosystem comprising a multitude of species. Even among extant
organisms there are innumerable examples of tectonic uplift where upland or
montane taxa have their closest relatives on the coast. Funny thing is that
while Darwin saw the obvious origin of fossil sea shells in the mountains
(Andes if I recall correctly) he failed to see that possibility for animals
and plants (well, perhaps not so surprising given the multitude of
subsequent evolutionists who also failed to see this possibility). And the
converse is true - that coastal species may be subject to subsidence and
even tectonic displacement.

Cheers, John

> Those of us who have studied life in the marine intertidal have known for
> about 50 years, through both observation and experiment, that tide pools

> fluctuating conditions the intertidal offers, the species generally have to
> be physiologically capable, which would "pre-adapt" them for land
> colonization, I suppose.
>
> Whether tectonic uplift or some other factor was involved in the case of
> fished, who knows. But there is nothing wrong with the statement about
> avoiding competition and predation.

> > The referenced article is Szrek et al 2025 "Traces of dipnoan fish
> document

> > Authors make a couple of interesting points: "The process of vertebrate
> > terrestrialisation was therefore multi-staged, suggesting that the
> ability
> > to walk and/or near-walking in very shallow water may have existed even
> > before the appearance of tetrapods." They actually used the term
> > pre-adaptation. To me this is fine.
> >
> > But then they say "By moving towards land, the fishes avoided competition
> > and predation, gaining access
> > to new feeding areas." This is where teleology starts to creep in. They
> do
> > not elaborate, but the purposeful nature of the event is evident. The
> > authors, along with just about everyone else in paleontology (as far as I
> > know) fail to consider tectonic uplift as the key process that imposes an
> > increasingly terrestrial situation for coastal organisms that become
> > sufficiently stranded. Those with the critical 'preadaptations' survive,
> > and under those newer conditions will continue to evolve. those that do
> > not, do not. Simple as that.
