On Tue, May 6, 2025 at 12:20?PM Majure,Lucas C <lmajure at floridamuseum.ufl.edu<mailto:lmajure at floridamuseum.ufl.edu>> wrote:
Some of the plant groups we work on are not even old enough on the continent to lend themselves for this explanation, much less so in the Caribbean. Cacti are likely less than 40 million years old, for example, and they didn?t make it into the Antilles until much later during the Pliocene/Pleistocene. Desert formation on the continent, specifically in the southern Andes, where most clades originated, also are not that old. So data from continental populations goes hand in hand with the even younger ages that we see on the islands.

As with any fossil calibration, there are always assumptions regarding relationships of the fossils and then their placement (stem vs. crown placement for example) and ages based on those fossil calibrations, so we have to work with what we have when trying to reconstruct the past.

All this discussion about dispersal got me to take a closer look at Cyclura
- last time was over two decades ago. Interesting (to me) is that of the 11
species I found listed, only two are perhaps sympatric on Hispaniola.Others
are allopatric - barring any marginal overlap (but need to yet read over a
paper that may have other information). So, with all this imagined rafting,
species land up on different places, and yet not on the mainland of Central
and South America or North America or lesser Antilles. But makes sense in a
tectonic context of ancestral Cyclura range being fragmented and
reorganized with movement of the northern edge of the Caribbean plate.
Happy to send my draft map to anyone interested. But if anyone knows of a
comprehensive published source of distributions for all species that would
be of interest to read. My current map is mostly from listings of islands
occupied.
