This observation points out the reality of Pacific patterns. The
NG/Aust-C America distribution is relatively unambiguous with respect to
the main massings being immediately adjacent to the Pacific basin. In my
Galapagos study I found the saltwort genus Batis to also be in north
Queensland/Papua NG as well as Central America/Baja
California/Galapagos/Caribbean/Atlantic Brazil.


The central USA-SW Australia pattern is less unambiguous as it is not
obvious which ocean basin is involved. Further outgroup comparisons
might help. Also in my Galapagos study I found the genus Pitnus recorded
from the Caribbean/Central America/W Mexico/SW USA/Galapagos and
southern/SW Australia. 

Trans Pacific patterns are almost beyond counting, being so frequent and
taxonomically broad.

John Grehan

John,

We have a group of carabid beetles that includes a very well supported
clade with one lineage restricted to the area of roughly Myanmar to
Vietnam and the other in North America (southern Canada) south to
Central America (Panama). This strikes me as a peculiar distribution and

so the first step to understand its significance (if any) is to look for

similar patterns of phylogeny and distribution.

Sister groups, by definition, have some useful properties, e.g. have
been shown to share a most recent common ancestor and are lineages of
the same age. Therefore they provide us with comparable evolutionary
entities.

I am specifically looking for sister groups since taxa s.l. may or may
not have an associated phylogeny and so a family, for example, might (or

might not) just be a group of convenience. Even worse, it may be a
paraphyletic construct that excludes some descendents leaving only those

that happen to be in the areas of interest. The distributional pattern
based on a paraphyletic grade would be incomplete and therefore
misleading. Of course if the taxon has been shown to be monophyletic,
that is it contains all the decedents of a common ancestor, and its
extant members are only from North America, Central America and S.E.
Asia, even if the internal relationships have not been resolved, it is
of interest to me.

Considering other patterns of phylogeny and distribution that involve
these areas, more widely distributed taxa and extinct organisms may be
necessary to develop the full picture.

best,
Kip
