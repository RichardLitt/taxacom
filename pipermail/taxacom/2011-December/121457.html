These things are the ones you never will solve. You simply must know that
the species does not occur in the wild in NZ. You can never trust a
database. Online resources cannot substitute human brains. Look at this
example:

http://data.gbif.org/species/5725685/
same imformation here:
http://eol.org/pages/3035217/entries/20741775/maps

This species Viviparus viviparus does not occur in Austria, Italy and
Hungary, a true gap in the range of a widely distributed European species.
But all aggregators will inevitably over and over again collect sources
where the species is repeatedly reported for localities in these
countries, as for example in these maps, because in these countries the
species is repeatedly being confounded with the very similar species
Viviparus contectus, which is also widespread.
You must know that it has firmly been established that there is this gap.
You will never be able to extract this information reliably from this kind
of online resources.

Other examples here:
http://www.habitas.org.uk/molluscireland/species.asp?ID=193
"Ranges from Pyrenees and central Italy north to middle latitudes in
Scandinavia. "

This happens if website providers rely on information given by data
aggregators.

But this is something you will not be able to change, I guess. You can
complain, but I see no way to change it.

Francisco


> post-2000 new names are also problematic, due in part to vagueness in some
> crucial articles of the current Code of Zoo. Nom. (don't know about Bot.)
>
> in fact, almost nothing is completely straightforward, and everything is
> subject to revision
>
> local biotic inventories (e.g., NZOR) also pose big problems with their
> apparently simple present/absent classification (relative to the region
> covered, e.g. N.Z.) Many taxa have been reported from N.Z., for example,
> almost certainly erroneously, but although they may have been dropped from
> subsequent checklists, often there has been no direct challenge of the
> record published. Do we include these as present or absent? There are a
> continnum of cases from clearly present to clearly absent.
>
> so, the most important thing with all these projects is to treat
> everything as a *theory*, subject to confirmation or refutation, and it is
> therefore the *evidence* which is important, so just dogmatically stating
> a name/author/date, or an alleged presence in an area of a taxon, without
> the associated evidence being discussed, but rather based on "trust", is
> completely meaningless ...
>
> for example, NZOR has this record:
> http://demo.nzor.org.nz/names/413a92a1-656e-4859-80b0-409d7c1f692c
> telling us that Ligia exotica is present (indigenous, in fact!) in the
> "wild" in N.Z. In fact, it isn't here, and has never been here, but some
> old records got published somehow, based either on misidentifications or
> mislabellings. NZOR *does not tell us the basis (evidence) for this
> record*, which makes it strictly irrefutable ...

> Linking names to their original description in zoology must be done
> manually. The most important names were stablished before 1900. These were
> the most frequent animals. Sherborn and others have too many mistakes and
> are outdated. It would certainly be extremely useful to get Sherborn's
> names linked to the sources as cited by Sherborn, but this will not
> produce reliable data up to date with present-day nomenclature. The link
> to the digitised original source must be checked manually, entries must
> contain 'verified' flags and it must be possible to see in such a flag who
> exactly verified the original source and when.
>
> In AnimalBase we have tried to do that with 50,000 names established
> before 1800. Even if Sherborn is used the task is impossible without
> trained students with relatively profound knowledge of present-day
> nomenclatural rules. I agree with Jim that the task is doable and finite,
> but I learned a lot of how difficult the task is, and how expensive and
> how long it would take until it would be done. It is definitely not a
> 5-years or 10-years job of a voluntarily working community.
>
> If nobody is paid to do it, it will definitely take longer. It is not a
> comfortable job. 80-90 % of the names might be easy to find. But it is
> really weird in 10-15 % of the cases (and the last 5 % you need to find
> and verify might take you more than 50 % of the time you are paid to do
> the entire job).
>
> Sherborn tried to escape the danger of losing too much time with a
> strategy that I have called careless research. If works were too difficult
> and time-consuming for understanding and extracting the new names,
> Sherborn simply solved the problem by not doing it thoroughly. He seemed
> to have thought, well, others can do this later. He did not understand
> German, French, Danish, Swedish, Dutch and Italian, and did not take the
> time to translate descriptive texts, and by consequence came to wrong
> conclusions and produced many mistakes.
> This was a solution that we are not allowed to take into consideration.
> Someone has to do the entire job. We have to get along with all the
> difficult and chaotically arranged works published before 1900.
>
> If we don't get the links to the original sources of the first one million
> animal species, then all the rest will just be a drop on a hot stone, as
> we say in German.
>
> After 1920 we have the problem that most sources are in-copyright without
> free online access. Any estimations how many thousands of original species
> descriptions are locked for this reason?
>
> Two more problems with links to original sources.
>
> We identified a big problem in that many names of important taxa are
> currently attributed to sources in which the names were not correctly
> established. We found many original sources showing us that the name had
> actually been mentioned a nomen nudum, not made available under the Code.
> But the names are used and important. Providing links to the original
> sources will not only result in smiling faces. In many cases careless work
> by previous generations of experts in zoological disciplines will show up
> and come to daylight.
>
> Names first published in synonymy are a major problem. Synonyms have never
> been recorded in nomenclators, they just pop up suddenly in a modern paper
> or internet source like Fauna Europaea where you just see that the author
> and date of a well-known species is suddenly changed and you are asking
> yourself why. And then, after some really hard literature research you see
> that someone discovered that the name had previously been mentioned as a
> synonym, often without description or even referring to a different taxon,
> but regarded as having been made available under the unfortunate ICZN
> Article 11.6.1. These are the most difficult "original descriptions" to be
> found in zoology, because no previous nomenclator had ever recorded them.
> Nearly 2 % of the European mollusc species have these problems, and this
> proportion is steadily increasing. If a name was not established in 1758
> you can never be sure if it has not been mentioned as a synonym in a
> previous source.
>
> My experience, of course, refers only to names established before 1900.
