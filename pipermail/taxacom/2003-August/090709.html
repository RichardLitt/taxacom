One of the hardest things to teach students, especially non-majors, is that statements such as most of those cited below, in which it is implied that something evolved TO or FOR something else, are not consistent with evolutionary theory.  I am not surprised that the author of the article presented the material in this fashion.   I doubt (hope) that the persons being interviewed described things as presented by the writer.  But, if so, woe is us!

Dick

John Grehan wrote:

> Agreed - they can be viewed as hypotheses - although testing may be problematic. Evidently hair loss took place at some time so the hypothesis has an empirical foundation. Testing when it occured is one matter, why it occurred is another. It's the 'why' that gets problematic if the 'why' is presented in terms of purpose. As far as I am aware it is not possible to test 'purpose' in evolution other than by intentional activity and that's where it seems to be more metaphysics than science. I would view why theories as inherently untestable (open to correction on that).
>
> The criterion of 'advantage' is also untestable since it seems that it is self-defining. If something exists along with the existence of an organisms then it is inherently not a 'disadvantage'.
>
> Even though evolutionary theorists generally accept that there is more to evolutionary change than natural selection (even if they won't acknowledge orthogenetic processes such as molecular drive) - natural selection is all one generally hears about when positing the origin of a structure that has an assigned function. And yet it seems there is no way of empirically testing a natural selection claim for a past event (usually what one sees is a rhetorical argument).
>
> So a clear indication that statements are hypotheses does not necessarily eliminate the possibility that some hypotheses are inherently garbage to begin with (I doubt a lot of stuff circulating in evolution education would not stand up to cross examination in a court of law).
