Robert, it is problematic to say  on one hand he says " calibration only
indicates the most recent possible time but it could have happened earlier"
(which is factually correct as fossils only provide the minimum age of a
taxon to which that fossil is assigned), but on the other hand  assert that
" In a molecular clock, you do not get a simple number but you get a range
and the range depends on a number of factors.". Sure, everyone gives a
range, but this does not come from empirical data. Fossils do not give a
range age of upper to lower limits as a 'ballpark'. They only give
minimums. As far as I can see, such ranges, or upper limits, are pretty
much invented (e.g. by selecting priors - which even some molecular
theorists admit is more akin to representing an inventive mind than
anything else).

This transmogrification of minimums to maximums (with or without a 'range'
is (in addition to any molecular theory issues outlined by Stephen) is the
mortal achilles heel of fossil calibrated  molecular divergence as
currently practiced (I say fossil calibrated, as one can use tectonic
correlation as an alternative - as suggested by some molecular clock
theorists). It is so damaging that molecular clock practitioners continue
to ignore or dismiss the problem out of hand (i.e. without falsifying
evidence), and it is probably why the Te Papa comment was suppressed
(otherwise it makes them look bad as they have no falsification to offer).
Fossil calibrated molecular divergence is easy to do, and a big, big
industry, so it's not going to go away, irrespective of evidence. And to
the point, no one on Taxacome has ever identified any falsification for
fossil calibrated molecular divergences representing anything but minimum
ages.

Cheers, John

> yes, of course you have at least two levels of uncertainty: (1) the
> calibration only indicates the most recent possible time but it could have
> happened earlier. (2) there is a large variation in the node age estimates
> that actually take these issues into account. In a molecular clock, you do
> not get a simple number but you get a range and the range depends on a
> number of factors. But numbers such as (40-)30(-20) Mya for a node is a
> typical result. As a consequence, one should not use these numbers as
> precise measures but rather as "ballpark" figures.
>
>
>
> Your example of comparing two sister lineages (A, B) is a good example of
> the underlying issues. When you calculate the genetic distance, you assume
> it is symmetrical, i.e. both branches have exactly the same length.
> However, this is rarely the case. Once you add the next outgroup lineage,
> you often get asymmetric branch lengths, i.e. A has a different number of
> changes relative to the outgroup than B. This then automatically means that
> A evolved either faster or slower than B. In a molecular clock, the two
> branch lengths will be adjusted to exactly the same, as both have a single
> origin and are extant. As a result, the substitution rate for A and B will
> be different, reflecting exactly this phenomenon. When you only compare A
> and B pairwise, you do not see this. So a clock does work, and it does
> reflect these evolutionary dynamics, only that one has to be very careful
> to take the exact numbers for granted.

> Dear Stephen,
>
>
>
> the molecular clock does work, but obviously it has to be relaxed, because
> otherwise in a phylogram all terminals would end along the same vertical
> line, which is never the case. The "relaxation" is the tricky part, because
> it variously adjusts the branch lengths in a phylogram to obtain an
> ultrametric tree, trying to achieve the best balance given the data and the
> chosen model. Therefore, there is variation in the outcome which can be
> reduced by certain settings, although that does not mean it is more
> precise. Such a clock tree can be run without any calibration, e.g. setting
> the root to 1. The calibration of a particular node is then done to add a
> real time scale, i.e. it interpolates the inferred age for all other nodes.
> Calibrations should be done closer to the root and not to the tips, as
> especially towards the tips there might be a the problem of lack of
> resolution if the chosen markers have ambiguously aligned regions that need
> to be removed (happens easily in broader taxon sets), and calibrations
> close to the tip may give highly unreliable age estimates towards the root
> (a problem often seen in early clock studies).
>
>
>
> The estimation of a divergence time based on genetic difference between
> two lineages is in my view tricky and less accurate and reliable than a
> molecular clock, because one simplifies the issue. For instance, between
> two sequences, differences are assumed to represent direct substitutions
> and identities as conserved, but in a tree context, it may turn out that a
> simple difference is a more complex one (e.g. two substitutions in a row)
> and identities may sometimes be homoplasies, which then puts the divergence
> time further into the past. Regarding your example " If A and B are 5%
> divergent, you don't know if it was 1 year of 5% divergence, or 5 years of
> 1% divergence", you precisely have that problem in a pairwise comparison
> but it will be resolved in a well-done molecular clock using an independent
> calibration.
>
