Try many of the species of Embioptera.  Males are distinct,
but females are often identifiable to genus only.  The only
sure way to determine the species for the female is to find
her in association with a male.
We have similar problems in many spider species - males are
distinct, whereas a female, without the male association,
often is not.
Don't worry about rules - that's just the way it is.

Perhaps you need to go beyond the usual morphological
features and start some genetic work.
Robin

> It also depends on the animal group. I am sure you could describe hundreds
> of subspecies of dogs if you only look at the morphological characters.

> into populations favouring a particular host species' nest and laying eggs
> which match those of that species in colour and pattern. The colour
> pattern is inherited. But these female phyletic lineages are not called
> subspecies.
