I'm not sure how others tackle their massive specimen database backlog, but we approach it strategically. You can either start databasing in the left corner of the collection and continue until you finish in the right corner - in several decades time; or you can target "high value" collections.

Take for example our Trichoptera (Caddisfly) collection. For almost 40 years, the recognized taxonomic expert of this group in the SW Pacific region built this collection from 6 specimens to over 500,000. This collection generated much taxonomic research, several PhDs and because we funded excellent wet collection storage facilities, all researchers in our region deposited their material in this collection. It is a "high value" taxonomic and scientific collection.

Guess what? It was one of our first major targeted collection dataset projects. We used the "lot" system in which we added a single registration number to a tube of caddis specimens which were all collected at the same location and date and are all of the same species. This reduced the number of individual registrations from approx 500,000 down to approx 50,000. The registration task was completed within 12 months by "minimally trained" staff, all of whom were recent Uni graduated scientists basically out on their first "professional" job. It was an excellent introduction for them on museology, taxonomy, working with large biological datasets etc. A number of these staff went on to work with private biological survey companies and one even set up her own survey company.? We are now image capturing the numerous types stored in the collection.

This public dataset has now created links with our EPA (Environmental Protection Agency), numerous fly fishing clubs and is a wonderful example of long term research leading to a valuable, public biodiversity output.

The taxonomy, geospatial and temporal characteristics of the Australian Trichoptera fauna are now at the fingertips of web users.

There are some good news stories from the efforts of digitizing Science collections.

Cheers

> After considerable experience with all this, I've concluded that a
> different approach is required: I effectively consider all label data
> to be guilty (flawed in some manner) until proven innocent. In
> practice, one should not serve records to data users that have not
> been confirmed, and our practice reflects this; nothing is trusted
> until it has been scrutinized, and our database contains only the
> cleaned data. I've been chastised for this, but if we've gone through
> the trouble of correcting omissions and errors, there are two
> significant points to make: (1) there is little utility in showing
> the pre-corrected verbatim data; admittedly, it has a few potential
> uses, but since every specimen being databased has a GUID assigned,
> tracking which specimen is which is NOT one of those potential uses.
> (2) Funded databasing projects are ultimately under severe time/labor
> constraints. If the verbatim label data are not *absolutely
> necessary*, then the amount of time needed to enter them (even if
> it's just by taking photos) cannot be justified, as every minute
> spent is a minute taken away from *necessary* work. As such, I
> consider many such projects to have their priorities backwards; that
> is, it is evidently common to invest in verbatim data capture first,
> and then only worry about quality control and data cleanup and
> georeferencing if there is time and money left over to do it. This
> approach looks great on paper, since verbatim capture takes (assuming
> labels are legible and often-duplicated) about 1/10th the time
> required to do thorough georeferencing and data checking, and
> verbatim capture can be accomplished with cheaper and less-trained
> technicians. The alternative is to check every label, and instead of
> entering it verbatim, enter only the cleaned, corrected, and
> georeferenced data; if time and money are left over at the end of the
> project, THEN go back and enter the verbatim data (or take photos).
>
> In essence, it comes down to this: which is preferrable - spending X
> dollars and getting 200,000 verbatim records entered/photographed,
> but not knowing for certain whether *any* of the data are trustworthy
> (and many, if not most, NOT accurately georeferenced), versus
> spending X dollars and getting 20,000 records entered, every single
> one of which is confirmed clean, trustworthy, and accurately
> georeferenced?
>
> This isn't a hypothetical choice, nor purely fabricated figures. It's
> a real choice, and most people who have made this choice went for the
> first option. It seems that funding agencies prefer that approach,
> but I'm personally not happy with the trade-off, and not happy that
> people assume that data cleaning and georeferencing are trivia that
> can be dealt with AFTER we have everything digitized and online.
> Online data that are full of invisible errors awaiting correction are
> like a fruit basket in which one out of every ten fruit is toxic.
