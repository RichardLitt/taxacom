Mark, 
To paraphrase somewhat, you used the best data available. I can more or less accept that, but there is a limit below which the "best available data" is way too unreliable to even bother (for a hypothetical example, basing an estimate of the number of grains of sand on a large beach, based on a sample of one single grain, even if it is the biggest sample available!) Note that, for example, CoL still gives only 1 species of Agrilus (see http://www.catalogueoflife.org/annual-checklist/2011/browse/tree/id/2423797), when there are about 3000 (see http://species.wikimedia.org/wiki/Agrilus)! 
At any rate, all the details get overlooked when the results of your paper get reported in such articles as this one: 
http://www.science.auckland.ac.nz/uoa/home/about/news/news/template/news_item.jsp?cidB8101
Making such claims should surely rest on a more solid foundation than "we used the best data available", when such data could be truly so terrible as to give massively misleading conclusions. Furthermore, not even the abstract makes any real sense. Everything is unclear. Perhaps not so much the methods as such, but the way that the methods allegedly support the conclusions, or even what the conclusions really are, is totally incomprehensible (to me anyway). If the number of new taxa per author is decreasing, so what? This is consistent with more authors each describing fewer new taxa, but the total number of new taxa per year remaining constant or even increasing. Even if it remains constant, you *can't* get an estimate of total global biodiversity out of this data! How could you? The number of new taxa per year will likely remain constant for some unknown period of time, until the unknown number of remaining new species gets depleted. There are too many
 unknowns to solve the equation!
Stephen

Indeed, we filtered CoL data using a set of rules (details in paper appendix) and used only a fraction of it. thanks to Mark for the partial clarification ... but I am still scratching my head over some *important* points:
?
(1) I still doubt somewhat that Mark fully grasps the fact that, imperfect as it may be in some regards, WoRMS is actually an unprecedentedly comprehensive resource for marine biodiversity data, the likes of which simply does not exist for terrestrial data. This fact, which is of course in itself a good thing, does however mesh badly with the fact that CoL uses WoRMS for its marine data, but has to scratch around for bits and bobs of terrestrial data, in the absence of WoRTS!! This creates a massive data bias. The data is massive skewed, in terms of reliability, in favour of marine species, but most global biodiversity is terrestrial!
?
(2) the problem in the paper, as I see it,?is not with reporting the "findings" as such, it is with the mysterious connections between these findings and the overall conclusions, which seem to be to bear little relation to the "findings" upon which they are based. I cannot see any basis for claiming that novel terrestrial biodiversity is becoming harder to find...
?
(3) if the rate of description of nonmarines has been roughly constant ("similar") since the 1950's, then how do you get from this an estimate of overall global diversity???
?
(3) the major issue that concerns me is not so much with the paper itself, but with the way others are citing it. I have seen it cited twice recently, both times inappropriately in my opinion. The Wheeler et 30 als paper straightworwardly got is arse about face and quoted it as saying that we only know a quarter of what is really out there, when in fact it said that we already know 3 quarters! The Samyn and De Clerk paper, on the other hands,?cites it as saying that rates for terrestrial species have remained roughly constant since WW2, which the paper may have said, but it is so very?unclear how this statement relates to the overall conclusions and other statements in?Costello et al., that massive clarification is needed before?one can have any confidence at all that this?apparent finding bears any resemblance to reality, particularly given my comments above about CoL only having *extremely* scrappy terrestrial data.
?
clearly, it is not the fault of an author if their paper is cited inappropriately by others! Nevertheless, we should all perhaps take great care when?writing or citing papers with potential implications for global conservation issues ...
?

Dear Stephen and others,

Thank you for the discussion. 

In our paper, figure 2 (please email me for a pdf if anybody wants a copy) shows an increased number of marine species being discovered per year since the 1950's. [Note more recent data shows the apparent decline in the last decade was an artefact of the delay in getting new species into WoRMS; thus the trend of more marine species being discovered continues.] Evidently, this is a most productive time for marine species discovery. 

For terrestrial and freshwater species from CoL the trend is different and the rate of description has been similar since the 1950's. However, for both CoL and WoRMS data the number of people describing species has been increasing since the 1950's (Figure 2), and the number of species per author has been decreasing (Figure 3). I think our paper accurately reports the findings. I was just as surprised by these results as others are, and they certainly caused me to reflect for a long time before publication.

>The Samyn and De Clerk paper contains a very important statement that perhaps deserves highlighting: 
>
>another underestimated part of the problem is that most taxonomists sensu Joppa et al., that is ?those individuals who describe new species? can only devote a fraction of their time on the description of new species. apart from the multitude of tasks that refrains many from inventorying and describing biodiversity, taxonomists also face a hypothesis driven scientific system that is ruled by Impact Factors or other metrics. This reality seduces many taxonomists to publish an ecological, evolutionary or biogeographic story rather than descriptive taxonomic papers. 
>The real impediment to the description of new species is not necessarily the availability of people with the knowledge and skills to do it but the fact that it is widely accepted in biological journals that a species name can be used without citing the original description.? 
>
>A new species is an original idea in the same way that a new phylogenetic hypothesis might be - it is formed through the process: data, data analysis, results, discussion, conclusion.? The hypothesis is: this sample (group of samples) is distinguishable from other known samples at a level that fits a concept of what a species is.? Clumsy wording and I'm sure someone else can do better.? The reality is that new species descriptions allow new work in all fields of organismal biology and help improve the understanding of ecology, phylogeny etc.? 
>
>Many readers of this list edit journals.? I would appeal to those editors to ensure species descriptions are cited to properly acknowledge the science that led to the descriptions.? That way descriptive taxonomy will have the same standing as other branches of biology and citations, impact factors, H factors and the rest will all follow.? There is an opportunity to put basic taxonomy back at the top table in science but we need to support ourselves and realise we have some control over that future. 
>
>Action points: cite references to all species you use in a paper and insist as an editor or referee that such citations go in.? 
>
>We are in an under-cited discipline and the solution is at least partially in our own hands.
>
>Alastair
