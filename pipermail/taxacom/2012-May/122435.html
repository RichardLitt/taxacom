John Grehan wrote:

>Such databases
>that include distributional information based on specialist research can
>also be very useful in biogeographic studies (so again quality of the
>source in terms of specialist input is more the issue)
>
>Databases that allow one to see the original labels and specimen are, for
>my purposes, potentially most useful.

Unfortunately, original data labels are often extremely misleading,
especially regarding distributional information. After georeferencing
over 200,000 insect specimens from multiple institutions, the figures
work out to over 20% of all original labels have significant
omissions or ambiguities (the latter category being primarily things
like abbreviations, uncertain reference points, uncertain date
formats, or handwriting problems), and some 5% of all original labels
have genuine errors of one sort or another (mostly typographical
errors). I doubt such problems are unique to insect labels. A
taxonomic specialist may be able to fix problems with taxon IDs, but
that same person may be ill-equipped to fix problems with label data
(or, even if so, may not have the required time to do this). Any
digitized resources based on verbatim label data (without a quality
control step to confirm each and every record and correct mistakes)
will serve only to propagate such errors. Add to that the potential
for additional errors introduced by data entry personnel, or careless
georeferencing (i.e., use of automated georeferencing protocols,
which have a staggering failure rate), and the locality and phenology
data in most online resources is - simply put - not trustworthy.

It is not very hard to capture specimen data, but it is excruciating
and massively time-consuming to exercise any legitimate measure of
quality control, whether it be taxon IDs or label data. My impression
is that far more people are aware of (and take measures to address)
the taxon ID issue than the label data issue. No one would dispute
that a specimen record filed under the wrong species name is worse
than worthless, but not many people seem to be similarly concerned
about records that place a species in the wrong time or place - if
only because they are unaware how commonly the label data are bad.
That, I feel, is a grave oversight.

On May 15, 2012, at 1:17 AM, Roderic Page wrote:

A few quick comments.
>
>Silos
>
>"Silo" has a negative connotation, perhaps due to Al Gore's 1998 speech 
>"The Digital Earth: Understanding our planet in the 21st Century" in which 
>he noted that the vast majority of Landsat images hadn't ever been seen:
>
>"Instead, they are stored in electronic silos of data. We used to have an 
>agricultural policy where we stored grain in Midwestern silos and let it 
>rot while millions of people starved to death. Now we have an insatiable 
>hunger for knowledge. Yet a great deal of data remains unused."
>
>Silo in this context is a set of data that is, either by accident or 
>design, difficult to access, share and reuse. Obviously, one can use "silo" 
>in any way you wish, but if you say "silos are great" don't be surprised if 
>most people will assume you are arguing for restricting access to your 
>data.
>
>Wikipedia
>
>I've also had rather mixed experiences with Wikipedia. I suspect it's the 
>nature of the beast. Anyone who reads Taxacom will see parallels between 
>threads on this mailing lists and some of the discussions on Wikipedia. For 
>some other thoughts on wikis and Wikipedia seen Jonathan Eisen's recent 
>post 
>http://phylogenomics.blogspot.co.uk/2011/12/pluses-and-minuses-if-wikipediafying.html 
>and the open access article "Making your database available through 
>Wikipedia: the pros and cons" http://dx.doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkr1195
>
>Evidence
>
>For me the take home message about the WoRMS/Wikipedia issue is not the 
>personal foibles of Wikipedia contributors but the lack of access to the 
>underlying primary literature. Most taxonomic databases are little more 
>than digitised 5x3 index cards with no links to the literature (by which I 
>mean digital links that take you to the actual publication). If you only 
>have naked lists of names then you end up in a slanging match over which 
>list is "authoritative". We all know that taxonomic databases have errors, 
>these could be exposed and fixed much more quickly if we linked them to the 
>underlying evidence.
>
>Databases
>
>The notion that people make databases in order to get funding has it back 
>to front. My sense is that most of the money available for biodiversity 
>informatics is for infrastructure rather than content, hence the emphasis 
>on developing networks, databases and tools.
>
>Certainly, I've not had any funding for projects like BioStor 
>http://biostor.org or iPhylo Linkout http://iphylo.org/linkout. I build 
>these because I want to be able to access taxonomic information and link 
>that information together. In terms of the bigger picture, if information 
>isn't online then effectively it doesn't exist. If we want taxonomic 
>information to be relevant then it needs to be online, so the issue becomes 
>how to do that most effectively.
>

I can confirm that mollusk labels have similar issues with similarly
high frequency, though I have not tallied percentages. In fact,
insects may have better than average quality of average data because
fewer specimens survive from the "I picked these up somewhere when my
ship sailed around the world" era.  Having a misidentified specimen
with good locality data is far more useful than a correctly identified
specimen with bad locality data, as the identification can be fixed
from the specimen.  Someone knowledgeable about the taxon or locality
may be able to recognize that a record is supect, even without
examining the specimen.  But all data in a database needs competent
checking to be reliable.  And many larger collections will include
items from various sources, of varying reliability for their
localities and identifications.

The problem is compounded when online databases grab information from
varied sources without careful verification or good harmonization
protocols.  In particular, homonyms can lead to perfectly good
information from one database being presented wrongly in another one,
which claims that corrections should be referred back to the first
database.

label data is a huge can of worms for all sorts of reasons! After having 
thought the issue through at some length, I have come to the probably rather 
unpopular conclusion that it is a bad idea to think that the totality of 
label data for any given taxon is a useful thing to have! It is somewhat 
analogous to the example of using WoRMS as a source for that squat lobster 
article on WP ... given the way the WoRMS page was at the time, it was best 
to just not mention it at all! There is a certain irony that it is common 
nowadays for scientists to spend money going on collecting tours to get 
fresh material of taxa for use in DNA analysis. Actually, it would be better 
to go on a collecting tour to gather reliable distributional information for 
the taxon! Any distribution map based on the uncritical acceptance of label 
data is likely to be problematic, unles the map is so coarse that points 
cover a significant proportion of the planet! Even worse is displaying a
distribution map of a tiny subsample of available label data! If you want to 
know the geographical distribution of a taxon, you gotta go out and measure 
it ... carefully!

I support access to specimen data.  But compilations with no access to
the underlying specimen data are not especially reliable, and not all
online databases have easy ways to submit corrections.  (Of course,
making it easy for anyone to change what it says is not a good idea,
either-the corrections should be carefully checked, too.)   Making
specimen data available is one way to allow people to check it; the
problem is uncritical acceptance of poor data and lack of support for
making it better.

Although we may ask for lots of funding, in my experience we don't get it.

Given the number of highly trained individuals who leave taxonomy
because they can't get employment, I suspect that there may be more
people interested in data validation than expected.  It's not
necessary to devote a whole career to data validation in order to help
substantially.   If reviewing data were regarded as a reasonable part
of a job, worthy of funding, and if the databases readily accepted
input suggestions, then one could contribute a lot simply in the
course of regular taxonomic work.  But if there's no support for
regular taxonomic work, and if the databases are difficult to correct,
poorly documented, etc., and if contributing to the databases is
assumed to be something that taxonomists can do for free in their
spare time, then there's little hope for improvement.

Even if the highly trained individuals don't want to do the data
validation, no one else can.  I can figure out the vast majority of
mollusk labels I see because I have a good idea of what genus
something may have been assigned to, because I've seen enough
Latinized binomials to have a good guess at likely possibilities,
because I've got an idea of what the possible localities are and know
a fair number of historic and current geographic names, and because
I've seen a lot of bad handwriting from the past 200 years.

and even online databases.  Some of those are easier to learn than
others, but it takes time and interest.

> > Unfortunately, original data labels are often extremely misleading,
> > especially regarding distributional information. After georeferencing
> > over 200,000 insect specimens from multiple institutions, the figures
> > work out to over 20% of all original labels have significant
> > omissions or ambiguities (the latter category being primarily things
> > like abbreviations, uncertain reference points, uncertain date
> > formats, or handwriting problems), and some 5% of all original labels
> > have genuine errors of one sort or another (mostly typographical
> > errors). I doubt such problems are unique to insect labels.
>
> I can confirm that mollusk labels have similar issues with similarly
> high frequency, though I have not tallied percentages. In fact,
> insects may have better than average quality of average data because
> fewer specimens survive from the "I picked these up somewhere when my
> ship sailed around the world" era.  Having a misidentified specimen
> with good locality data is far more useful than a correctly identified
> specimen with bad locality data, as the identification can be fixed
> from the specimen.  Someone knowledgeable about the taxon or locality
> may be able to recognize that a record is supect, even without
> examining the specimen.  But all data in a database needs competent
> checking to be reliable.  And many larger collections will include
> items from various sources, of varying reliability for their
> localities and identifications.
>
> The problem is compounded when online databases grab information from
> varied sources without careful verification or good harmonization
> protocols.  In particular, homonyms can lead to perfectly good
> information from one database being presented wrongly in another one,
> which claims that corrections should be referred back to the first
> database.
