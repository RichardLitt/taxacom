In zoological nomenclature, author citation and year of publication do not form part of the name of a taxon and their citation is optional, although customary.

The zoo-Code has this Recommendation 51C. Citation of multiple authors. When three or more joint authors have been responsible for a name, then the citation of the name of the authors may be expressed by use of the term "et al." following the name of the first author [...]. Note that this is not a requirement. The choice of "[first author] et al." is based on the fact that this is the predominant practice in the taxonomic literature.

The zoo-Code uses "&" in all Examples and Recommendations where situations with only two authors of a scientific name occur.

An AI response to a query I just made after reading this exchange provided an interesting answer, but I am not suggesting that we should blindly follow what a machine suggests!

- I copy and paste verbatim:

Botanical Nomenclature: Governed by the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants (ICN).
Zoological Nomenclature: Governed by the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN).

Both codes emphasize stability and clarity, and et al. remains the accepted abbreviation when citing multiple authors. The ICZN, for instance, does not allow changes like replacing et al. with & al. in formal naming.
Unfortunately, there are no published quantitative statistics comparing the frequency of "et al." vs "& al." or other variants in botanical or zoological literature. However, here are some qualitative observations:

  *   Academic Journals and Databases (e.g., ZooBank, IPNI, Tropicos) overwhelmingly use et al. in author citations.
  *   "& al." is more likely to appear in:
     *   Informal writing or older texts
     *   Non-standardized databases or OCR-scanned documents
  *   Trend Toward Simplification: In digital platforms and casual contexts, there's a mild tendency to drop Latin formalism, but this hasn't penetrated formal nomenclature.

My feeling (which I believe is correct) is that botanical tradition is to use a bit more Latin than zoological tradition (these days). So a botanical work is more likely to say (e.g.) Smith et Jones, 1905, while a zoological work would use Smith & Jones, 1905. For my compendium, which spans both botanical and zoological names (the latter outnumber the former by around 4:1), I standardise everything to a single format, using "&" and not "et"
to link names together ("et al." being an exception).
