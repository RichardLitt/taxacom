Stephen, There is nothing in science to support the contention that " If
the oldest and most primitive member of a group is discovered as a fossil
in the S Hemisphere, then that evidence suggests a S Hemisphere origin for
the group." There is just no basis for that. You are incorrect to say that "
 such an origin is suggested by the available evidence." It is not. The
location of the oldest and most primitive fossil member of a group is only
evidence of the oldest (known) and most primitive (known) fossil member
being at a particular location at a particular geological date.

John Grehan

> any certainty of a S Hemisphere origin for the group, merely that such an
> origin is suggested by the available evidence. I see no particular problem
> with this, except perhaps the overly heavy weight that science puts on
> working with mere inconclusive suggestions from empirical evidence. For
> applied science, at the end of the day, somethinbg either works or it
> doesn't, but, for pure science, the inconclusive suggestions just lead to
> inconclusive theories with which scientists occupy their time and
> resources, but for what???

> Stephen - the assertion that a southern hemisphere origin "is suggested by
> the new evidence" is indeed nonsense. The fossil can only suggest that a
> clade was present at a particular place and time. Empirically it says
> nothing about the origin of the taxon with respect to younger fossils
> elsewhere. Its quite possible that the group had its origin from a
> widespread ancestor that was in both the northern and southern hemisphere
> and the (current) oldest fossil happens to be located in the south. It does
> not matter that the fossil may represent the basal lineage as this may just
> represent an initial vicariance. So dreaming up a local (southern) origin
> is not science at all, just nonsense.
