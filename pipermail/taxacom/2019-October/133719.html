"Seems to me that Rob's argument is essentially that the dates aren't
EVEN minimums, but may also be overestimates due to various kinds of
error. If so, then what use are they at all?"  They provide a probable
temporal window for events based on a few assumptions, (some better
supported than others) and the available data at hand. They are still
useful if nothing else if as a way to anchor the discussion along a
more limited range of dates.

"However, if Rob were inclined to use the Principle of Charity more in
debates he might attribute to Michael the more sensible view that the
dates CAN only be minimums, EVEN WHEN free of the kinds of error which
in practice may result in overestimates."

I am not sure what you mean by this Stephen. The resident
panbiogeographer´s position, made time and time again can be (overly)
simplified as follows: molecular rates are flexible; models based on
these rates are fallible; fossils can only be used to estimate a
minimum. Ergo, any distribution can be explained solely by vicariance
(by the judicious stretching and compressing of molecular rates to
suit this view). And since the molecular based rates are flexible (and
did I mention fallible) they can´t falsify my hypothesis. In fact,
neither can fossils because lack of fossils (even in very well
researched localities) can be explained away by the difficulty of
fossilization, etc. All perfectly "logical" but a clear rejection of
the abductive process on which science is also (mostly?) based. This
perspective would be fine if it weren´t accompanied by the constant
trickle of little jabs to get some sort of discussion going.

The accusation of sophistry is not wide off the mark either when you
consider other prior arguments (i.e. "dispersal"). If you can´t recall
previous exchanges on Taxacom let´s just say that the tag applies as
well.

My take on Rob´s post is that constant baiting is irritating and that
some circumspection on their part is advisable. If they have fresh
epistemological ideas that are clearly fleshed out then great, it is
always good to spar as better understanding ensues. But if the whole
exercise becomes yet another tit-for-tat mud slinging exercise based
on the same sacred texts, then it is pointless and default reaction is
to shrug shoulders and move on (although sometimes you make the
mistake of taiking the bait and you throw a salvo their way to detox).

Jason

> Stephen
>     On Monday, 21 October 2019, 12:15:03 am UTC, Rob Smissen via Taxacom <taxacom at mailman.nhm.ku.edu> wrote:
>
>  Once again Michael Heads promotes his self-serving slogan, claiming "As discussed, fossil-calibrated, molecular clock dates for clades can only ever be minimums."
> Current Bayesian molecular dating software gives a probability distribution of dates. How can a distribution of dates (a range) be a minimum? Well, Heads takes the oldest date from a published range and transmogrifies that into minimum.
>
> But that is only the beginning. The "only ever minimum" argument ignores the fact that other sources of error can contribute to misleadingly old date estimates. These include
>
>   1.  Ancestral polymorphism (splits in gene lineages are older than splits among populations). This has the opposite effect to the missing fossil problem. Gene lineage divergences necessarily predate population divergences, and therefore one might (wrongly) infer using Heads' logic that molecular date estimate are necessarily maximum.
>   2.  Rate variation among lineages.
>   3.  Miss-specified models.
>   4.  Incorrect attribution of fossils to extant lineage.
>   5.  Imprecise ages of fossils (often themselves given as ranges).
>   6.  Stochastic effects when sequences are short.
>
> Sometimes the data underpinning molecular dating papers is consistent with a 0 age (by which I mean that the samples could as well have been taken from a single panmictic population), yet Heads transmogrifies an age of millions of years into a so called minimum. I don't believe intelligent people would support that, at least not if they were honest.
>
> We should all take molecular clock dates with a big grain of salt, but slogans don't make for good science. I don't mind an argument that molecular date estimates may be underestimates, but I do mind the slogan that they are necessarily minimum. That is sophistry.
>
> Using the current generation of software, it is possible to explore the effect of using different priors. In fact it is quite easy. In particular, it is easy to explore the effect of increasing a calibration age to make dates older. It is also easy to constrain or relax the allowed rate variation among lineages.  If Heads was serious he could undertake these analyses. He could make the calibrations as old as he likes. Or stipulate that conspecific allopatric populations diverged in the Mesozoic as he oft claims. Then he could quantify how much rate variation, and how old the earth has to be, to accommodate his "biogeographic" assumptions. Despite claiming an understanding of molecular data and the workings of Bayesian analyses, and the fact that the software is free, Heads seems remarkably slow to provide analyses of how much older than their oldest fossils clades needs to be to fit his paradigm.
