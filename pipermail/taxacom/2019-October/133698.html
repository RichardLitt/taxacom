> >> >  John,I'd like to know what other Taxacomers think about this, as it
> is crucial to the proper understanding of science itself. You seem to be
> trying to undermine scientific inferences on the basis that there are other
> possible explanations of the same facts. That might be a sensible approach
> to certain kinds of cases, but not ones of this kind, I suggest. You seem
> to be conflating certainty with empirical support (for a hypothesis).
> Science isn't about determining the truth, it is about being led by
> evidence. If the oldest and most primitive member of a group is discovered
> as a fossil in the S Hemisphere, then that evidence suggests a S Hemisphere
> origin for the group. Sure, there are other, more convoluted possibilities
> which could be wheeled in ad hoc, but that's not science! The authors are
> not claiming any certainty of a S Hemisphere origin for the group, merely
> that such an origin is suggested by the available evidence. I see no
> particular problem with this, except perhaps the overly heavy weight that
> science puts on working with mere inconclusive suggestions from empirical
> evidence. For applied science, at the end of the day, somethinbg either
> works or it doesn't, but, for pure science, the inconclusive suggestions
> just lead to inconclusive theories with which scientists occupy their time
> and resources, but for what???Stephen

> >> >  Stephen - the assertion that a southern hemisphere origin "is

> Empirically it says nothing about the origin of the taxon with respect to
> younger fossils elsewhere. Its quite possible that the group had its origin

> hemisphere and the (current) oldest fossil happens to be located in the
> south. It does not matter that the fossil may represent the basal lineage
> as this may just represent an initial vicariance. So dreaming up a local
> (southern) origin is not science at all, just nonsense.
