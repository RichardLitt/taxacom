Hi Michael,       I wasn't discussing the division of Crossosomatales into two main clades (southern and northern).  That might have been by vicariance or maybe not.  What I was discussing, and which you did not address in your comments, was the subsequent evolution and biogeography of the southern clade.  That is a more complicated issue which simple vicariance cannot readily explain---unless of course, one once again uses the fossil calibration excuse to push back the time scale tens of millions of years earlier.  Funny how that excuse comes up so often in these discussions.  
       In any case, the maximal ages of eudicot orders are fairly well constrained because the pollen record is so good (far better than the macrofossil record).  You would be hard pressed to find any botanist who believes Crossosomatales as a whole is older than 110 million years (and 125 million years is the accepted age of the entire eudicot clade).  Dispersal to Madagascar (and from there to the Seychelles and Mascarene Islands) looks like a fairly safe bet. What's so complicated about that?  
     And I certainly wasn't arguing in favor of "everything was everywhere".  However, Antarctica formerly played an important role in so many taxa as they dispersed through or from that continent  As more fossils are discovered in Antarctica, it will no doubt show that evolution is often more complicated than we once thought, and molecular evidence already has (ratite phylogeny certainly seems to be).  Invoking too much "simple" vicariance seems overly simplistic to me.

Hi Ken, The Indian Ocean clade of Crossosomatales (Madagascar - New Zealand and vicinity) has its sister group neatly allopatric to its north (New Guinea, Sri Lanka etc. northwards), so the simplest explanation is that they both originated by vicariance. The break between the two (located between Madagascar and Sri Lanka, between New Caledonia and New Guinea, etc.) coincides with belts of major rifting as well as breaks in countless other groups. 
 The 80 m.y. date for the clades is fossil calibrated and so it is a mimimum age. This is useful as it rules out earlier events (not older ones) as possible causes.   For the ratite clade, you seem to be supporting the idea that everything was everywhere and then gradually the distributions formed by extinction. So how and why were they everywhere to start with? It just gets more and more complicated for a very simple case of allopatry.

Hi Mike,                               
        The Crossosomatales southern clade is probably only about 80 million years old, so dispersal to Madagascar seems to be a good bet (and I believe it also dispersed to the Seychelles and Mascarene islands as well).  This clade probably existed in Antarctica and Australia (fossil pollen records), so the current distribution is no doubt a combination of early vicariance, later dispersals, and then extinction in intervening areas (including Antarctica and Australia).  I don't know much about the beetle family Chaetosomatidae, but perhaps Stephen Thorpe might have an opinion on that.   

         In any case, since only that beetle family has the same distribution as the elephant bird-kiwi clade, I would certainly not call that much of a pattern!!!  And the elephant bird-kiwi clade is probably even younger than 80 million years old, so dispersal of a flighted ancestor (of kiwi and elephant birds) from Antarctica in the early Cenozoic seems a very probable hypothesis, and again followed by extinction of the clade in Australia (due to competition with other ratites) as well as extinction in mainland Africa (due to competition with early ostriches).  

        So if there is a pattern to be seen in such cases, I would suggest that Antarctica is going to be a major factor, and that the massive extinction of the biota in Antarctica during the early-to-mid Cenozoic (and paucity of a known fossil record there) makes vicariance more appealing than it actually is for many taxa.  In particular, the multiple losses of flight among ratite taxa makes perfect sense to me.  Why John calls such suggestions ignorant (much less stupid) baffles me.

Hi Ken, On p. 74 of my 2014 book I cited the beetle family Chaetosomatidae, known only from Madagascar (Malgassochaetus) and New Zealand (Chaetosoma, Chaetosomodes). A similar pattern in plants occurs in the southern hemisphere clade of Crossosomatales; it's in Madagascar (also the adjacent coast of Africa and Cape Town) (Aphloia, Geissoloma) , New Zealand (Ixerba) and New Caledonia (Strasburgeria). (Both examples are cited, with references, in my 2014 book, p. 74).
