Dear John and Michael,

I was not aware that there was an implicit time limit. I travel a lot
and drop in and out of Taxacom as time allows so if you require
immediate answers please keep this in mind. Also, to give Michael the
benefit of the doubt, I decided to reread his 2011 paper and at least
a few of the papers contained within to refresh my mind. The essence
of Michael?s paper boils down to this statement: ?But if a single
tectonic event related to a node that is distant in phylogeny and
space can be used to date the overall phylogeny of a group, presumably
separate tectonic events can be used to date many individual nodes
within the group itself. This approach involves a de- tailed,
systematic engagement of biology with tectonics at many levels, ...
this approach combines the best of molecular biology (the clades and
their distributions) with hard-rock geology (rather than stratigraphy
and fossils) and avoids the many problems of fossil calibration. ?
;geographic distribution has turned out to be the ?character of
characters? for molecular phylogeny in general.?

Tectonic events are subject to the same uncertainties as any other
temporal data source. You are not breaking a biscuit. Some areas will
separate first and partial connections (islands, etc) may act as a
filter. Can you account for partial filtering as the barrier emerges
or  due to disruptions along the barrier (river valleys in mountain
ranges, land bridges or connecting islands between separating
continents? Your idea sounds like it involves collapsing multiple
splitting dates into one ?educated guess?. The patterns may look the
same but not the dates.

On a more practical note, phylogenies calibrated with vicariance are
useless for testing relative times due to inherent circularity. You
will still need ?outside? data (fossils, independentl calibrated
genetic distances) to corroborate your dating.

Rocks on their own do not have inherent solidity. Even you exploit
this idea when you say that islands can be older than the sampled
surface material implies. Having said that, I could invoke your trump
card of (selective) variable divergence rates and simply say that they
coincide perfectly. I just need to make the rate that much faster and
presto.

Regarding Ho & Phillips (2009), your citation is not quite in the
spirit of the article. I had to check as it has been a while and as I
thought, their opinion of exponential priors is that ?..., it can also
be used when there is strong expectation that the oldest fossil lies
very close to the divergence event being represented by the node,
relative to a distant, soft maximum. ? Otherwise they recommend a
lognormal as this can take into account the ambiguity of temporally
placing a fossil along the stem group. So if you disagree on an
author?s taxonomic guess and have reasons other than pattern fitting,
then go ahead and criticize. For me the major take-home message from
Ho & Phillips was to use whenever possible multiple calibration points
and increased matrix size. That is, lots of data to reduce the
uncertainty caused by poor prior choice. This is the point that
Richard mentioned and I agree with it. Reducing the choice of data to
the ?character of characters? goes against their very sound advice.

Finally, John, keeps drumming on the ?dispersal from the centers of
origin?. I guess you are fixated on Hennig?s interpretation, and since
I am neither partial to his definition nor I care, I stand to one side
on that one.

Best

Jason

>> All the Bayesian dating studies I've seen use very tight priors (see

>> only be a little older than the oldest fossil of the group (e.g. 2 million years).
>> This generates 'evidence' that the group is young, and so vicariance
>> is ruled out and dispersal is 'revealed'. Many studies use exponential
>> priors (which stipulate that the probability of earlier clade ages
>> decreases very rapidly), and cite Ho as support for this (even though
>> Ho specifically warned against this). Many studies stipulate that the

>> again, there is no logical basis for this, but it generates the desired results.
