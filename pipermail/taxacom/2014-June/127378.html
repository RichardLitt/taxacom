John,


Statements such as ?This is the philosophy that if something is
correct it will be supported by the majority. History shows that this
is not always the case.? and ?Not so imaginary. [other side] indicate
that you feel like you are battling something or someone. Further, you
label this opponent ?dispersalism? (a label that suggests that they
somehow reject vicariance) and paint them with an interesting choice
of adjectives (mystery, allure, etc). If your arguments are solid the
adjectives are superfluous. In any case, when I say that the majority
of biologists would agree that vicariance is likely to be the most
common, why are they wrong? Because they are not saying that
vicariance is the only mechanism?


Anyway, you contend that ?... the point here is that the
misrepresentation of minimal ages as actual or maximal has led to
erroneous results.? I am sure that you are aware that phylogeny
calibration allows for priors to be used and that this is common
practice to use log priors to accommodate not just for the uncertainty
in the stratigraphy of fossils but also for origination uncertainty.
So is your complaint against someone in particular who used a very
tight prior or about calibrations in general?

Finally, reliance solely on patterns is methodologically flawed
without a timing reference point (the one you ditched). You could, for
example, argue that the prevailing winds, currents or disperser
migrations aided the movement of some taxa resulting in a pattern that
is accidentally congruent with vicariance. Without the time frame it
would all look the same.

There are uncertainties inherent in the use of fossils or any other
calibration reference but it seems preferable (and more logical) than
suggesting that a commonly observed phenomenon (organisms moving
about) has absolutely no effect on biogeographical patterns at the
macro scale.

Best

Jason
