What I have found over the years that there are dispersalist who allow for
vicariance but only under a dispersalist paradigm that involves dispersal
from centers of origin to explain differentiation.



?and paint them with an interesting choice of adjectives (mystery, allure,
etc).?



The adjectives are those that are used by various dispersalists to refer to
origins being ?mysterious? etc. True, not all dispersalists have used such
terms, but it is interesting that such terms turn up quite frequently, even
in modern literature. In such cases there are non mysterious vicariance
possibilities that such authors do not address.





? If your arguments are solid the adjectives are superfluous. In any case,
when I say that the majority of biologists would agree that vicariance is
likely to be the most common, why are they wrong? Because they are not
saying that vicariance is the only mechanism??

 Interesting that you say that the majority of biologists would agree that
vicariance is likely to be the most common [mechanism]. But in biogeography
that does not appear to be the case.



There are certainly a number of biogeographers who have said that there is
both vicariance and dispersal. But the issue is not whether there are both
or not, but how they work in relation to each other. In dispersalism
differentiation is usually attributed to dispersal from centers of origin.
In vicariance the origin of a vicariant taxon is attributed to local
differentiation of a widespread ancestor.

?Anyway, you contend that ?... the point here is that the misrepresentation
of minimal ages as actual or maximal has led to erroneous results.? I am
sure that you are aware that phylogeny calibration allows for priors to be
used and that this is common practice to use log priors to accommodate not
just for the uncertainty in the stratigraphy of fossils but also for
origination uncertainty. So is your complaint against someone in particular
who used a very tight prior or about calibrations in general??



? Finally, reliance solely on patterns is methodologically flawed without a
timing reference point (the one you ditched). You could, for example, argue
that the prevailing winds, currents or disperser migrations aided the
movement of some taxa resulting in a pattern that is accidentally congruent
with vicariance. Without the time frame it would all look the same.?



True, if it were. But panbiogeography looks to geomorphic correlation to
provide spatial hypotheses on temporal origins, and it does also use
fossils to provide MINIMAL estimates. I would be interested to hear your
critique of that approach for Heads? Australasia book.

?There are uncertainties inherent in the use of fossils or any other
calibration
reference but it seems preferable (and more logical) than suggesting that a
commonly observed phenomenon (organisms moving about) has absolutely no
effect on biogeographical patterns at the macro scale.?



As above, panbiogeography does use fossil calibrations.
