Hi Jason,

You wrote: ''I believe you are confounding dating the object with dating
the biological significance
of the event'.

No, they are obviously different, and I'm arguing that the age of the
object (fossil) is very different
from the age of its clade. Fossils are often very accurately dated; the
problem is the clade age.

You wrote: 'rifting continents and rising mountains have a beginning and an
end'. No-one is
arguing with that. The point is that the events (their beginning and end)
are often very accurately
dated with radiometric techniques. Also, the delimitation of the clades and
their geographic
distributions are now often very well supported in molecular studies. (In
contrast, the fossil-based
clade dates are very problematic)  Why not use these results to date a
large number of clades
and see what happens? Does it lead to a coherent synthesis or does it go
nowhere, with contradictory
conclusions? Instead of testing the approach, the orthodox line is to
compare it with creationism
and call for it to be banned (Waters et al., 2013 Syst Biol 62: 494). Would
you support testing
or banning?

You wrote: 'As Richard rightly pointed out, so many taxa and so few
patterns'. No. I'm the one
supporting few patterns. (Richard said: 'By using the word "generality",
are you
implying that *most* (nearly all?) species of life on earth are members of
these groups with
repeated patterns of distribution?  If so, then one would surmise that
there are a finite set of
patterns, and the vast majority of species fall into one of these group
patterns.  That's certainly
not what I see on coral reefs').

I asked the question, just how much older is a clade than its oldest
fossil? You wrote: 'Asking
me to give you a hard rule of thumb for fossils is pointless because it
would depend on each
case'.  OK, how about an estimate for any group? You seem to disagree with
the view that
there is a fundamental problem here (termed 'the problem of the priors' by
recent authors) - is that
right?

You wrote: 'Timing is the key and the circularity of your method makes it
impossible to
determine without resorting to circularity'.

How is the method circular? The use of vicariance events to provide dates
is based on a
simple deduction: if the phylogenetic break was caused by a spatially
coincident tectonic feature,
and this is dated at *x* million years old, then the phylogenetic break is
also *x* million years old;
there is no circular reasoning here, whether or not particular cases of
tectonic-vicariance
calibration are incorrect. If a model of vicariance caused by tectonics is
adopted, deductions
about the age of a break can be tested against each other and other events.
This follows a
normal process of investigation based on the proposal of hypotheses,
testing of predictions
deduced from the hypotheses, and the framing of new hypotheses.
