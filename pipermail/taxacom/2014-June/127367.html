Hi Ken,

You say: ' I wasn't discussing the division of Crossosomatales into two
main clades (southern and northern). That might have been by vicariance or
maybe not. What I was discussing, and which you did not address in your
comments, was the subsequent evolution and biogeography of the southern
clade'.

You've missed my point. I was suggesting that the distribution of the
southern clade was attained by vicariance with the northern clade. There is
no need for it to disperse from one side of the Indian Ocean to the other
if it already existed on both sides (and on the Seychelles etc.) at the
time of its origin.

'That is a more complicated issue which simple vicariance cannot readily
explain---unless of course, one once again uses the fossil calibration
excuse to push back the time scale tens of millions of years earlier. Funny
how that excuse comes up so often in these discussions'.

That's because it's a fundamental flaw that comes up so often in the
standard argument. The fossil record provides minimum ages. That's not an
excuse, it's simple logic.

'In any case, the maximal ages of eudicot orders are fairly well
constrained because the pollen record is so good (far better than the
macrofossil record)'.

Just because it is better than the macrofossil record doesn't mean it is
'good' in an absolute sense, i.e. that the clade ages based on it are
maximum ages. There are many problems interpreting the fossil pollen record.

'You would be hard pressed to find any botanist who believes
Crossosomatales as a whole is older than 110 million years (and 125 million
years is the accepted age of the entire eudicot clade)'.

Whether or not ideas are accepted or popular is interesting for sociology,
but is not relevant to logical, scientific argument. The centre of
origin/dispersal view is the dominant paradigm, and has been for thousands
of years, but that does not mean it's right. In any case, the eudicot age
is problematic (there is a whole section on it in my 2014 book (p. 67).
Smith et al. (PNAS 2010) wrote that:


Using the first appearance of tricolpate pollen as a fixed calibration may
underestimate the origin

of eudicots and, by extension, other age estimates that have relied on this
constraint. Tricolpate

grains first appear in separated geographical areas and the grains
themselves are not uniform in

morphology . . . , both observations implying that the tricolpate clade
originated some time before

its appearance in the fossil record . . . .


'Dispersal to Madagascar (and from there to the Seychelles and Mascarene
Islands) looks like a fairly safe bet. What's so complicated about that?'

It's not complicated at all, a child could understand it. But if you accept
that, how are you going to explain the very simple global
allopatry (northern and southern clades)?

'And I certainly wasn't arguing in favor of "everything was everywhere".'

You suggested that the elephant bird/kiwi clade was formerly also
on mainland Africa and Australia, although there is no evidence for
that and other ratites occur there. Why bring in ad hoc hypotheses?

'However, Antarctica formerly played an important role in so many taxa as
they dispersed through or from that continent'.

If you have a northern  hemisphere group with a southern
hemisphere/Antarctica sister, both groups can have evolved by simple
vicariance and there is no need for any dispersal *within the groups*,
across Antarctica or anywhere else. There may have been dispersal in the
ancestor.

'As more fossils are discovered in Antarctica, it will no doubt show that
evolution is often more complicated than we once thought, and molecular
evidence already has (ratite phylogeny certainly seems to be). Invoking too
much "simple" vicariance seems overly simplistic to me'.

Why not use a simple process for a very simple pattern? The nine ratite
families (including the fossil lithornithids in the north) are almost
entirely allopatric (rheas overlapping with tinamous in South America and
moas overlapping with kiwis in New Zealand are the only exceptions, these
can be explained by local dispersal *within *those regions). Chance
processes do not really explain this very striking global pattern, simple
vicariance does.

It is very likely that fossil ratites will eventually be found in
Antarctica, but a fossil emu in East Antarctica and a fossil rhea in West
Antarctica, for example, would not upset the global allopatry at all. What
would upset it would be, say, a fossil ostrich in Australia or New Zealand,
or a fossil kiwi in South America. That would be good evidence for more
extensive dispersal following the original allopatry.
