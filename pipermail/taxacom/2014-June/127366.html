Ken,

The point about the division is that if one is going to invoke extensive
dispersal then one has to explain how that dispersal happened in just such
a way that it did not eliminate the allopatry between the Indian Ocean
clade and the northern clade. Vicariance does not raise that contradiction,
and a vicariance origin of the two clades does address the evolution and
biogeography of the southern clade as having originated over its current
range from a widespread ancestor with a range that also included the
northern group. Nothing complicated about that.

It's a bit perplexing to understand the fossil calibration 'excuse' to push
back the time scale. The fact is that fossils and fossil calibrations are
only minimal dates. To project them as anything but is to invoke a kind of
mysticism for scientific method. While one may (or may not, I presume you
have not done a survey) be hard pressed to find any (actually Mike is a
botanist so your assertion is thereby falsified) who believes
Crossosmatales as a whole is older 110 million years, but a majority
opinion of itself does is not a necessary correlate of actuality. One would
have been hard pressed to find many biogeographers who accepted continental
drift back in the 1950's, but that did not mean they were correct in their
belief.

On what analytic basis is dispersal to Madagascar a "fairly safe bet"?

Antarctica was naturally an important element in Southern Hemisphere
biogeography, although perhaps less as a highway than as being associated
with different centers of differentiation as highlighted in Heads' book (a
section that should interest you very much).

Cheers,


John Grehan

> Hi Ken, The Indian Ocean clade of Crossosomatales (Madagascar - New
> Zealand and vicinity) has its sister group neatly allopatric to its north
> (New Guinea, Sri Lanka etc. northwards), so the simplest explanation is
> that they both originated by vicariance. The break between the two (located
> between Madagascar and Sri Lanka, between New Caledonia and New Guinea,
> etc.) coincides with belts of major rifting as well as breaks in countless
> other groups.
>  The 80 m.y. date for the clades is fossil calibrated and so it is a
> mimimum age. This is useful as it rules out earlier events (not older ones)
> as possible causes.   For the ratite clade, you seem to be supporting the
> idea that everything was everywhere and then gradually the distributions
> formed by extinction. So how and why were they everywhere to start with? It
> just gets more and more complicated for a very simple case of allopatry.
