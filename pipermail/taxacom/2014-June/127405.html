Hi Jason,

You wrote that: 'Tectonic events are subject to the same uncertainties as
any other
temporal data source'.

Radiometric dating means that many geological events are very well-dated.

Estimating the age of a clade from its oldest fossil is much more
problematic. Just
how much older is a clade than its oldest fossil? One million years? 20?
50? Most
authors pick something like 2-10? What do you think?


On Wed, Jun 18, 2014 at 9:52 AM, JF Mate <aphodiinaemate at gmail.com> wrote:

> This doesn?t really answer my key complaint.
> You could avoid the circularity by not testing tectonics with
> geologically dated phylogenies. A pity and a real loss of potentially
> useful data but surmountable. A bigger issue is the fact that tectonic
> "dates" are a range which can be very wide. So when you say "... if
> the phylogenetic break was caused by a spatially coincident tectonic
> feature, and this is dated at x million years old, then the
> phylogenetic break is also x million years old;..." you are ignoring
> two very important facts. Firstly, that geological dates are a range,
> from first opening to last connection (and I am ignoring the
> uncertainty in determining each of those dates, so there are ranges to
> each of your endpoints). Secondly, that taxa will vary in their
> response to the barrier emerging. Some will be cut off immediately,
> others will be able to cross it for a while, while others may have
> populations right around the barrier. With such complex scenarios the
> idea that you can collapse multiple splitting dates into a single
> common one is just not possible. The current iterative method, albeit
> imperfect, serves better the purpose of crosschecking (our differing
> opinions on dispersal not withstanding).
