I am happy to align with Rich.

In the original description of Nessiteras rhombopteryx, Nature provided a short abstract, which shows that the journal [topic editor?] saw the paper as the authors' intent of naming "a species whose existence is still a matter of controversy".

The only evidence in the paper itself is the statement that:
"their existence [i.e., the animals] seems closer to being established".

The zoo-Code applies only to taxa containing at least one "animal then known to exist in nature, past or present", and the authors of Nessiteras rhombopteryx admittedly appear to have had no doubts about the reality of "the large animal species living in Loch Ness". And if just one of the syntypes represented by the many sightings and photographs is of a fish or a seal (https://www.icrwhale.org/pdf/SC039151-157.pdf), then this requirement would probably be fulfilled. 

No matter how precise the zoo-Code is worded, we cannot avoid the shades of probability nor can we avoid the penumbra of not knowing exactly what is or was on any author's mind.

/Thomas

I'm going to do something I very (very) rarely do: disagree with Thomas:

> This is why scientific names proposed for 'Nessie', 'Bigfoot', and 
> other fictional creatures are not available.

As far as I can discern, the scientific names for both "Nessie" (Nessiteras rhombopteryx Scott & Rines 1975) and  "Bigfoot" (Homo sapiens cognatus Ketchum 2013) are available under the Code.  I am well aware of arguments (e.g., Notton et al. 2011) that the former is unavailable under Art. 1.3.1; but I think a careful reading of both Art. 1.3 and the original description (https://eur02.safelinks.protection.outlook.com/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.nature.com%2Farticles%2F258466a0.pdf&amp;data=04%7C01%7Ctpape%40snm.ku.dk%7C2d3c4ccb51e34bb86be408d8bf794152%7Ca3927f91cda14696af898c9f1ceffa91%7C0%7C0%7C637469875272089367%7CUnknown%7CTWFpbGZsb3d8eyJWIjoiMC4wLjAwMDAiLCJQIjoiV2luMzIiLCJBTiI6Ik1haWwiLCJXVCI6Mn0%3D%7C1000&amp;sdata=YF5WYCM4WUNnxFIDKVHxjYoob2mlU12euI3BYE9mgHo%3D&amp;reserved=0) make it clear that Art. 1.3.1 does not apply.  Note that Art. 1.3.1, in full (including the text of Art. 1.3), reads: "[Excluded from the provisions of the Code are names *proposed*] for hypothetical concepts;"  I emphasized the verb "proposed" because it references the context in which a name is established.  There is no indication that Scott and Rines *proposed* a name for a hypothetical concept -- there is every indication within the original publication that they proposed the name for what they asserted to be a very real organism (complete with photos purported to depict the type specimen).  It is often pointed out that the name itself is an anagram for "Monster hoax by Sir Peter S".  But this no more represents evidence that the authors intentionally proposed an name for a hypothetical concept than the fact that the name is also an anagram for "Yes, both pix are monsters – R" represents evidence that they believed it to be a genuine organism. Nothing in the original description suggests that it was proposed for a hypothetical concept.

It is likewise clear from the original description of H. s. cognatus that the authors very-much considered the taxon to be real (not hypothetical): https://eur02.safelinks.protection.outlook.com/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fheavy.com%2Fwp-content%2Fuploads%2F2013%2F10%2Fnovel-north-american-hominins-final-pdf-download.pdf&amp;data=04%7C01%7Ctpape%40snm.ku.dk%7C2d3c4ccb51e34bb86be408d8bf794152%7Ca3927f91cda14696af898c9f1ceffa91%7C0%7C0%7C637469875272089367%7CUnknown%7CTWFpbGZsb3d8eyJWIjoiMC4wLjAwMDAiLCJQIjoiV2luMzIiLCJBTiI6Ik1haWwiLCJXVCI6Mn0%3D%7C1000&amp;sdata=M3jY%2BQLgSPmzGAlQaAskYSo8J0r5pzelMS6x76lFqZg%3D&amp;reserved=0 

The point is, I think the verb "proposed" in the context of Art. 1.3 (and, by extension, Art. 1.3.1) clearly implies that the description itself must assert that the name is intended for a taxon that is not based on actual specimens (or, what are believed to be actual specimens), but rather a hypothetical concept (e.g. the hypothetical common ancestor of two extant taxa, absent any fossil evidence for such). If it were interpreted otherwise, then *any* name for which an extant type is not available could be argued to be "hypothetical" after the publication of the description, and declared unavailable (anagrams or not).

I haven't read the original description for Bicingulatus ninjabearus, but I gather it is evident from the paper itself that the taxon is hypothetical (e.g., "Therefore, this paper seeks to test the limits on the application of phototypes." -- evidently a misguided attempt to malign the need for the Code to accommodate non-extant type specimens). Thus, in this case, I think a strong case could be made that Art. 1.3.1 does apply to this name, if it was indeed proposed by the authors to apply to a hypothetical concept.

As for this:
> the circumscription of a species can be seen as a scientific 
> hypothesis

I fully agree with Thomas that it *can* be seen this way.  And in very rare cases it may actually be framed this way.  But in the vast, vast majority of cases, new species are proposed as *defined* entities, not *hypothesized* entities.  Hypotheses are falsifiable; definitions are not.
