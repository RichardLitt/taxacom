Hi Ken,

The idea that there has to be a constant molecular clock throughout the
phylogenetic history of a group is not an empirical finding. It is a model
of evolution that may not be true. We see this in the morphological aspect
of molecular genetics where different groups separated or dislocated by a
tectonic feature show different levels of morphological divergence (for
example with the NZ Alpine fault dislocation where correlated distributions
may be conspecific or species level differences even after more than 20
million years. So the panbiogeographic implication of tectonic correlation
is that this provides an approximation of age for the correlated groups. It
has no necessary implication for the upper limits of their phylogenetic
origins involving other sister taxa. If molecular clock models
incorporating a tectonic correlation result in unacceptable results that
does not invalidate the tectonic correlation. But it does raise questions
about the molecular model.  Nothing ridiculous about it at all. This has
been commented on in the panbiogeography literature.

Whether or not the acceptance of geological correlations as an
empirical source of evidence for vicariance is 'extreme obsession' or not
is neither here nor there. Fact is that fossil calibrated divergence
estimates are minimums and no one, including yourself, has demonstrated
anything to the contrary. And now what has been drawn to attention is the
larger and more problematic issue as to whether there is a constant
molecular clock over long periods of time. Like priors, molecular clocks
are also theoretical models (and there are different models) and open to
question or refutation. I would like to see you, Ken, show how a constant
molecular clock is substantiated as empirical evidence (I guess you won't
based on your past reluctance concerning fossil calibration, but I might as
well ask).

Cheers, John Grehan
