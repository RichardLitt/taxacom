Hi Roland

"And still, annoyingly, animals move and seeds are dispersed, even over
long distances. Who could imagine that this ever would have an effect on
anything"

Not sure why it's annoying. Every species had to disperse one way or
another to be where they are, even over 'long' distances. That is why one
sees some species with near global ranges. And that is consistent with taxa
that have global ranges with allopatric sub-clades - the ancestor was
global (more or less) and differentiated allopatrically (in other words
vicariance).

Your host-parasite outline is quite interesting. If one views hosts as
'island habitats' then I think the situation is comparable to the idea of
metapopulations of taxa on physical islands, where the islands may be
geographically isolated, but are temporally connected by past islands and
island interactions. But even with that interchange between islands,
vicariance may occur as tectonic events isolate particular island sectors.
That is why one gets, for example, different taxa that are reciprocally
allopatric in the Pacific despite each spanning multiple islands. Just as
you outline for parasites that there is no 1:1 relationship between the
animal host and parasite phylogenies, so too one usually finds the same
situation with  plant host and parasite (phytophage) phylogenies, and yet
both can provide informative biogeographic patterns. Also, just as one
cannot assume that a host 'endemic' to a particular host originated with
that host, one cannot assume that an island endemic originated with that
island (and one sees the irony of some molecular studies using island age
as a calibration point when at the same time others find other taxa on the
same island calibrated by fossils to be older than the island).

Cheers, John Grehan

> As fascinating as these interminable reruns are, I can't help but
> notice the parallel between this and Fahrenholz' rule.
>
> Fahrenholz' rule states that ectoparasitic animals that do not have a
> free-living stage are bound definitely to their hosts, and thus will
> speciate in parallel to these. Stated differently, it predicts that
> dispersal from one host to another is of no practical or evolutionary
> importance, and that vicariance on a single host lineage is the
> exclusive mechanism that dominates louse evolution. This results in
> the famous "Parasite phylogeny should mirror host phylogeny", which
> inevitably lead to the idea that *any* louse species found on a novel
> host species must represent a new species; if on a new host family or
> host order, it may represent a new louse genus. Morphology was
> speedily disregarded by the maxim "It is methodologically more correct
> to say that we don't know the morphological differences than to state
> that there are none" (Eichler, 1942 I think). Multitudes of species
> were then described based on host associations (or with the regular
> phrase "Species X is well separated from other species by characters
> of the head", with no details or detailed illustrations). This
> artificial host specificity was subsequently taken as evidence for
> Fahrenholz' rule, in an excellent example of circular reasoning.
>
> In recent decades, both morphological revisions and molecular work has
> been very unkind to Fahrenholz. Both lines of evidence have
> independently shown that host specificity varies greatly and some
> species of lice occur on great numbers of hosts with few or no
> morphological and genetic differences between populations on different
> host species. The same species of lice is known to be well established
> on host species belonging to different orders in many cases (e.g.
> buzzard, falcons, and pigeons). In other cases, of course, there are
> high degrees of co-speciation and host specificity; it all depends on
> the groups examined. In general, the more louse species there are in a
> genus, the more likely it is that there will be some species that
> break Fahrenholz' rule. We recently published an example of a species
> that is known (genetically, it is not described yet) from over a dozen
> different host families ranging from Australia and the Philippines
> through South China all the way to Malawi. Specimens from across this
> range are genetically more or less identical, and there are no real
> morphological differences other than what may be expected within a
> species. From morphological samples, we have found about 50 more host
> species, which include more or less the whole Old World tropics, and
> about 20 different host families.
>
> Similarly, groups of genera that are morphologically and genetically
> very similar occur on widely distantly related hosts. We recently
> realised that lice on pigeons, penguins, sandpipers, and grebes form a
> monophyletic group genetically, and share at least two morphological
> characters that are not found in any other lice. Yet these bird groups
> are not very closely related at all. Similarly, the
> Degeeriella-complex occurs on a range of distantly related bird
> groups, with no obvious patterns (e.g. some but not all kingfishers,
> grouse and pheasants but not other galliforms, some New Guinean
> passerines and pittas, but barely any other passerines, etc.). At the
> time of writing, there is not a single published example that has been
> studied in any detail during the last 30 years in which Fahrenholz'
> rule applies without exception.
>
> Thus, even millimetre-long lice, that live their whole life on the
> host, and have no free-living stage and no intermediary hosts, and
> cannot move even a centrimetre on a non-feather surface, can somehow
> move between hosts, including over vast distances and between
> distantly related hosts and establish viable populations there. There
> are many different mechanisms known for this (water walking, phoresy
> on flies, etc.), and many of these have been dismissed by adherents of
> Fahrenholz' as having no or little impact. Yet obviously they have.
> Comparisons with animals that can actually move longer than a few
> millimeters on their own volition are perhaps obvious to all.
>
> These days, Fahrenholz' rule is all but abandoned, except in the sense
> of a null hypothesis that can be tested. The great adherents to this
> rule (Eichler, Zlotorzycka, etc.) are all gone. Of all the louse
> researchers in the world, there seems to be only one person who gives
> this idea any credence any longer; he published a half-hearted defence
> of this rule last year, reiterating Eichler's comment from 1942
> paraphrased above. The pretty pattern so dogmatically adhered to by
> would-be-theoreticians is ruined completely by the fact that animals
> move.
>
