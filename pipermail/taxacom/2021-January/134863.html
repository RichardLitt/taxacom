OK, thanks Francisco.  I think we agree.  My main point is that the question of treating a taxon for a proposed new name as "hypothetical" (or not) -- i.e., whether or not Art. 1.3.1 applies -- should be based on information contained within the original publication; not based on subsequent information external to the original publication.

> Someone who publishes a figure of such an ancestor and composes that of an

> represent the concept.
> In contrast to the koala bear case that author would have had serious
> scientific intentions, but nevertheless both cases can probably be compared

> suggest that the concepts are hypothetical.

> Am 24.01.2021 um 00:42 schrieb Richard Pyle:
> > Thanks, Francisco.  I think the distinction with respect to Art. 1.3.1 is not so
> much whether the authors "believed" that the organism existed; but rather
> whether they were basing the name on physical material.  To me, a name
> proposed for the hypothetical ancestor of one or more other organisms,
> absent any physical evidence for the existence of such an organism, is what
> Art. 1.3.1 should be interpreted as clearly unavailable in this context.  In other
> words, I think there has to be some clear explicit (or very clearly implicit)
> indication on the part of the authors that the existence of the organism being
> name is speculated, bot based on physical material.  The case of "Nessie" is
> right on the edge, per my reply to Thomas.
> >
> > You raise a good point about the fact that B. ninjabearus did indeed include
> an image of a physical organism, so this might also be one of those "edge
> cases".  Perhaps it is better treated as an available name and subjective
> heterotypic junior synonym of Phascolarctos cinereus?
> >
> > In general, I think the key is to avoid rules in the Code that allow subsequent
> subjective declarations to impact the availability of the name, such as "I
> seriously doubt that the organism you claim to represent the type specimen

> > But I agree we should put some thought into this for Code5.
