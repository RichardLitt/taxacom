Thanks, Thomas!  Good points (as usual)!  I'm not sure that the "abstract" (clearly authored by someone other than Scott & Rines) has any bearing on the hypothetical nature of taxon for which the new name is proposed.  But I missed the line in the article itself about "their existence seems closer to being established", and agree that suggests that the authors themselves were not certain about the non-hypothetical existence of the taxon.  Thus, I could be persuaded to agree that Art. 1.3.1 applies to N. rhombopteryx.  Of course, there is also the subsequent assertion that the "organisms" represented the images of the purported specimen(s) are actually non-biological, and therefore the name could be regarded as unavailable under Art 1.1 (among others).

And, of course, I fully agree that the Code cannot be perfect, and that "fuzzy" bits are inevitable.  My main point was that Art. 1.3.1 should be interpreted in the context of the name *as proposed* in the original publication, not on subsequent community consensus regarding the "reality" of the organism as named.

> Note that Art. 1.3.1, in full (including the text of Art. 1.3), reads: "[Excluded
> from the provisions of the Code are names *proposed*] for hypothetical
> concepts;"  I emphasized the verb "proposed" because it references the
> context in which a name is established.  There is no indication that Scott and
> Rines *proposed* a name for a hypothetical concept -- there is every
> indication within the original publication that they proposed the name for
> what they asserted to be a very real organism (complete with photos
> purported to depict the type specimen).  It is often pointed out that the name
> itself is an anagram for "Monster hoax by Sir Peter S".  But this no more
> represents evidence that the authors intentionally proposed an name for a
> hypothetical concept than the fact that the name is also an anagram for "Yes,
> both pix are monsters – R" represents evidence that they believed it to be a
> genuine organism. Nothing in the original description suggests that it was
> proposed for a hypothetical concept.
>

> The point is, I think the verb "proposed" in the context of Art. 1.3 (and, by
> extension, Art. 1.3.1) clearly implies that the description itself must assert that
> the name is intended for a taxon that is not based on actual specimens (or,
> what are believed to be actual specimens), but rather a hypothetical concept
> (e.g. the hypothetical common ancestor of two extant taxa, absent any fossil
> evidence for such). If it were interpreted otherwise, then *any* name for
> which an extant type is not available could be argued to be "hypothetical"
> after the publication of the description, and declared unavailable (anagrams
> or not).
> 
> I haven't read the original description for Bicingulatus ninjabearus, but I
> gather it is evident from the paper itself that the taxon is hypothetical (e.g.,
> "Therefore, this paper seeks to test the limits on the application of
> phototypes." -- evidently a misguided attempt to malign the need for the
> Code to accommodate non-extant type specimens). Thus, in this case, I think
> a strong case could be made that Art. 1.3.1 does apply to this name, if it was
> indeed proposed by the authors to apply to a hypothetical concept.
> 
> As for this:
> > the circumscription of a species can be seen as a scientific
> > hypothesis
> 
> I fully agree with Thomas that it *can* be seen this way.  And in very rare
> cases it may actually be framed this way.  But in the vast, vast majority of
> cases, new species are proposed as *defined* entities, not *hypothesized*
> entities.  Hypotheses are falsifiable; definitions are not.
