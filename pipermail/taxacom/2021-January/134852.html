> Thanks Rich,

> Well, I think the intention of the author and the state of scientific
> knowledge at the time of publication are things that should be taken
> into accont, and I agree with you, certainly not today's state of
> scientific knowledge. Nessie existing in the real world or not cannot be
> a decisive criterion.
>
> Thomas pointed out that Nessie is on the fair side of the edge, but only
> because of the line "their existence seems closer to being established".
> I think we agree that this is where the name becomes admissible for
> nomenclature.
>
> The term "hypothetical concept" has to do with a person having something
> in mind. The koala bear did exist, but the author distinguished his new
> species from the koala bear by having carnivorous teeth. So the concept
> was "koala bear-like animal with carnivorous teeth", and this taken
> together was something that only existed in the mind of the author, who
> used this example in the intention to test the limits.
>
> A name for a hypothetical ancestor should also be covered that rule. I
> agree with you that this is probably the intention of Art. 1.3.1. But
> also here we would probably need to look closely on which ones would be
> the decisive points where to look at.
> Someone who publishes a figure of such an ancestor and composes that of
> an image of an existing specimen, to which are added some hypothetical
> structures that the author believes that the ancestor would have had,
> would technically have based that taxon partly on an existing specimen.
> But in the mind of the author that physical specimen was not intended to
> represent the concept.
> In contrast to the koala bear case that author would have had serious
> scientific intentions, but nevertheless both cases can probably be
> compared (= they would be equally far away from the edge, I think they
> land on the far side of the edge) because in both publications the
> evidence in the work would suggest that the concepts are hypothetical.
>
> Cheers
> Francisco

> Am 24.01.2021 um 00:42 schrieb Richard Pyle:
> > Thanks, Francisco.  I think the distinction with respect to Art. 1.3.1
> is not so much whether the authors "believed" that the organism existed;
> but rather whether they were basing the name on physical material.  To me,
> a name proposed for the hypothetical ancestor of one or more other
> organisms, absent any physical evidence for the existence of such an
> organism, is what Art. 1.3.1 should be interpreted as clearly unavailable
> in this context.  In other words, I think there has to be some clear
> explicit (or very clearly implicit) indication on the part of the authors
> that the existence of the organism being name is speculated, bot based on
> physical material.  The case of "Nessie" is right on the edge, per my reply
> to Thomas.
> >
> > You raise a good point about the fact that B. ninjabearus did indeed
> include an image of a physical organism, so this might also be one of those
> "edge cases".  Perhaps it is better treated as an available name and
> subjective heterotypic junior synonym of Phascolarctos cinereus?
> >
> > In general, I think the key is to avoid rules in the Code that allow
> subsequent subjective declarations to impact the availability of the name,
> such as "I seriously doubt that the organism you claim to represent the
> type specimen of your new species ever actually existed".
> >
> > But I agree we should put some thought into this for Code5.
