Hi Michael,
> Why aren't species relevant to a discussion of speciation mechanisms?!
> The species are putatively monophyletic groups

* No, this goes back to the previous taxacom discussion on "paraphyletic species" - I think it is a confusion to apply monophyletic/paraphyletic concepts to species, only GROUPS OF SPECIES.

* The fact that particular species may have insular distributions is irrelevant to the discussion of speciation, because it is too easily explained by other factors. For example, the fact that the giant flightless weevil Anagotus turbotti has an insular distribution (today) is easily explained by extinction on the mainland - it is hardly a coincidence that the only giant flightless weevils surviving in N.Z. today are either (1) on rat-free offshore islands, or (2) in the middle of speargrass clumps on the mainland, which no sensible rat would attempt to go near! A. turbotti is almost identical to A. stephenensis, today confined to Stephens Island, but with subfossil remains known from the mainland.

* Some island endemics certainly didn't have a formerly more widespread distribution, as there is no reason why they should have become extinct on the mainland - they are genuine (single island group) primary endemics, probably evolved from a small founder population of a mainland sister species. Example, the small flightless stag beetle, Paralissotes triregius, on the Three Kings. There is an almost identical species surviving in Te Paki, and other close relatives elsewhere in Northland. A published record of a new species of Paralissotes on the Poor Knights, allegedly closest to P. triregius, has been shown to be bogus. It is P. mangonuiensis, which also occurs on the Northland mainland, and without a phylogenetic analysis, it was a purely subjective call that it is closest to P. triregius.

* Anyway, I'm not sure where this discussion is going! It started from someone's comment about "paraphyletic species". I said that paraphyly is probably wrongly applied to species, and suggested that speciation may well normally happen by way of rapid evolution of small populations that become isolated by vicariant or dispersal events. I gave an example of the Three Kings Islands, suggesting that many of its endemics are clearly derived from mainland forms. OK, so that DETAIL could be wrong if either (1) the whole northeastern island arc was a continuous (or semicontinuous) landmass at some stage; or (2) due to "island hopping", there are insular monophyletic species groups, so Three Kings taxa could be closer to Poor Knights taxa, than to mainland taxa. Nevertheless, both possibilities are consistent with my point about "paraphyletic species". I assume you are not suggesting that a monophyletic group could somehow evolve "in concert" on unconnected
 islands, without gene flow between the islands!!!

Stephen

>>>> Hi Richard,
>>>> ???????Such an expectation by some cladists that
>>>> ancestor-daughter species "inexorably" become sister species seems to be
>>>> largely wishful thinking. This might tend to occur in cases where the
>>>> daughter species has a population that is not greatly smaller than the
>>>> population of the mother species. However, such an expectation seems
>>>> rather unlikely in frequent speciation where there some element of
>>>> "founder effect", especially when the mother species is highly
>>>> polytypic. This would be most evident in extreme cases where the founder
>>>> is a single pregnant female (such as for metazoans) or even a single
>>>> seed (for plants) which manages to survive in a new environment and is
>>>> reproductively isolated.? ? ? ? ?
>>>> ? ? ? The paraphyly is even more evident when one adds the time
>>>> dimension and considers the earlier history of the mother species (which
>>>> would usually be broader both genetically and phenotypically) than it
>>>> was at the later time when it gave rise to its daughter species.? If I
>>>> was a strict cladist, I would find it VERY disturbing to conceive of one
>>>> sister species having perhaps evolved a considerable period earlier than
>>>> its sister species.? Given the lack or sparsity of a fossil record
>>>> (along the stem between nodes) leading to most such species pairs,
>>>> having no good evidence of relative age makes it even more problematic.???
>>>> ? ? ???Extinction and a poor fossil record is truly a double-edged sword
>>>> that can make the recognition of clades both useful in the face of that
>>>> lack of information, but often only temporarily useful and subject to
>>>> destablizing challenges to that cladistic recognition when fossil
>>>> intermediates are eventually discovered.? The trick is to occasionally
>>>> use paraphyly where cladistic assumptions seem likely to be overturned
>>>> by new information (fossil or otherwise).? Paraphyletic speciation (and
>>>> paraphyly at higher taxonomic levels) is too common for strict cladism
>>>> to continue hoping that it won't cause them any major problems (and
>>>> classificatory unstability for us all).? Stability and usefulness
>>>> require the occasional use of paraphyletic taxa at appropriate points in
>>>> the Tree of Life.
>>>> ? ? ? ---------Ken Kinman
