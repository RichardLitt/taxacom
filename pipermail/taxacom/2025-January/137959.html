The species names which are clearly adjectives and not possibly nouns will
need to be changed. According to the Code, grammatical agreement supersedes
prevailing usage or tradition. It may take some years for people to
recognize the change and go with it, but it is significantly easier to
update online taxonomies now than it has been in the past, so the risk to
stability in the long run is low, in my opinion.

The same applies to any genus ending in -dasytes, like Eudasytes, which is
mentioned in the linked article. That article is behind a Paywall, so I did
not look at it directly.

Recognizing the gender of a genus name has been used incorrectly is not a
First Revisor action, however - that's only for choosing one spelling when
two are given in an original description.

> > There is a genus name derived from Old Greek which was introduced in
> 1799, and its author considered it masculine in grammatical gender,
> evidenced in the fact that the author transferred three species to his
> taxon and changed the epithets from feminine to masculine endings. And that
> was the prevailing usage for 226 years. Until now: Recently, it was
> discovered that the Old Greek word used as genus name is feminine in
> grammatical gender. Thus, a change is mandatory according to the Code - the
> parts of a binomen must agree in grammatical gender. But what about the 226
> years of "tradition" - can we simply ignore it? What is nomenclatural
> stability in this case? I'm asking because I fear that there will be coming
> a phase of both correct and continuous wrong usage: We experts can
> propagate the news, write correct ID labels and publish with the correct
> endings. But most users will stick to the "tradition", because they use
> outdated works and do not often consult the experts. Unless the change
> makes it into the databanks online, which will be checked hopefully.
