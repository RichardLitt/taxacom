Hi Mary, all,

I guess my comments above were mainly indicating my mild surprise that the
worlds of virologists and other taxonomists (as perhaps represented on
Taxacom) seem to intersect so little; maybe in years gone by, virology was
not taught much in biology classes and vice versa (certainly the case in
the education system that I was part of). Whether things are different
today, I do not know.

At any rate, virology has just about finished going through its own
nomenclatural revolution, allied to that attributed to Linnaeus in the
remainder of biology, in converting all of its "vernacular style" names to
a binomial system, a process without its total supporters as recently as
2020 (Gibbs, A., 2020. Binomial nomenclature for virus species: a long
view. *Archives of virology*, *165*(12), pp.3079-3083). As late as 2023,
Bimrew & Abera (Review on Classification and Nomenclature of Viruses.
*Sciences*, *11*(2), pp.11-23) wrote:

"There isn't a standardized format for virus species names yet, in contrast
to the binomial nomenclature used for cellular species. The ICTV now
requires that a species name comprise as few words as possible while still
being distinct, and must not merely contain the term virus and the host
name [32]. Especially for higher plants and animals, species names
frequently have the form of [Disease] virus. The ICTV released a proposal
in 2019 for a vote in 2020 to adopt a more defined system of binomial
nomenclature for viral species names. Some virologists argued that the
debate took place while many in the field were preoccupied owing to the
COVID-19 pandemic, which is why they later opposed the prospective
modification to the naming system [66]."

However in fact that statement had already been overtaken by events: in
2021, Walker et al. (Changes to virus taxonomy and to the International
Code of Virus Classification and Nomenclature ratified by the International
Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (2021). *Archives of virology*, *166*(9),
pp.2633-2648.) reported: "[In March 2021] ICTV mandated a uniform rule for
virus species naming, which will follow the binomial 'genus-species' format
with or without Latinized species epithets. The Study Groups are requested
to convert all previously established species names to the new format."

This process was then undertaken with the result that all but a handful
(maybe 10 out of 14,000+ virus species names) of species names in the 2023
ICTV species list are now in binomial form. It should also be noted,
however, that what we commonly call a "virus", such as the the coronavirus
SARS-CoV-2 that is the cause of Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), are
actually strains which live a level below species rank; in this case the
strain SARS-CoV-2 belongs in the species now called Betacoronavirus
pandemicum (genus Betacoronavirus, family Coronaviridae, etc. etc.) along
with (according to Wikipedia) SARS-CoV-1, Bat SARS-like coronavirus WIV1,
Bat coronavirus RaTG13, plus "Numerous other bat-hosted strains". I guess
papers such as the following give an overview of the process of switching
to binomial nomenclature as a 2022 snapshot:

Turner, D., et al., 2023. Abolishment of morphology-based taxa and change
to binomial species names: 2022 taxonomy update of the ICTV bacterial
viruses subcommittee. *Archives of Virology*, *168*(2), p.74.

The above all refers to the issue of translating virus names from
vernacular to binomial format, an aspect raised earlier in this thread but
in fact not in my original post (but clearly important as well). Not being
a virologist, the perhaps bigger issues that I originally sought to bring
attention to, namely the massive changes to virus higher taxonomy
(including the introduction of 10 kingdoms within a set of 6 new "realms")
as well as the proliferation of new named virus species over the past 5+
years, I leave to others more experienced than myself to possibly comment
upon (or not...)
