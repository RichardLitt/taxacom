Hi Jason,

Thanks for the latest comments. I have inserted my responses below:

“ Anyway, it seems that we can all more or less agree that timing is the key
difference between both mechanisms”

In my experience I would say that what one believes about timing may
determine how vicariance proposals are accepted or not.

“ patterns in themselves can´t distinguish either mechanism, so they are not
informative in this specific instance and we can dispense with tracks and
other such pattern searching.”

Well that is your view. I hold a different view.

“The problem is that if the timing is not in agreement with the tectonic
evidence then
vicariance can no longer be a contender for the time being.”

If the timing is correct, then sure. But the timing that is usually invoked
is in the form of divergence estimatins that are minimal rather than
actual. That’s the problem clear and simple.

 This is not a rejection of vicariance but a simple observation that
the evidence
available just isn´t´t in agreement and dispersal must be considered as
likely.”

Trouble is that what is often presented as falsifying evidence is not. When
divergence stimates are presented as falsifying evidence of earlier origins
one is, whether intentional or not, presenting a große Lüge.

“is semantic footplay posing as scientific rigour. There are limitations
and these have always been acknowledged by molecular taxonomists from
the beginning,
but not to be used as an underhanded, semantic mallet to clobber dissent.”

OK. But still a problem for me.



“And therein lies the issue I have with you and Michael. Nobody is
questioning vicariance, you question dispersal.”

Very sorry about that. Problem is that biogeography leads me to question
the role of dispersal. Can’t be helped.



“So really, we only need one example of dispersal to invalidate your
epistemological
building”

No.

“It only involves the Big Lie about molecular estimates.” Quacks like a
duck and all that.”

Meaning molecular estimation quacks like a duck?



“ Neither vicariance nor dispersal are on trial here. They are both
generally accepted mechanisms (except by you two it seems)”

How they work is on trial. Panbiogeography presents evidence that
vicariance and dispersal do not work according to Darwinian assumptions.


“ The available evidence, the research on this topic, is pretty much in
agreement with Ken´s assertion.”

What evidence?


What do you bring to the table to refute this”

Refute what?


“Claiming that “One can only assert otherwise by the Große Lüge that fossil
calibrated molecular estimates are not minimums.” is pure semantics.”

A minimum is a minimum is a minimum. The semantics come in when people
misrepresent minimums as something else.


“The burden of proof is with you providing fossil evidence or a new dataset
that, when calibrated, contradicts the previous studies”.

That’s one argument. Or there are other forms of evidence such as
biogeographic correlation.



“Ken also mentions the Malagasy fauna as having recent elements that precede
its split from Gondwana, and he is correct in this regard as well.”

How so?

“There are truly ancient lineages that are vicariant but much more recent
ones that cannot have arrived by means other than dispersal (e.g. The dung
beetle fauna is a combination). “

Evidence?

“If you can provide counterfactual evidence that can be profitably
discussed then that would be great. Semantics not so much.”

Need to know your evidence first before profitable discussion.

Cheers,

John Grehan

> >> first step, but lack of suitable habitat, and more importantly
> competitive
> >> exclusion by other taxa already well-established, are obviously

> >> even good dispersers being automatically spread geographically. Such

> >> John,
> >>
> >> analyzing biogeographic distributions is not very useful in the
> >> absence of a time scale. Timing is often the only difference between
> >> dispersal and vicariance, and all the arguments I can recall revolve
> >> around the absolute or relative timing of splits of one lineage vs
> >> another and/vs tectonics. That is why I think you focus so much on the
> >> only proxy we have to complete the extremely patchy fossil record.
> >> In the particular case of the Platyrrhini, the available evidence
> >> suggests that the age for the group is c. 25-32mya
> >> (https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msg172) and this is the most widely
> >> accepted date (give or take but close to this range) using well
> >> accepted molecular dating methods and fossils. You can quibble about
> >> fossils and calibrations if the window was small enough, but the gap
> >> is a chasm considering what you would need for the alternate scenario,
> >> so we can only conclude, based on the available evidence at hand, that
> >> the NW monkeys arrived there over sea and not as a result of
> >> vicariance. Should fossils be found at a later date that push the
> >> origin back sufficiently to consider the latter scenario then great,
> >> but so far this is not the case. If they made it there swimming,
> >> rafting or island-hopping (all three possible perfectably reasonable
> >> dispersal mechanisms) is a matter of testing the ability of these
> >> monkeys to survive each of these scenarios. None of this is a fairy
> >> tale, pseudoscience nor an attack on vicariance.
> >>
> >> This sort of dovetails with Michael´s often repeated question of "why
> >> only once". My answer is because dispersal is hard, unplanned and the
> >> chances of success slim to nil.
