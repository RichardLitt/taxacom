>
> Many thanks for the kind words, Adam. To be fair, I live in a part of 
> the world where the time zone is UTC-10, which means when the clock 
> struck midnight UTC on September 4, I was banging on my keyboard at 
> 2pm (14:00) in the comfortable afternoon of September 3rd. As I write 
> this now, September 4 has now caught up to my part of the planet.
>
> Regarding the issue of the need for evidence of registration to be 
> overtly visible within electronic documents, I think you make a fair 
> point.  The reason it was worded that way (not actually part of the 
> legislative text of the Code, but rather in an Example) is because of 
> the nature of electronic documents containing information *useful* to 
> readers, even if not visible to the eyes of readers.  It was an 
> acknowledgement of direction electronic publication was heading.
>
> In the ten years since the de-facto start of Code-compliant electronic 
> publication, we've learned a lot about what works reasonably well, and 
> what doesn't work reasonably well.  Some of the latter have been 
> addressed by many individuals (including extensively by several 
> lengthy publications by Dubois and co-authors, including one recently 
> published item linked by Dubois earlier in this thread). A few of the 
> issues have not been discussed as much, in part because they are not 
> as obvious or visible.  I mention this, because I think it's helpful 
> to take a moment to review the broader context of all of this stuff. 
> And as today marks the tenth anniversary, it seems like a good 
> opportunity to capture this moment.  I can think of no better use of 
> my leisurely Sunday than to wax on about this noteworthy moment in 
> nomenclatural history. Fair Warning: If you’re not interested in the 
> history of scientific nomenclature or the evolving nature of Codes 
> governing nomenclature, NOW is the time to stop reading.  But if you 
> are interested in these matters, I offer my take on them as follows:
>
> Over the centuries prior to Linnaeus, there was no universal standard 
> for applying text-string labels to defined sets of organisms (i.e., 
> inferred taxa). By the early 18th century, the general practice of 
> binominal nomenclature was codified in various editions of Linnaeus' 
> /Index Plantarum/ and /Systema Naturae/, after which there was much 
> greater (though not perfect) consistency in the practice of assigning 
> text labels to taxa.  Things got a bit confused a little over a 
> century later with the ideas of Wallace and Darwin beginning to gain 
> widespread acceptance in academic circles, but somehow the 
> nomenclatural system developed by the Creationist Linnaeus managed to 
> survive this philosophical and scientific transition regarding the 
> relationships of living things to each other and the nature of the 
> origins of biodiversity. This was probably largely due to the way in 
> which Linnaeus’ hierarchical classification system and associated 
> nomenclature was more or less applicable to representing patterns of 
> evolutionary relationships.
>
> Towards the latter part of the 19th Century, many practitioners of 
> taxonomy recognized problems in nomenclatural stability.  Though the 
> system championed by Linnaeus brought a large degree of universal 
> practice in biological nomenclature, it included insufficient detail 
> to address all aspects of scientific nomenclature.  Thus, Codes of 
> nomenclature for both Plants (inclusive of algae and fungi) and 
> Animals were created to provide more guidance and promote universal 
> practice, with the hope of stabilizing biological nomenclature.  The 
> early editions of these Codes were a step in the right direction, but 
> unsurprisingly were imperfect. Over time, shortcomings and 
> imperfections of the rules established in these Codes were recognized, 
> and so they needed to be updated.  For Zoology, this led to the 
> second, third, and fourth Editions of the ICZN Code (with various 
> Amendments), and in Botany the practice of revising the Code every six 
> years (in association with meetings of the International Botanical 
> Congress).  Similarly, the International Code of Nomenclature of 
> Bacteria (now the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes) 
> underwent multiple versions and revisions.
>
> All three of these major nomenclatural Codes acknowledge the need to 
> change over time, for two main reasons.  First, the rules of each 
> version/edition essentially represent reactions to problems or 
> imperfections that were either not addressed by earlier versions, or 
> in some cases were created by modifications made in earlier versions.  
> In each case, the community of taxonomic practitioners who follow 
> these Codes would come to encounter problems that threatened 
> nomenclatural stability, so solutions were proposed to address those 
> problems. Each proposed solution and modification was something of a 
> prediction or hypothesis along the lines of “With this change to the 
> rules, there will be an increase in nomenclatural stability.”  Only in 
> the years following the implementation of those changes would the 
> “evidence” be accumulated to test this hypothesis.  In some cases, 
> nomenclatural stability is increased (i.e., successful rule change).  
> In other cases, there was no net effect on stability (i.e., increased 
> bureaucracy without benefit).  In a few cases, rule changes may have 
> actually lead to a /decrease/ in overall nomenclatural stability. But 
> the point is, one of the reasons why the rules of the Code need to 
> evolve over time is that they are not perfect, and an iterative 
> approach of “try a different rule and see if it works” is a necessary 
> model for progress.
>
> The second reason that the rules need to change over time is that the 
> priorities and needs of the constituents (i.e., the community of 
> practicing taxonomists) change.  Sometimes this is a result of the 
> evolution of what role nomenclature plays in how taxonomists (and 
> biologists, and other scientists and non-scientists) communicate with 
> each other (and what factors are regarded as the highest priority for 
> communication).  For example, in Linnaeus’ time, there was very little 
> priority (none, really) placed on representing the evolutionary 
> relationships of organisms through nomenclature and classification.  
> Since the time of Darwin (and especially over the past half-century or 
> so), this priority has increased.  Technological advancement also 
> plays a role.  The increasing utilization of molecular analysis 
> (especially DNA sequencing) has changed somewhat dramatically over the 
> past few decades, and this also has an impact on the rules of 
> nomenclature (see multiple discussions about how to interpret Art. 
> 13.1.1/13.1.1, for example).  And of course, the nature of 
> “publication” has changed rather dramatically since about the turn of 
> the most recent century… which brings me back to the topic at hand.
>
> Speaking in terms of the Zoological Code of nomenclature (with which I 
> am most familiar, but parallels exist in the other Codes as well), the 
> three Editions of the ICZN Code that existed when I began my career as 
> a taxonomist in the early 1990s had all been crafted/drafted in the 
> context of a widely adopted mechanism for scientific communication. 
> That is, in the form of words and symbols printed with ink on physical 
> pages of paper (or, as my friend Paul Kirk likes to call them, “very 
> thin slices of wood”).  This, of course, made perfect sense: the 
> practice of capturing and disseminating knowledge in this form had 
> gone back many centuries, and with certain famous examples 
> notwithstanding (e.g., Library of Alexandria), had a pretty decent 
> track record for promulgating information from one generation to the 
> next.  Thus, the rules of the various Editions of the Code were 
> structured around the notion of “publication” in this context. It 
> would have made no sense to craft them otherwise.
>
> During the latter part of the 20th century, the advent of the internet 
> and electronic forms of communicating information began to grow 
> rapidly. In fact, during the 1990s the internet was by many metrics 
> the fastest adopted new technology in all of human history (evidently 
> since surpassed by cryptocurrency, but that’s a whole ‘nother 
> Pandora’s can of beans I won’t dare to open here).  Also at that time, 
> the Fourth (and still current) Edition of the ICZN Code was drafted 
> and disseminated for public commentary, prior to going into effect on 
> 1 January 2000.  This was the first Edition of the Code that needed to 
> confront the evolving way in which scientists were starting to share 
> information with each other via electronic forms of documentation.  
> The internet was still so new (and perhaps scary), that the Fourth 
> Edition of the Code added a provision (originally Art. 9.8) that 
> excluded any work consisting of “text or illustrations distributed by 
> means of electronic signals (e.g. by means of the World Wide Web)” 
> from being “published” in the sense of the Code. This was further 
> indicated by adding the word “durable” to Art. 8.1.3.  However, 
> acknowledging that electronic information technology was a “thing”, 
> and was rapidly changing at the time the Fourth Edition of the Code 
> went into effect, the drafters of that version of the Code did allow 
> for works to be considered published if they were produced and 
> disseminated on “optical disc”, provided some additional requirements 
> were met.
>
> This “optical disc” business is worth an additional moment of 
> examination.  At the time the Fourth Edition was being drafted, the 
> internet was deemed too ephemeral and “new” to be considered as a 
> mechanism for documenting and promulgating Code-governed nomenclatural 
> acts (inclusive of new names), so it was excluded from consideration.  
> But it was obvious that computer technology more generally was a real 
> “thing”, and would continue to be so into the foreseeable future, so 
> the Code needed to be modified to accommodate it.  Although references 
> to “optical discs” (and earlier in the Introduction of the Code, 
> “laser disks”) seem quaint (almost comical) to us now, at the time it 
> was the best that the drafters of the Code could do to anticipate how 
> to accommodate /future/ needs of taxonomists.  In doing so, they felt 
> the need to add extra requirements to this form of “publication” 
> (e.g., they “must contain a statement naming at least five major 
> publicly accessible libraries in which copies of the optical disc were 
> to have been deposited”).  It was the best they could do to 
> accommodate a rapidly changing landscape of information dissemination, 
> so we should cut them some slack.  In the decade that followed the 
> implementation of the Fourth Edition of the Code, it became clear that 
> this approach to accommodating technological change was not quite as 
> prescient as had been hoped, so the Code was amended to stop this 
> practice. Of course, that wasn’t the only part of the Code that was 
> amended…
>
> This now brings us to the year 2008. The Fourth Edition of the Code 
> had been in effect for nearly a decade, and a couple of things had 
> become clear at that point. First, as already alluded to, the laser 
> disk/optical disc thing was not panning out, so it needed to be 
> changed in the Code (preventing the practice from continuing further). 
> This is an example of the iterative trial-and-error nature of changing 
> the Code, and fixing the bits that turn out to have not contributed to 
> increasing nomenclatural stability. Second, and more acutely (and more 
> directly relevant to the topic at hand), the internet was very rapidly 
> taking over as THE mechanism by which taxonomists (and biologists, and 
> other scientists and non-scientists) communicate with each other. The 
> practice of mailing out physical copies of reprints to colleagues via 
> the post was giving way to e-mailing electronic documents (especially 
> PDFs) to colleagues and others.
>
> The ICZN met in Paris that year to discuss a variety of issues that 
> were occupying the time and thoughts of the Commission, and the need 
> to deal with “electronic publication” was high on the list of topics 
> to discuss and debate. At the time, it was clear that PDFs were not 
> only beginning to dominate the publication world in terms of replacing 
> reprints, but in some cases it was starting to look like they were 
> replacing paper-printed editions of scientific publications.  Several 
> examples of electronic-only works introducing new names and/or other 
> nomenclatural acts had been identified, and the Commissioners realized 
> the trend was only going to increase, so urgent action (via an 
> Amendment to the Code) was needed.  After much discussion, a draft 
> Amendment was hammered out and debated within the Commission. The two 
> biggest issues of those debates were: 1) how to address the obvious 
> disparity between the centuries-old proven method of ink-on-paper 
> publication and the decades-old and very-much-Unproven method of 
> electronic documents – especially in terms of ensuring perpetuation of 
> original works?; and 2) what role should the then-new ZooBank play?
>
> The first issue is pretty self-evident: The reason the Code forbade 
> works “distributed by means of electronic signals” was because there 
> was only a short history of electronic means for storing information 
> long-term. Moreover, that short history had not inspired a lot of 
> confidence (I recall a lot of references to 8-track audio tapes, lost 
> or corrupted floppy discs, crashed hard drives, etc.).  Several ideas 
> to address this issue were proposed and debated, with a wide range of 
> practical and philosophical implications. None of these satisfied 
> everyone and, indeed, I seriously doubt that there is such a 
> universally satisfactory solution – at least not with existing 
> available technology. In the end, the cracks in the damn holding back 
> the deluge of electronic publications were ominous enough that the 
> need to accommodate electronic publication within the Code exceeded 
> the fear of losing all copies of electronic documents due to 
> insufficient long-term electronic storage technology, so we ended up 
> with an extremely feeble (in my opinion, anyway) compromise.
>
> As for ZooBank, at the time it was relatively new, and very-much 
> “unofficial” from the perspective of the Code and Commission. It 
> existed as a website and associated database, and had been launched 
> publicly on 1 January of that same year (2008) to coincide with the 
> 250th anniversary of the official start of modern zoological 
> nomenclature (see Art. 3.1 of the ICZN Code), and also to coincide 
> with the publication of the first prospectively registered scientific 
> names (five new species in the genus Chromis; DOI: 
> 10.11646/ZOOTAXA.1671.1.2); but it was not yet in any way associated 
> with the Code of Nomenclature. It had even less of a track record than 
> electronic publication, and therefore an even more uncertain future, 
> so there was a wide range of opinion for how much it should be 
> incorporated into the Amendment to the Code.  For the record: I was 
> NOT one of the people arguing for its inclusion in the Amendment as a 
> requirement.  As the person who primarily developed and maintained it, 
> I had wanted to allow it more time to evolve as an online service 
> before bearing any form of role  for Code-compliance. I was eventually 
> persuaded that the Amendment to accommodate electronic publication was 
> the obvious golden opportunity to introduce ZooBank to Code 
> regulations in a limited way, so I acquiesced.  However, I did 
> successfully persuade the majority of Commissioners that its role 
> should be limited to ONLY electronic publications, and ONLY require 
> registration of works (not of names or acts).  My key rationale was 
> that we were going to amend the part of the Code that dealt with 
> published works, so we should confine any registration requirements 
> accordingly.
>
> At the time, my perspective was that a centralized online/electronic 
> registration system would eventually serve the goal of nomenclatural 
> stability very effectively (this is still is my perspective).  
> However, the key word there is “eventually”.  Major changes to 
> international standards like the ICZN Code are most effective when 
> they are evolutionary (as opposed to revolutionary).  The thing that 
> most persuaded me to get on board the “require ZooBank registration 
> for electronic publications” was the opportunity to embark on a “test” 
> period for registration as a concept in this space.  I was very 
> nervous because I knew how rapidly the technology was changing, and I 
> was wary of creating another “laser disk/optical disc” situation for 
> the Code. But real progress often requires real risk, so I looked upon 
> the Amendment for electronic publication as an opportunity to kick off 
> a grand experiment that would allow us to test (and more importantly, 
> refine) our “hypotheses” about how best to shape the Code of 
> nomenclature to accommodate the rapidly evolving landscape of 
> scientific norms and practices.
>
> So… was the Amendment to allow electronic publication (and introduce 
> ZooBank registration) successful?  In one sense, clearly not.  There 
> are many issues that have come to light during these past ten years 
> that highlight the imperfections of the how the rules were modified. 
> This includes a wide variety of issues, most of which are in the realm 
> of what the Commission refers to as “limbo names” (i.e., names 
> published in electronic works that meet all of the “spirit” of the 
> Code rules, but fail on the basis of one or mor specific 
> technicalities).  On the other hand, in a much broader (and more 
> important) sense, the Amendment was a nearly-unqualified and 
> monumental success. Why?  Well… consider how many “limbo” names we’d 
> have if NONE of the electronic works of the past decade would be 
> published in the sense of the Code?  Sure, a some journals that now 
> publish only in electronic form might have taken the Code seriously 
> enough to have continued the practice of producing an issue containing 
> “numerous” “durable” ink-on-paper copies, but many (most?) would not 
> have done so.  And even the ones that did continue with paper copies, 
> would likely have done so with minimal compliance in mind.  
> Technically, only two paper copies that are made simultaneously 
> obtainable would fulfill the “numerous” requirement, which very-much 
> would NOT be keeping with the spirit/intention of the Code.  
> Obviously, many journals do continue to produce paper versions of 
> their publications, so the old-school practice does continue. But 
> there is still a great deal of confusion with respect to the date of 
> publication, especially when PDF versions are often obtainable months 
> before the paper editions are.  Moreover, I’m pretty sure that the 
> majority of new names and nomenclatural acts today are published only 
> in electronic form, without paper versions.
>
> Keeping in mind that it’s nearly impossible to predict the future 
> (especially in the dynamic way in which “publication” continues to 
> change), I’d say that the Amendment was crafted the best way it could 
> have been, at the time.  Some might suggest that they could have 
> foreseen the problems at the time, and would have argued for a 
> different approach to how to accommodate electronic publication.  That 
> may well be true, but if so, then I wonder why they didn’t contribute 
> to the public discussion that spanned several years (2008-2012) on 
> multiple platforms (including this list, Taxacom).  And if they did 
> contribute, then their suggestions certainly were considered at the 
> time, but ultimately were offset by alternate considerations.
>
> As Adam noted, we are now in the midst of a wonderful opportunity to 
> capitalize on ten years of real-world experience with both electronic 
> publication and online registration via ZooBank.  We have learned a 
> LOT over those years, and unsurprisingly there are a LOT of good ideas 
> on how to improve things.  The Commission is carefully (and somewhat 
> painstakingly, but with good reason) going through the text of the 
> Fourth Edition of the Code, scrutinizing every word (sometimes 
> ad-nauseum) to generate the first draft of the Fifth Edition.  
> Francisco Welter-Schultes has done a MASTERFUL job leading this effort 
> on behalf of the Code editorial committee.  The Fifth Edition of the 
> Code will most likely be the most substantive change (and improvement) 
> in the Code’s history.  It will be open to public commentary for at 
> least a year (I suspect longer), and I consider this to be the most 
> important part of the process.  The Code, and the Commission, exist to 
> serve the needs of the taxonomic community, so the highest priority 
> should be on understanding and accommodating those needs as 
> effectively as possible. The experience we’ve had this past decade 
> (and by “we”, I include myself, the Commission, and the entire 
> taxonomic community) has proven to be immeasurably valuable for 
> shaping the Fifth Edition of the Code – not just for things like 
> ZooBank and electronic publication, but for almost all aspects of the 
> Code.  An enormous amount of work has already been done, and perhaps 
> even more work remains.  But overall, I’m increasingly excited about 
> the future of the Code, and of nomenclature and taxonomy in general.
>
> As a closing thought, I wanted to point to what I think are the most 
> prescient words in the entire Fourth Edition of the ICZN Code, located 
> in the Introduction section, as the last sentence of the second 
> paragraph under the heading, “Development of underlying principles”:
>
> “But it seems likely, in the longer term, and with the development of 
> new information systems, that the solution will not lie in patching up 
> a definition of publication but, rather, in scrapping it and finding a 
> means of replacing "publication" as a primary determinant of 
> availability.”
>
> These words capture my own perspective very eloquently – something 
> I’ve articulated many times on this and other public forums.  There is 
> no doubt in my mind that this is where zoological nomenclature is 
> heading.  The only real questions are how long it will take to get 
> there, and how best to navigate the transition.
>
> If you made it this far, then you’ve earned your title as 
> “nomenclatural nerd”. Welcome to the club!
>
> And again, happy 10th Anniversary to the era of Code-compliant 
> electronic publication!
