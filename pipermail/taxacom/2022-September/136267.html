I’m not sure that’s exactly in keeping with the intention and spirit of the requirement.  I know the value of indicating an online archive is low; but what you propose would literally set it to zero value.

  

If you’re going to go that route, why not just select CLOCKSS (or whatever) whenever registering a new work?  The fact that any author can do this is why the value of the requirement is zero.  The fact that very few do is why the value is non-zero.  But it does create the problem of (technically) unavailable electronic works, but this requires proving a negative (i.e., confirming that there was, in fact, no paper edition).  That’s hard (impossible?) to do.  You could argue that the publisher adamantly does not produce paper editions.  But if an author takes an unpublished (in the sense of the Code) PDF (unpublished because it failed to meet the ZooBank requirements), prints out “numerous” identical copies, and makes them simultaneously obtainable for free or by purchase, then doesn’t the work become published from that paper edition produced by the author?

  

The only argument for why the Code might prevent that is the phrase “when first issued” of Art. 8.1.2, but there are a bunch of reasons why that can’t really be used to determine that the self-printed copies of an otherwise unpublished (sensu Code) work doesn’t fulfill the criteria for publication.

  

Back to you point… the archive requirement is for the archive “intended” to permanently archive the work.  I think most would agree that the “intention” here refers to that of the author or publisher or issuer of the work, not of the ICZN or the ZooBank website.  By having a default value for online archives added to every new registration, it is representing the “intention” of whoever wrote the software code that assigns that archive.

  

I get what you’re suggesting (i.e., an easy way to conform to the existing rules, without generating technically unavailable names, and without the need for the cumbersome process to amend the existing Code).  But I would argue there are far more elegant ways of dealing with this particular flavor of “limbo” names then asserting a default archive for every Journal, asserted with “intent” by the ZooBank developers (or ICZN), rather than the intent of the author/publisher.

On Wednesday, 7 September 2022, 06:45:53 am NZST, Richard Pyle <deepreef at bishopmuseum.org> wrote: 

 

 

> In retrospect, it possibly should have been made impossible to register a 

> journal on ZooBank without specification of an archive, given that doing so serves no useful purpose.

 

Although I more or less agree with you that the requirement to indicate an intended archive serves little purpose, if indicating an online archive was a requirement for all journals in ZooBank, then how could we facilitate the (optional) registration of paper-printed journals (for which no online archive requirement applies)?  In the same way that we strongly encourage the registration of new names in ZooBank even though this is not required, we also strongly encourage the registration of paper-published works (including historical ones, going back to Linnaeus) even though this is not required.

 

Indeed, one of the earlier prototypes of ZooBank had a registration workflow that began with the first question: “Will this work be published electronically, or on paper?”  If the “electronic” option was selected, registration could not be completed without the required ISSN/ISBN and intended online archive.  After some initial testing, this workflow was abandoned because most registrations were contributed by authors, rather than journal editors or staff, and most authors had no idea whether a journal they planned to publish in used an archive or not (or which one).  This meant that authors who selected “electronic” either selected ALL of the online archives (not understanding what the purpose of the field was in ZooBank), or switched to registering it as a paper publication (often having no idea whether there was a paper edition or not).  After a fair bit of thought and discussion, the workflow was changed to avoid requiring authors to know whether an archive was used (and which one), and instead a warning is given for all prospective registrations that lacked an archive, letting the author know that the work and the names/acts contained within can only be made available under the Code if an archive is indicated, or from a paper edition of the work.  The hope was that the warning would prompt the author to contact the journal editor and sort it out.  I’m not sure whether that hope was achieved (I’d guess probably not).

 

This approach is not perfect, but it is the “least-bad” option.  As I’ve mentioned already, there are many ideas for how to improve things for the new paradigm under Code-5, which we hope to capture in the draft that will be released for public commentary.

> Not doing so would appear to invalidate nomenclatural acts in earlier issues of

Just to walk through the scenarios here:

 

1. On 1 April 2022 I register a new article ("A") in ZooBank, and create a new Journal ("Jx") for it, but without indicating an online archive for either the article or the new journal. This article fulfills all other requirements of the Code on 2 April 2022, except that it's only issued in electronic form, and is thus not published in the sense of the Code (because there's no online archive indicated).

 

2. After being admonished by my colleagues for failing to comply with the Code, I log in to ZooBank on 15 April 2022 and indicate Zenodo as the online archive for the article ("A").  

 

3. On 1 May 2022 I register another Article ("B"), within my existing Journal "Jx", and it fulfills all other requirements of the Code on 2 May 2022.  However, it's not available as a published work, because (again) there is no indicated online archive in ZooBank (recall that the archive for Article "A" was directly linked to the article, not to the parent journal).

 

4. I am again admonished by my colleagues, so I contact the Editor of the journal "Jx" and ask what online archive they routinely use.  I get a reply the same day that "Jx" has been using Biotaxa for the past 5 years.  I update the ZooBank record for the journal "Jx" on 15 May 2022, to indicate that all articles published in this journal inherit Biotaxa as the online archive.

 

5. On 1 June 2022, another colleague of mine (we'll call him "Stephen") registers another Article ("C") that he submits to the journal "Jx".  However, there is a glitch in the ZooBank software such that existing journals with titles shorter than 3 characters in length do not show up in the Journal drop-down list.  Thus, "Stephen" is understandably unaware that "Jx" is already in ZooBank, so he creates a duplicate copy of the Journal.  He doesn't assign it an online archive, because he believes that the journal will be published on paper.  This article ("C") is published electronically, and fulfills all other requirements of the Code, on 15 June 2022, except that it's only issued in electronic form, and is thus not published in the sense of the Code (because there's no online archive indicated).

 

6. Those mean old colleagues now direct their ire at "Stephen" for having created yet another unpublished work.  But "Stephen" has been using ZooBank extensively for years, and knows all the tricks to work around the bugs and glitches, and after taking a closer look he discovers there are indeed two copies of the journal "Jx" in ZooBank, and that the one he created was a duplicate.  He contacts the ZooBank Administrator about the problem, who then eventually corrects the inadvertent error by merging the two duplicate journals on 1 July 2022 (the Zoobank Admin was travelling to attend a wedding that week, so was slow in responding to Stephen's request).

 

In my interpretation:

- Article "A" was published in the sense of the Code on 15 April 2022 (when all Code-required criteria for electronic publication were fulfilled).

- Article "B" was published in the sense of the Code on 15 May 2022 (when all Code-required criteria for electronic publication were fulfilled; in this case, when an online archive was indicated for the parent journal).

- Article "C" was published in the sense of the Code on 15 June 2022 (following the logic explained in my previous email to a different "Stephen", whereby the duplicate journal "Jx" is considered an inadvertent error, and would have been selected as the correct parent journal had there not been a glitch in the ZooBank website software code).

 

Addressing your point, even though the Journal "Jx" has been using Biotaxa as an online archive for 5 years, it cannot have published any electronic works earlier than 15 May 2022, unless the individual Articles were registered and assigned directly to an online archive prior to that date.

 

Continuing the example, suppose that during one of his routine sweeps for duplicate journals, the ZooBank Admin discovers that the Journal "Jy" was registered five years earlier, on 15 November 2017.  Because it was accidentally misspelled, nobody realized that it was the same as the journal "Jx".  When "Jy" was registered in 2017, it was assigned to Biotaxa as the online archive.  In this scenario, my Article "A" would have been published in the sense of the Code on 2 April 2022, and my Article "B" wuld have been published in the sense of the Code on 2 May 2022.  It's just that nobody knew that to be true until the inadvertent duplication of Jy/Jx journal was discovered.

 

Again, in an ideal world there would be no gap between "truth" and what is visible from the work and/or ZooBank record.  But in this ideal world, I wouldn't have spent my entire 3-day weekend doing ZooBank stuff, etiher...
