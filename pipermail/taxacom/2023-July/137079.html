I think Doug's proposal is a necessary step no matter what outcome you
wanted, except reverting to original spelling which I think would be a
disaster. I favour keeping current/ prevailing usage as of a set date ie
when Code 5 was released, and then fixing the spelling from then on. Even
if the species is recombined.

The solution that retains gender agreement does not address the issue of
automation with ai. If we think we can continue to document millions of
species in this modern world without working with the abilities of
computers (and keeping that simple), well that's kind of a failure. The
solutions that retain gender agreement assume there is money to pay
taxonomists by the thousands to deep dive all taxa and continue to do so.
That money is not there. Humans have to be paid, computers maintained and
programmed, its cheaper and more efficient to work with computers not
against them.

Personally I do care about the etymology, but that is set by the author
when they describe the species, the spelling has little bearing on this in
the end. Humans can easily recognise partial match words and still
understand their meaning.

In turtles where I work there are some 35 professional taxonomists world
wide, there are some 600-700 professional biologists who just want a fixed
name, there are a further some 2-3 thousands people interested in turtles
for various reasons who also just want a fixed name. Then there are
managers who for legal reasons need fixed names. We who delve into the
intricacies of this are a minority of the end users of taxonomy.

Cheers Scott

> instead preferring to search for the original names used for each species.
> It might even satisfy the gender agreement camp as it superficially looks
> like gender agreement even though it isn’t.

> taxacom at lists.ku.edu> wrote:
> >
> > ﻿Man, this discussion does really seem to have hit quite a nerve.
> >
> > I don't have a lot of time today, but please allow me to make a few
> observations:
> >
> > Several times in the past - in fact, almost every time I have raised
> this topic - I have in fact addressed proposals to "fix" existing epithets,
> both based on "prevailing usage" (as Adam Cotton recently suggested) or an
> arbitrary date (as George Beccaloni recently suggested), or original
> spellings (suggested by several). These suggestions are not new, either to
> me, or to other members of the Commission.
> >
> > Others here have already pointed out numerous conceptual and practical
> issues with the various ways of "fixing" names, and I have also raised
> these criticisms in the past.
> >
> > Nearly all of the criticisms I've raised before have been presented in
> the recent discussion (e.g., by David Redei), with the exception of one,
> which a few people have alluded to, but not spelled out in full. I think it
> does bear repeating, and analyzing point-by-point.
> >
> > That is, ALL of the three models that seek to fix the spelling - either
> original spelling, prevailing usage, or arbitrary date cutoff - cannot be
> implemented in a manner that makes the *decisions on each individual
> species name **accessible**to people* unless there is an authoritative list
> provided.
> >
> > (1) For original spelling, that list is the complete list of original
> spellings of all 6 million available species names; no one can know for
> certain whether a published combination is using the original spelling
> unless they consult that list. This list is moderately difficult to
> compile, as it requires access to each and every piece of original
> literature. However, much of this work has been done, for many groups of
> organisms, under different BioCodes.
> >
> > (2) For prevailing usage, that list is a complete list of all species
> names, annotated as to whether they are in prevailing usage. Again, no one
> can know for certain whether a published combination is using the correct
> spelling unless they consult that list. This list is EXTREMELY difficult to
> compile, as it requires access not just to each and every piece of original
> literature, but *every single publication ever using a given name*. Worse,
> the definition of prevailing usage is EXTREMELY subjective, and the source
> of interminable disputes. Additionally, it's unclear how this model
> proposes to treat names not presently used as valid.
> >
> > (3) For an arbitrary date, that list if a complete list of all species
> names, annotated as to what spelling they had on date X. Again, no one can
> know for certain whether a published combination is using the correct
> spelling unless they consult that list. This list is harder to compile than
> the original spelling list, but much easier to compile than the "prevailing
> usage" list, *assuming* that a "dispute resolution" mechanism can be
> devised when a species has more than one spelling "in use" on date X; it
> does not completely eliminate subjectivity. It's also unclear how *this*
> model proposes to treat names not presently used as valid.
> >
> > Now, please let that sink in for a moment.
> >
> > All of these approaches mandate that we compile a complete list of every
> species name, ONE BY ONE. If there are 5000 names published as "striatus",
> and another 5000 published as "striata", and another 500 "striatum", they
> must all appear individually in the list.
> >
> > The model I have proposed is instead to provide (1) a single list of all
> genus-rank names and their genders, and (2) a list of names and suffixes
> that are adjectival and have to change spelling.
> >
> > This does not require a list of every species name ever published ONE BY
> ONE, nor access to ALL of the original literature.
> >
> > It requires, e.g., just a single entry for the name
> "striata/striatus/striatum", listing it as adjectival, instead of 10,500
> entries. *This model reduces the 6 million available species-rank names
> down to a few thousand genuinely adjectival names and suffixes*. No
> distinction needs to be drawn between valid species names versus invalid
> names. Anything that is unambiguously a noun doesn't even need to be listed
> at all.
> >
> > I cannot imagine why people think that this model is a worse alternative
> than the proposals to "fix" spellings in place forever. Those proposals are
> nightmares by comparison.
> >
> > Moreover, we already have an essentially complete list of all available
> genus-rank names (something around 500,000 ambiregnally), the overwhelming
> majority of which have either had their genders already clearly established
> by expert cataloguers, or for which the assignment of gender is mandated by
> the Code (at least, for the ICZN). There are online dictionaries that list
> essentially all known Latin and Greek adjectives.
> >
> > Compiling the two lists I propose is an *order of magnitude easier* than
> compiling any of the three different lists that would be required under the
> proposals to "fix" spellings, largely because there is only a small
> percentage of names - on the order of 10% of available names - for which
> the original descriptions would *need* to be consulted. Digging into 10% of
> the original literature is a lot easier than digging through 100%. Not just
> that, but if we on the ICZN Commission revise (or maybe even eliminate)
> Article 31.2.2 - where the ICZN allows different authors to determine that
> certain epithets can be an adjective for one author's species, and a noun
> for a different author's species - then we can potentially drop that
> percentage of original literature down to *zero*, for species names. Why
> should "agricola" be a noun in one case, and an adjective in another? The
> botanists don't allow this, so eliminating this would be a step towards
> Code harmonization, as well.
> >
> > Do you see now why I have been saying that keeping gender agreement is
> the best option we have? ONLY if we keep gender agreement can the problem
> facing us be reduced in scope to just the WORDS used to name species,
> rather than tracking *each individual nomenclatural act*, ONE BY ONE. This
> is, in plain fact, the most important advantage offered by gender agreement
> - you only need to apply a RULE to know how to spell a name, instead of
> having to research each individual name.
> >
> > Yes, under the status quo, where taxonomists have to do linguistic
> research, because they either don't know the rule, or how it applies to a
> certain case, it's painful and wasteful - but my model proposes to take all
> the linguistic research AWAY from individual taxonomists, and apply it
> exactly ONE time (for each name among the 10% of names that require it), in
> the process of compiling those two lists. Looking a name up in the lists
> would be the only "research" any taxonomist would need to do from that day
> forward.
