Hi Jose,

My comment about it being a massive waste of effort was about the gender
agreement part of the list. Someone, Doug it seems, needs to spend years
determining the genders of all the genus-group names - which could IMHO
have been better spent on doing other things. Global catalogues of names
are (of course) of vital importance and I am pleased to say that I have
been doing my bit! Not only did I create the Cockroach Species File
(synonymic catalogue of all 'true'' cockroaches - unfinished of course),
but I also created the Global Lepidoptera Names Index (LepIndex), which is
the nearest thing there is to a global list of Lepidoptera names (I hasten
to add that masses of work is still required by someone to turn LepIndex
into a synonymic catalogue and sort out all the errors).

Cheers,

George

> Dear Tony,

> Thanks a lot for your comments about my message. Indeed what I was trying
> to bring to the attention of the group was not necessarily the gender
> agreement topic (which has been discussed extensively) but rather the angle
> of creating master checklists for different taxa. This would not only
> render the gender agreement topic irrelevant (as Doug has already mentioned
> in his latest emails) but also, and most importantly, it would provide
> other benefits that go beyond dealing with gender agreements, such as
> having a single list with all names (synonyms, homonyms, etc.), accounting
> for all species of a taxon, species distribution, etc. In that sense it
> would not be "a monumental waste of time and effort" as George considered
> it would be in his reply to my email.
>
> But indeed what I wrote about has been done before and Tony points out many
> good examples of that (and certainly more could be mentioned). I emphasized
> doing so from a bottom to top approach instead of the (I think most common)
> top to bottom way, as perhaps that would be more practical.
>
> Tony wrote: "The coordination of activities in that respect would appear to
> me to be the area that would benefit most from additional work that is not
> presently being undertaken". And I totally agree with his assessment.
> Again, perhaps because I am too naive (or perhaps I am too prone to embark
> into monumental waste of time and effort enterprises? ;-)  I was
> considering the possibility of mobilizing the wider community to work
> together in their respective taxon groups and produce those lists. The only
> "novelty" I could claim from my previous email is to make those lists as
> published paper (or papers) first, so that the authors implied in those
> efforts get the recognition they deserve (and are also encouraged to work
> towards those goals). And the idea of encouraging scientific societies to
> somehow organize the work to be done... instead of relying on the admirable
> enthusiasm and hard work of a few fellows.
>

> > - the European Register of Marine Species or ERMS - a coordinated

> > publication
> >
> > - its electronic/web based successor, the World Register of Marine
> Species,
> > again a (semi) coordinated activity to which several hundred experts now
> > contribute, sometimes with sub-Registers for "branding" purposes such as

> >
> > - individual (or small group) ongoing databasing and/or publishing
> efforts
> > such as Eschmeyer's Catalog of Fishes, the world Diptera catalog "Systema
> > Dipterorum", similar for Fungi, Algae, and other groups; many then
> collated
> > in / feeding through to the "Catalogue of Life"
> >
> > - contributions to scholarly journals, a particular, self-identifying
> > series being that entitled "All genera of the world" in the journal
> > Megataxa, initiated by Zhi-Qiang Zhang from New Zealand
> >
> > - contributions (probably much less coordinated) by numerous individuals
> to
> > the online "The Paleobiology Database" for fossil taxa.
> >
> > From my point of view (as a top-down viewer in this instance) the main
> > drawback of these numerous and certainly most valuable and praiseworthy
> > activities is the lack of a facility to determine what is missing /
> > generate any idea of completeness, i.e. a "master template" in order that
> > areas that are outdated, or missing altogether, can be identified and
> > hopefully, filled. The coordination of activities in that respect would
> > appear to me to be the area that would benefit most from additional work
> > that is not presently being undertaken.
> >
> > For that one would need as a starting point at least a master list of
> > families of the kind that was produced some decades ago now (again by
> > co-opting relevant experts in every field) resulting in the 1982
> > publication of the Sybil Parker edited 2-volume compendium "Synopsis and
> > Classification of Living Organisms", and the 1993 Mike Benton-edited "The
> > Fossil Record 2", both landmarks in the field. More recently, Mike
> Ruggiero
> > did prepare a 2014 listing "Families of Living Organisms (FALO)" which is
> > indeed useful, but probably requires an update already.
> >
> > Operations like the Encyclopedia of Life, Catalogue of Life, GBIF, etc.
> > already produces "integrated classifications" (=backbone taxonomies or
> > hierarchies) of this sort but are of course secondary users of such
> > information as is produced in the first instance by relevant taxonomic
> > specialists.
