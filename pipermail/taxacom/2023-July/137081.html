Personally, I think we absolutely *need* option 1 that Doug suggested—a
database of all species- and genus-group names ever coined—*irrespective* of
whether or not we use it for gender agreement or anything else. When I was
first starting out as a taxonomist, I was *shocked* that such a list didn't
yet exist. How can we have let it get away from us so quickly? 6 million
names isn't that many, in the grand scheme. Such a list is absolutely
critical to our field. It would be both an invaluable resource, and a vital
documentation of the history of the field. Its absence contributes to the
problem of homonymy, both at genus and species level.
As you and others have pointed out, we have already come a considerable way
in the production of such lists for various taxa (some of which are
aggregated in a few places like CoL and ITIS), and I think it is undeniably
a goal that we should be striving towards as a community.

I see your point about a system that would track all possible names, and
automatically yield correct formulations if the gender of the genus is
know. But I have major concerns about how realistic that is. So far, I have
been badly underwhelmed by the performance of available resource on the
internet for doing this or anything like it. In fact, I often use German
websites for help with Latin, because the English ones with which I am
familiar are rubbish (please let me know if you have good suggestions!).
People will get creative with compound words or prefixes, and then it will
become just part, and not the whole word, that you have to decline.  I
might not want to use a combination someone has used before, but extend it
to form a unique name. A system would have to be robust indeed to handle
the messes that are on the horizon, as new generations, further and further
removed from Latin education, enter and proliferate in this field.

Cheers,
Mark
