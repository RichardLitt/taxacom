I'm not sure I see anything in the Code that precludes use of simple English for names. Sure, use of the names of people as nouns in apposition is recommended against, due to the potential confusion with authorship, but where does it say it is not possible to use simple English terms? It seems to be only a widely accepted convention. We've got New Guinea Tok Pisin names being created, from a language with English roots (in herpetology, the species Carlia ailanpalai, and the genus Palaia, and we came close (nomen nudum due to the untimely death of the author and a manuscript that went through after his death) to having Liklikpalaia, all based on Tok Pisin lizard, with variously island (ailan) and small (liklik) being added), so I don't see much of a step to having "blackhead" or "redwinged" (or even "thicc"!) being used as specific epithets, which would avoid any need for gender agreement. Not sure I support it, but I can't see the Code preventing it.

But if we're at the stage of having taxonomists not knowing Latin (I can understand that very few taxonomists have an training in Ancient Greek), yet with Latin names (or Latinised/transliterated Greek names) being preferred by the Code, presumably modern taxonomists are having to go to Latin dictionaries (either hard copy or online) to come up with the vast majority of names being coined annually. Those same sources will tell you whether the name is a noun or adjective. Many of the hard copy Latin dictionaries come with a short Latin grammar at the front. So not sure I see that there is any great step involved in completing the process in Latin for adjectival names to have gender agreement, either for those taxonomists creating new names, or for those taxonomists who need to change generic combinations. The main pity is that there is no requirement in the Code for an etymology for the name to be provided, including such a statement.

And for those who argue that suffixes are not important, I remind you of the unfortunate situation where the two endemic frogs on Fiji bear the somewhat confusing names Cornufer (or Platymantis) vitianus and C. (or P.) vitiensis.

Scott, nobody said it was the rewuired language for nomenclature. I said that the system is based on Latin or latinised Greek, which is true. There are caveats, of course. Names can be based on non Latin or Greek nouns, but not adjectives. For example, if there was a place called Bighead, then a species epithet could be bigheadensis. Maybe it could be used as a noun in apposition, to get the species epithet bighead, but this would not be recommended. However, if a species has a big head, then you can't use the species epithet bighead, you must use something like megacephala (or megacephalum or megacephalus, depending on the gender of the combined genus epithet).Stephen
