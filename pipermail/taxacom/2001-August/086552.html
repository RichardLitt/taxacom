Sehr geehrter Herr Dr. Lorenz,dear TAXACOMembers,following the zoological Code, we must make a clear distinctionbetween those cases in which, according to each relevant Article,"prevailing usage" is to be considered, and those - such as mostcases of incorrect or variant spellings - for which there areprovisions without regard to "prevailing usage". (In my opinionwe are very fortunate that the latter concept has not (yet) beendeclared the guiding principle of nomenclature. I would be gladto supply anybody interested with arguments for this opinion,e.g. see further below, but that is not the topic of Dr. Lorenz'inquiry.)There is nothing in the Code's fourth edition that justifies theraising of catalogs, checklists, etc. to a kind of super-publicationswith higher authority than others. On the contrary, there actuallyis evidence for the opposite: for example, see Article 23.9.in which the occurrence of a name in a "nomenclator or indexor list of names" (23.9.6) is declared irrelevant for determinationsof solutions in cases of homonymy.May be, yet another definition is needed here, distinguishingbetween "real catalogs" and "mere nomenclators"? I don't thinkso. Even the best-made catalogs can be little better than theprimary publications they are compiling data from. Authorsof midsized to large catalogs usually do not have or take the timeto verify a high proportion of the individual data processed.And the handling of large quantities of data always results inadditional errors that were not yet in the primary literature.I know, because I've written such a catalog myself, and I don'tthink a species should be considered out of use because, foranother example from my own work, a simple lapsus caused itto be omitted from the only catalog available on the faunathe taxon is a part of.Instead, the real issue we need to pay attention to is whetheror not names and publications (including catalogs) are the resultof work that can be called scientific. For this I'd propose twocriteria probably still agreed on by everybody as essentials ofthe scientific method:1) Whether an author tells us the necessary definitions under whichresults were obtained, and whether we find that within thesedefinitions other people can and will reproduce the same resultas the first author.2) Whether the definitions under which different results wereobtained together form a coherent system, and not an illogical,more or less random conglomerate.Experience indicates - and I'm afraid most taxonomist colleagueswill have to agree - that we are still quite far away from ataxonomy and nomenclature where the above two (ratherminimal) requirements are thoroughly and unequivocally met.Until then, I would hope that less colleagues would interpret"stability" of nomenclature so rigidly that they even begin tosell out the tried and tested type concept for things like"prevailing usage" which is not and can never be as clearlydefined, and therefore causes every serious taxonomistenormous and unnecessary amounts of extra work.Of course, in- and outside of the Code we should have provisionsagainst meaningless or frivolous alterations of names. But on theother hand we must allow those changes representing advancementsin scientific knowledge. For example, if there is clear evidencethat the current (!) "prevailing usage" of a name is based onobvious past mistakes - be they methodical in nature or theresult of personal motives of earlier authors (yes, that happened;and no, it is not as rare as we would like) - then taxonomy andnomenclature as scientific disciplines should follow the provenfacts and not a past majority of voices that may have beenwell-meaning but definitely was ill-informed.After all: every other scientific discipline lives with (if not for!)the changes resulting from progress in its knowledge.Why not taxonomy, and nomenclature within it?Thank you very much for your attention.Sincerely,--Martin SpiesSchraemelstr. 151D-81247 MuenchenGermanyTel.
