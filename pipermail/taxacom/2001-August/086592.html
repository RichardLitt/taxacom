>This raises the question how different two distribution patterns must be
>to be distinguishable by such different means of dispersal. Given the
>choice between a butterfly and a freshwater group, would one be safe in
>venturing that a pattern ranging from New Guinea to Samoa is more likely
>to be the butterfly and a pattern restricted to Borneo more likely the
>fish? I do not ask for certainty - simply for a rational reason to make a
>choice.

One might make such assertions but that would be on the basis of other
preconceptions about the origin and evolution of particular areas. The
point about this example is that Mayr said that poor dispersing primary
freshwater fish have totally different distribution patterns from
butterflies. I emphasize the word 'totally'. The examples given were
presented as a fish and butterfly group with similar distribution ranges
with some local differences. There is no objective criterion to predict
which range is necessarily the fish or butterfly. One may claim that the
range in the Pacific is "more likely" to be a butterfly based on some
preconceived position, but that's all it would be. The same goes for the
more restricted distribution of one group than the other in the Middle
East. Means of dispersal does not provide a predicable relationship with
distribution range or pattern.
