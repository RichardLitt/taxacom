Stephen Thorpe wrote:

>>But...what if the hybrid populations continue to persist?  Are they a 
>>third species? Or no species at all?
> That wasn't your original question! I'm not sure that there is an answer 
> to that, other than just "they are hybrids of extinct species".  I would 
> lean to the third species option, since some of the species we recognise 
> today could have been formed as hybrids with subsequent extinction of 
> parents. Different issue, though ...
>

Well the hybrid species are recognised as distinct species if they manage to 
persist and retain their distinct characteristics. There have been classical 
cases of Tragopogon mirus, Senecio cambrensis, and even our Triticum 
aestivum. I has described Tragopogon kashmirianus in 1976 with some 
reservation as tetraploid hybrid between two diploid species of the genus. 
Molecular studies after 30 years of its recognition, have established its 
identity (paper presented in August 2006 by Mavrodiev et al., at Conference 
of Botanical Society of America, based on Molecular studies on Tragopogon 
kashmirianus.)
