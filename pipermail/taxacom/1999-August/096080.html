It was not my intention to be so summary in my dismissal so I will try to
elaborate. I agree that the examples below do provide instances of
evolutionary theory being applied to particular patterns of biology,
but what I am interested to know is how one would describe this
application in methodological terms. Does one study endosymbiosis in
a particular way if one is an evolutionist? None of the studies themselves
require an evolutionary framework as I understand them.

An off-the-top-of-my-head list of biological studies that *require* an
evolutionary framework:

* endosymbiosis (why did God create chloroplasts and mitochondria with
bacterial genes?)

Even if a creationist would not answer that question, would a creationist
be unable to recongize endosymbiosis? My feeling is that a creationist would.

* biogeography (the distribution of each *created* species must be studied
individually; in the case of vicariance, it *must* be established whether
the vicariants are separate species or the same)

I would have thought a creationist would have no problem comparing
the distribution of species. There have been creationist biogeographers
and they did have biogeographic explanations for distributions.

* genetic engineering, especially transgenics (No a priori basis to assume
that genes artificially transfered between species will behave in the same
way as those transfered within)

I am less a geneticist, so I would like further elaboration. Are you saying that
a creationist could not be a genetic enginner?

* animal models in biomedicine (who among creationists would have ever
expected that chimps and only chimps could be infected with HIV?)

They might have if chimps and humans were seen to share a created
design plan and so have other features in common.
