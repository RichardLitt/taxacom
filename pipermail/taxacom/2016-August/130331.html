Hi Donald,
Thanks for the comments. I think you might agree that the opening para in this summary/report (http://www.iucnredlist.org/news/paper-reveals-nature-conservation-knowledge-is-a-bargain) of the published paper quite misleadingly and improperly claims that [quote]A new study released today finds that the knowledge databases which underpin nature conservation and environmental decision-making – such as the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species™ - cost US$ 6.5 million annually to manage, plus the dedication of thousands of volunteers[unquote]. The paper, as you correctly note, "explicitly gives these cost estimates as those for four databases: IUCN Red List of Threatened Species, the IUCN Red List of Ecosystems, Protected Planet, and the World Database of Key Biodiversity Areas." Surely these 4 cannot be the totality of knowledge databases which "underpin nature conservation and environmental decision-making"? Far from it, I would have thought! However, I think it is fair enough not to include costs of "the associated work of field research, taxonomy, etc. which underlies the work of the expert groups adding entries to these datasets". The relevant cost is not the cost of knowledge generation, but rather the cost of knowledge aggregation into useful database systems. But even then, the quoted costs must be way to low. Surely "nature conservation and environmental decision-making" should take into account all species and the results of all published taxonomy and other information about all species? Such publications are added to every single day, and no existing database has yet achieved a way of keeping the totality of this evolving knowledge updated. In fact, no existing database yet has a comprehensive checklist of all known species names! The costs associated with that pipe dream must be truly astronomical, don't you think? For example, the journal Zootaxa publishes up to 10 papers 5 days a week. Although they preregister all papers on ZooBank, they do not have the resources to register all the new taxa, let alone other taxonomic changes in those papers. Even just trying to keep up with that would be a herculean task!
Cheers,
Stephen

 The paper explicitly
 gives these cost estimates as those for four databases: IUCN
 Red List of Threatened Species, the IUCN Red List of
 Ecosystems, Protected Planet, and the World Database of Key
 Biodiversity Areas.  The estimates do not seem to touch on
 the associated work of field research, taxonomy, etc. which
 underlies the work of the expert groups adding entries to
 these datasets.
 
 The
 comparison of these expert knowledge datasets with the
 Global Observing System for Climate is far from perfect. 
 That is a globally interconnected network of systems feeding
 a wide-range of continuous data from very large numbers of
 sensors into a complex data management and modelling
 framework.  For conservation databases to carry out a
 similar costing would probably require inclusion of the
 associated field work costs.
 
 In the case of GBIF, we've been in
 existence for 15 years and run with a core budget of around
 €3 million per year - this covers not only the informatics
 and standards work, but also capacity enhancement activity
 around the world.  At national and institutional levels, a
 vast amount more (I'd estimate another order of
 magnitude at least) is spent on staff time collecting or
 entering data, curating and sharing databases, etc. All of
 this of course then really depends on the much, much larger
 investment over many decades in studying organisms,
 collecting specimens and observations, etc.  
 
 I see activities like GBIF in
 part as a relatively small additional effort over and above
 the work of researchers to try to ensure that the data they
 collect remains discoverable and useful into the future and
 that it can grow in value through combination with other
 data.
 
 Donald
