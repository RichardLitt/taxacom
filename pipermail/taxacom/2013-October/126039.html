On the data quality issue: Yes, dumping an entire museum's label data into
a database will generate a huge mix of qualities from complete
misidentifications / incomplete identifications to totally confident IDs
etc. I still see value in digitizing but I think as a community we are
missing out on a great opportunity:

GenBank is were it is today because people are usually expected / required
to submit sequences as part of the publishing process.

The natural analogy in taxonomy with specimen data is to expect/ require
specimen data to be submitted to some database that serves to GBIF as part
of the publication process. Accession #s of the submission would be in the
publication just like we do with GenBank #s.

This would capture the highest quality specimen data and spread the effort
much wider (rather than concentrate it mostly with museums). Usually a
revision involves the study of specimens from many different museums, often
museums that have no digitization programs... these data are in most cases
being used by the taxonomist for the revision and then published but never
made digitally available to GBIF / the wider user group.

ZooKeys is one journal moving in this direction although I think one can
still publish in ZooKeys without submitting data in this manner (it is
recommended but not required).  One step we can all make as a community is
when reviewing manuscripts and grant proposals we can put pressure on the
authors/PIs to submit their specimen data to a database that serve GBIF.
Some brave journal may someday actually make it a requirement.

-Derek


On Fri, Oct 18, 2013 at 6:51 AM, David Campbell <pleuronaia at gmail.com>wrote:

> Projects such as GBIF indeed have great potential, but at present the data
> quality is so uneven that it is often positively misleading.  A user can
> readily be impressed with boasts about how many entries are in a database
> and perform all sorts of grand metanalyses without realizing that much of
> the data is unreliable, often enough to make the analyses untrustworthy.
> The biggest problems seem to be the lack of support for doing the taxonomic
> groundwork that is necessary to provide reliable data, lack of metrics for
> data reliability, and failure to design the databases to allow for common
> taxonomic problems (homonyms, misidentifications, incorrect spellings or
> emendations, etc.)  Does GBIF itself need to fund taxonomy?  Probably
> having some taxonomic experts on staff checking things would be advisable,
> but it's not unreasonable to promote other programs as the primary funding
> source for taxonomy.  Too often, however, databases have generated
> resentment by "we've spent millions developing this system so that you can
> volunteer to generate and enter data".
