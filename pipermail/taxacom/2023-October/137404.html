>  Francisco,
> You seem to be making the distinction between diagnosis and description in
> a very nonstandard way! I think you may be confusing a description with
> what I would call a treatment. Both are longer than a diagnosis. A
> treatment is a diagnosis and/or description, plus some nomenclatural
> details (such as the name of the taxon, type data, etc.)
> A diagnosis allows the reader to distinguish the species from all other
> *known* species. Suppose the species is the only one with two heads. The
> diagnosis need only mention the two heads. However, down the track, another
> new species might be discovered which also has two heads, but other
> differences. Hence the previous diagnosis fails to distinguish between the
> two two-headed species. That is why we need a longer description, just in
> case another new species is discovered later which also agrees with the
> diagnosis.
> This is all pretty standard stuff, from "Taxonomy 101", I would have
> thought!
>
> Cheers, Stephen

>  Dear Art,
> It is necessary to distinguish between taxonomy and nomenclature. The
> terms "diagnosis" and "description" have different meanings in the two
> disciplines.
>
> In taxonomy, these are headlines of chapters or paragraphs, but
> apparently the two terms are not used in the same sense in all animal
> groups. This is a question of style. In molluscs a diagnosis is a short
> text, while the description is a long text. In other animal groups this
> may be different.
>
> The Code cannot consider this. The Rheindt et al. paper employed the
> terms for nomenclatural purposes. We have been asking ourselves which
> should be the requirements for the Code. A species diagnosis in this
> sense is one that satisfies the provisions of the Code. A species
> description is a successful proposal of a new name in combination with
> some information that is required for making the name available.
>
> Taxonomists can employ the terms "description" and "diagnosis" in their
> taxonomic studies following the traditions in their animal groups. It is
> no problem to continue in the same frame in the future.
>
> The Code can only require the presence of a text with a certain content,
> and cannot regulate the length and style in which the information is
> presented. In this case, at least one character state to distinguish a
> taxon from a closely related taxon needs to be given. The Code cannot
> regulate in which style this should be satisfied, and cannot regulate
> that a short and a long text need to be provided. This is all up to the
> authors. The Code only requires a minimum frame.
>

> Am 12.10.2023 um 20:23 schrieb Art Borkent via Taxacom:
> >     I am responding to the recent paper by Rheindt et al on diagnoses
> and proposals for changes to the ICZN.  These are my thoughts:
> >
> >      It is certainly an excellent idea to tighten the definition and
> presentation of diagnoses, as I argued in my 2021 paper “Diagnosing
> diagnoses – can we improve our taxonomy?” in ZooKeys 1071: 43–48. However,
> I think the article “Tightening the requirements for species diagnoses….”
> could be strengthened.
> >
> > It could be made more clear that a diagnosis be separate from a
> description. Descriptions of new species are often lengthy and should
> indeed provide clear and thorough evidence that the newly described species
> is distinctive from all other species; it should include all the evidence
> the species is distinct. Diagnoses should also provide evidence of a
> distinct taxon but be minimalist, a confirmation of an identification by
> others using the work. I am unsure whether this should be a rule or a
> recommendation, but in large measure, those who use our taxonomic work
> generally desire to have a confirmation of an identification after using a
> key (which are often challenging). For those of us who are unfamiliar with
> another group (which includes for me, nearly all the other groups other
> than my own), a direct confirmation by the author(s) would be comforting.
> Failing that (the vast majority of the time), a simple statement “This is
> the only species of genus x with … (a feature or a minimum set of
> features)” would be extremely helpful.  It should provide the answer for
> the reader “Yes, I have correctly identified it or No, I need to go back to
> the key”. This provides the reassurance of correct identification. As the
> situation stands now, most diagnoses (in Diptera at least) are too lengthy
> and ultimately unclear whether all the features need to be checked or not
> (mostly not). I would like to see the ICZN make either a rule about a
> simple, succinct presentation of diagnoses or at least a strong
> recommendation.
> >
> > And this brings up another point. Who is in “our community”? The article
> by Rheindt et al. suggests our cliental are “biologists”. For most
> taxonomists, however, their writing clearly reflects an assumption that the
> reader is intimately familiar with the group.  Especially in a time where
> taxonomy is often seen as irrelevant, authors should be encouraged to
> present diagnoses that can be used by a much broader community of
> scientists and, if we really want to expand the usefulness of our work, of
> interested amateurs.
> >
> > In summary:
> >
> > 1. Make a strong distinction between descriptions and diagnoses.
> >
> > 2. Diagnosis should be simple, short, succinct (see Borkent, 2021).
> >
> > 3. Remind users of ICZN that our community should include even amateurs.
> >
> >  A final, non-related point: the article notes that bar-coded diagnoses
> might state “.. new species differ from all other species of the genus by
> two synapomorphies in the CO1 gene:”  In reality, virtually no presenters
> of barcodes discuss synapomorphies and this would be a new arena for many,
> including developing some skills in cladistic thought and analysis.  That
> would be an excellent idea.
