 Rich, getting my first rant of 2023 out of the way (Clearly not rankless or rantless, this classification stuff!), I think that your example of Homo sapiens is a bad one, for the reason that it is extremely atypical in that it has the ultimate stability for any taxonomic name. All would be well if such stability was widespread in taxonomy, but, of course, it isn't, so we have the instability of uncertain and often changing and/or disputed generic placements wreaking havoc with too many names. Therein lies the problem.Stephen
    On Sunday, 1 January 2023 at 12:14:44 pm NZDT, Weakley, Alan <weakley at bio.unc.edu> wrote:  
 
 Thank you, Rich.? I was going to write something of the sort, but you said it better than I would have.

For all the complaining about scientific names, and that the names change when we split or lump genera (because the genus name is part of the species name), it turns out people (users of the taxonomy) actually quite like that aspect of the names that indicates relationship at genus rank.? It's not a bug, it's a feature. The non-taxonomist users of taxonomy (I'm mainly a vascular plant person, so, in my world, conservation NGOs, gardeners, reserve managers, citizen scientists, conservationists) don't want serial numbers, or rankless taxonomy, or to have to know phylogenetic trees.? 

Replacing "Genus specific-epithet" (for all its complexities, as discussed by Rich) with "epithet + author + year" would be horribly unpopular with the users of taxonomy who are not also taxonomists. Fussy to remember, not user-friendly, replacing desired information about relationship with an irrelevant mini-bibliographic reference of no use to them, and further emphasizing the white European dominant history of biodiversity naming.? It'd just drive users of species taxonomy further towards throwing out scientific names for hopelessly imprecise common names.

One of the strengths and weaknesses of the Linnean system of binominal nomenclature is that it conflates classification with nomenclature (and to some degree, circumscription).? To clarify what I mean by this:

Nomenclature: the literal text string used to label or represent a semi-defined set of organisms that we aggregate as a "taxon".
Classification: the hierarchical arrangement of taxa as nested sets of other taxa (which, ultimately, means nested sets of organisms)
Circumscription: the actual nested set of explicitly or implicitly included organisms within a purported taxon.

Over the past two years, the late David Remsen, Nicolas Bailly and I have been brain-storming ways to model taxonomic information, and we realized through this effort both the blessing and the curse of how these three axes of biological information are conflated within the Linnean system.

The blessing is that a name like "Homo sapiens" is both a widely-recognized label (nomenclature) used to represent a well-understood circumscription (all humans on Earth, going back to some point in evolutionary history), and includes within it a classification (i.e., that the set of humans assigned the species epithet "sapiens" are in some way closely associated with organisms labeled with other species epithets combined with the genus Homo, such as "neanderthalensis").? Thus, with a simple, memorable text-string label, we can communicate both classification and circumscription at the same time.

The curse is that by conflating these things, we add much more complexity and, to some extent, ambiguity to our information exchange.? This is important because many people (myself included) see the assertion of sets of organisms comprising "taxa" and labelled with standardized names (indeed, the entire PURPOSE of taxonomy) as a mechanism to facilitate better communication among humans (biologists, policymakers, resource managers, enthusiasts, etc.). By overloading a single term (e.g., "Homo sapiens") with both explicit classification and implicit circumscription, the stability/persistence of the label is at the mercy of classifications and circumscriptions du jour.

In any case, while it is true that a binominal like "Homo sapiens" could function as a de-facto rank-less label to represent sets of organisms, this only works if the implied classification aspect (i.e., that organisms associated with the label "Homo sapiens" have any greater affinity with organisms associated with the label "Homo neanderthalensis", than they do with, say, organisms associated with the label "Pan troglodytes" -- or, for that matter, organisms associated with the label "Cocos nucifera") is abandoned.? Intuitively, I think most people would have a hard time abandoning this implied classification embedded within nomenclature, so while I certainly agree that one "could" do it that way, I'm not so sure they "should".
