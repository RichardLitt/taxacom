Dear Francisco,

Thank you so much for this detailed argument! Some comments:

1. The "syntype slide" labels probably do not actually contain the 
information "leg. Denny", this is likely an assumption of Najer et al. 
(2020), and a requirement (I believe) of the journal. I have worked with 
other slides from the same collection, and they do not have "leg. Denny" 
written on them, although I have never seen this specific slide in real 
life. At the moment it seems impossible to download the digitized slides 
from the NHML, but the louse-information of the slide is visible, and 
states only: "Philopterus reguli Denny (1842) Type ?" in ink, with a 
question mark added in pencil. It is visible here: 
https://nam10.safelinks.protection.outlook.com/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fdata.nhm.ac.uk%2Fdataset%2F56e711e6-c847-4f99-915a-6894bb5c5dea%2Fresource%2F05ff2255-c38a-40c9-b657-4ccb55ab2feb%3Fq%3Dphilopterus%2Breguli%26view_id%3D6ba121d1-da26-4ee1-81fa-7da11e68f68e%26filters%3Dproject%253ASlide%2BProject%2B-%2BLouse%257CcollectionCode%253Abmnh%2528e%2529%26field%3DassociatedMediaCount%26value%3D&amp;data=05%7C01%7Ctaxacom%40lists.ku.edu%7C365544b058174a92a8c108dadcdafb71%7C3c176536afe643f5b96636feabbe3c1a%7C0%7C0%7C638065130334517929%7CUnknown%7CTWFpbGZsb3d8eyJWIjoiMC4wLjAwMDAiLCJQIjoiV2luMzIiLCJBTiI6Ik1haWwiLCJXVCI6Mn0%3D%7C3000%7C%7C%7C&amp;sdata=hGx12r%2FNx8vVs7bkvWoD2dbineLqJzPqyHf8o28EFnM%3D&amp;reserved=0

certainly has not made it easy to understand what he's talking about.

the specimens in the NHML collection are types or not, just list how 
many are present. Under Philopterus reguli, he states that 1 ?" is 
present at the NHML. Why Mey would drag up that this list is incomplete, 
when it appears that the syntype specimen in question is clearly listed 
in the table is unclear to me. Thompson's list is available here:

Hopkins & Clay's checklist is available here: 
https://nam10.safelinks.protection.outlook.com/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fphthiraptera.myspecies.info%2Fcontent%2Fcheck-list-genera-species-mallophaga&amp;data=05%7C01%7Ctaxacom%40lists.ku.edu%7C365544b058174a92a8c108dadcdafb71%7C3c176536afe643f5b96636feabbe3c1a%7C0%7C0%7C638065130334674146%7CUnknown%7CTWFpbGZsb3d8eyJWIjoiMC4wLjAwMDAiLCJQIjoiV2luMzIiLCJBTiI6Ik1haWwiLCJXVCI6Mn0%3D%7C3000%7C%7C%7C&amp;sdata=yRbga3esiSL60IenCfCPo16b8H6nArRnoDtUyr1CXDw%3D&amp;reserved=0
- Finally, Gray (1852) stated in his catalogue of specimens at the NHML 
that "The British Museum possesses the type specimens described and 
figured by [Henry Denny]" (Part XI here: 
https://nam10.safelinks.protection.outlook.com/?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.biodiversitylibrary.org%2Fbibliography%2F1582&amp;data=05%7C01%7Ctaxacom%40lists.ku.edu%7C365544b058174a92a8c108dadcdafb71%7C3c176536afe643f5b96636feabbe3c1a%7C0%7C0%7C638065130334674146%7CUnknown%7CTWFpbGZsb3d8eyJWIjoiMC4wLjAwMDAiLCJQIjoiV2luMzIiLCJBTiI6Ik1haWwiLCJXVCI6Mn0%3D%7C3000%7C%7C%7C&amp;sdata=b%2BXwSkuh1WnbXfMLjZzSjhL8zdyoeGxFiz2emuCeWEA%3D&amp;reserved=0). He lists 
Philopterus reguli, but does not state how many specimens there were.

Does this not suggest that it is plausible that -- however many 
specimens Denny originally had -- the single female examined by Najer et 
al. (2020) may be the same as that listed by Gray (1852), Thompson 
(1937), and Hopkins & Clay (1952)? If so, does this not mean that there 
is sufficient chance that the preserved syntype is actually a syntype, 
and that 75.3.4 is not satisfied?

3. About 75.3.5 -- I think Mey is relying heavily of "other sources". He 
belongs to a school of thought in which host associations take 
precedence over louse morphology, host specificity is rigidly strict, 
and co-evolution of lice and their hosts is always the case. His 
neotype, for all I know, is consistent with the species that is normally 
found on this host (i.e. "Ph. gustafssoni"), which was previously 
redescribed and illustrated (as Ph. reguli) by one of Mey's teachers, 
Jadwiga Zlotorzycka, who never saw a reason to use louse morphology if a 
list of hosts could be used instead. Her redescriptions are all of "Ph. 
gustafssoni", but she never examined any of Denny's specimens, and just 
assumed identity based on host associations. She was one of those people 
whose every publication set taxonomic knowledge back rather than advance 
it...

4. The pronotal setae are difficult. They are not in the London specimen 
according to Najer et al. (2020), which Mey (2020) accepts. They are 
also not in Denny's Fig. 4. However, most lice have at least *some* 
setae in this area (usually 1 on each side), and these are *never* 
illustrated in any of Denny's figures, as far as I can see, even in 
cases where these should be much longer and thicker than the setae that 
are illustrated. Their absence in the illustration may therefore not 
mean more than that Denny consistently overlooked these setae in all his 
specimens, or that something in his preparation method meant they always 
broke off, or who knows what. Mey may not consider this significant 
since they are universally absent in Denny's figures, and thus not 
decisive.

Much else I have to think about more, but thanks a lot for all your 
work!

Cheers,
Daniel

>>>> Daniel:
>>>> 
>>>> As for your question on standards and possible limits for "common
>>>> usage", one of the underlying principles upon which the Code is 
>>>> based
>>>> is that "There is no "case law" in zoological nomenclature. Problems
>>>> in nomenclature are decided by applying the Code directly, and never
>>>> by reference to precedent. If the Commission is called on to make a
>>>> ruling on a particular case, the decision relates to that case 
>>>> alone".
>>>> 
>>>> As for the specific issue, the authors behind Set 1 base their
>>>> decision on the wrong assumption that the syntype specimen is the
>>>> holotype. This specimen is a syntype (if it was one of the original
>>>> specimens seen by the author in 1842), but the fact that it is the
>>>> only specimen recovered does not give it any priority over the other
>>>> (and potentially lost) specimens in the type series. Any specimen in
>>>> the type series is eligible for being designated as a lectotype, but
>>>> considering the syntype to be a holotype does not qualify as a
>>>> lectotype designation for designations made after 1999. Set 1
>>>> therefore does not solve the central issue of a possibly mixed type
>>>> series.
>>>> 
>>>> Set 2 rests on their arguments that what has been called a syntype
>>>> specimen is actually NOT part of the original set of specimens seen 
>>>> by
>>>> the author in 1842. If their arguments are accepted, their 
>>>> designation
>>>> of a neotype is valid (if fulfilling other conditions provided by 
>>>> the
>>>> Code), and the name proposed by the authors of Set 1 is a junior
>>>> synonym. [NB: neoparatypes are not recognized by the Code.]
>>>> 
>>>> A thorough assessment rests on whether or not the single syntype is
>>>> actually a syntype, and whether or not this specimen is in a 
>>>> condition
>>>> that allows for a reliable taxonomic decision.
>>>>

>>>> Dear colleagues,
>>>> 
>>>> I'm looking at a case where a parasite species was described about 
>>>> 180
>>>> years ago (1842) from what may be a mixture of species (amount
>>>> unknown), of which only a single syntype specimen remains.
>>>> Unfortunately, this syntype specimen appears to not be the same
>>>> species as has been reported from this host in the intervening 180
>>>> years. The original description is largely useless even to get to 
>>>> the
>>>> genus level, and the original illustration is poor, but shows at 
>>>> least
>>>> one potentially important character (setae in a certain area) which
>>>> are absent in the illustration *and* in the remaining specimen, but
>>>> present in the species that normally parasitizes this host. Two
>>>> authors in the 1960?1970s redescribed the species poorly, and this
>>>> redescription is of a different species than the syntype specimen,
>>>> which they did not examine.
>>>> 
>>>> In 2020, two sets of authors discussed this species (one set in
>>>> reaction to the other), and reached different conclusions:
>>>> 
>>>> Set 1 -- the syntype specimen (which they erroneously call the
>>>> 'holotype') is conspecific with the illustration and not 
>>>> contradicted
>>>> by the original description. Therefore, the name of this species can
>>>> follow the specimen, which thus is assumed to be from an unknown 
>>>> host.
>>>> The species of parasite that is normally found on this host thus 
>>>> needs
>>>> a new name, which they provide, including a description and
>>>> illustration and a review of the previous reports. They have visited
>>>> many of the museums where previously reported specimens are stored,
>>>> and invariably these are the same as the new species, and not the 
>>>> same
>>>> as the syntype specimen.
>>>> No further specimens conspecific with the syntype specimen have been
>>>> found, but the group is very poorly known so this is not a surprise.
>>>> These authors do not redescribe or illustrate the syntype specimen,
>>>> and appear to have made no efforts trying to compare this specimen
>>>> with other specimens in the same collection from the same collector
>>>> 180 years ago. The question of mix-up of specimens is thus open.
>>>> 
>>>> Set 2 -- it cannot be established beyond reasonable doubt that the
>>>> syntype specimen is actually part of the original set of specimens
>>>> seen by the author in 1842, and much of his collection is lost. The
>>>> collection has changed hands at least once since 1842, it is unknown
>>>> who slide-mounted the specimens, and at least two earlier authors
>>>> contradict each other concerning which specimens were included in 
>>>> the
>>>> original purchase. It is therefore possible that the rest of the
>>>> original type series included the species normally found on this 
>>>> host,
>>>> and that the syntype specimen is either a mix-up, a contamination, 
>>>> or
>>>> some other form of error. All other reports of parasites in this 
>>>> genus
>>>> from this host have used the 1842 name, and for the purposes of
>>>> stability, we should continue to use this name and treat the new 
>>>> name
>>>> established by Set 1 as a subjective junior synonym. To this end, 
>>>> Set
>>>> 2 erects a neotype and four neoparatypes, which correspond to the
>>>> parasite species normally found on this host, as well as the one
>>>> illustrated and described as a new species by Set 1. Set 2 keeps the
>>>> door open to applying to the Commission for an official ruling if 
>>>> this
>>>> neotypification meets well-founded resistance.
>>>> 
>>>> In the ~180 years between the original description and the revision,
>>>> there appear to be about 25?30 publications that use the old name 
>>>> for
>>>> this species. However, all except two of these are either just
>>>> inclusions in lists of members of a given genus, with no further
>>>> information added, or regional faunal lists where identity of the
>>>> specimens has been assumed based on host associations. Until 2020,
>>>> there has been no way to identify the original species either from
>>>> descriptions or illustrations, and as Set 1 also do not describe the
>>>> syntype specimen, this is still true.
>>>> 
>>>> Without going into which set of authors is correct (although I'd 
>>>> value
>>>> input on this as well), I'm wondering about the process of setting
>>>> aside type specimens that are suddenly found not to represent the
>>>> common understanding of the species name. Where, if anywhere, is the
>>>> limit for "common usage" the stability of which could be threatened 
>>>> if
>>>> the names are changed? I've seen various case reports and opinions 
>>>> in
>>>> the Bulletin, where often authors will list a number of publications
>>>> within a certain framework to show that the name is in use, but is
>>>> there any official standard for this?
>>>> 
>>>> Further, if there is an accepted official standard, is this affected
>>>> by the circumstances of usage? Apart from faunal lists where 
>>>> identity
>>>> is assumed based on host associations, there is no information of 
>>>> any
>>>> kind attached to this name between 1842 and the present. The 2020
>>>> authors also do not attach any new information beyond that the 
>>>> syntype
>>>> is not conspecific with the species normally found on the host.
>>>> 
>>>> It would seem to me that this kind of usage would "count" towards 
>>>> any
>>>> kind of standard for the number of publications a name needs to 
>>>> appear
>>>> in to be commonly accepted. Is just listing a name without making 
>>>> any
>>>> effort to establish whether you're applying the name to the correct
>>>> taxon sufficient to assert that the name is in common, universal
>>>> usage?
>>>> Seems to me this would be rewarding laziness.
>>>> 
>>>> I'd value any input on this.
>>>>

>>>> Daniel
