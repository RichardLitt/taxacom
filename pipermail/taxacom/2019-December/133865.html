Hi Stephen,

 

First, there is more information behind the scenes than what is visible on the website.  For example, we log every change in the database, including who created a record and when, and also whenever any fields change (including a log of who changed, it, when, and what the value was prior to the change). This is slightly different from the date shown on the web page as the date of registration.  In most cases, this is only a few milliseconds after the record was created, but in some cases a record may already exist and gets registered later.

 

Anyway, as to your question:  The dates/times shown for the registration (i.e., the mouse-hover on the orange LSID), and the date shown for when an archive was added, are embedded in the system and cannot be altered.  The date shown as the date of publication is entered by whoever created the record (or whoever last edited that value).  So yes, we rely entirely on the integrity of the publisher or author (or whomever entered the publication date for the record) to get it right.  There is absolutely no way for ZooBank to verify the accuracy of that date – any more than one can rely on the date stated in a paper-published work. The same rules for determining correct date apply for both paper and electronic works.  The date of publication as indicated in ZooBank carries absolutely no authoritative meaning in the sense of the Code.  So yes, someone could back-date the publication – especially if self-published – which is the same problem we already have for self-published paper works.

 

Again, sounding like a broken record, I imagine a better system, wherein such critical dates were locked in algorithmically/objectively within the registration system, rather than relying on interpretations of people who enter values in the records – which are at the very least subjective, or worse nefarious.

 

One thing we could do, however, is look for cases of electronic publications where registration (noneditable) happened after stated date of publication.  That at least sets a limit on back-dating.

 

Aloha,

Rich

Just out of curiosity Rich, can you comment on the following issue?

For an e-only publication, we can know the actual date of ZooBank registration (well, subject to some possible caveats!), and we can know the *stated* date of publication, but we rely on the integrity of the publisher for the actual date of publication. There is no way to independently verify the actual date of (online) publication. This could be a problem if the author is one and the same as the publisher, etc. They could deliberately backdate the stated publication date in order to gain priority over a rival.

Hello Stephen,

Thank you for your reply. Please note that "ZooBank itself will record the date of registration as 04.01.2020" but we will not be able to see it. We will only see the content of the "Date Published" field, which is manually entered and does not reflect the date in which the record was created. Unless that ZooBank is further developed, we cannot see who created a record and on which date. I will explain below how ZooBank can be hacked.

Meanwhile, imagine that two papers on the same genus come out in very close dates. The first paper, the one from my previous example, would be believed to be validly published on 01.01.2020, even if it was registered in ZooBank on 04.01.2020 and the pdf edited and republished on that date. A second paper, dealing with the same genus and new species comes out on 03.01.2020, being validly registered in ZooBank in advance (prospective registration). What will the community see? The community will see the pdf of paper 1 displaying the date of 01.01.2020 (because it was not edited, just like in Meierotto et al. 2019) and they will see the same information in ZooBank, 01.01.2020, because the person that wrote the entry in 04.01.2020 would have chosen 01.01.2020 manually in ZooBank. The community will also see the pdf of paper 2 displaying the date of 03.01.2020 and they will see the same information in ZooBank, 03.01.2020. Someone having to decide on the priority of the genus and/or the species, will deem the republished, later registered paper 1 as the one holding priority for three days over paper 2, when in fact it is paper 2 the one that has priority over paper 1 for one day. The result: The rightful author of paper 2 will be deprived of priority over his names and the true senior names will be put in synonymy with the junior names. 

How to hack ZooBank? It is possible to hack priority in ZooBank simply by selecting "This work has already been published" and manually entering a past date (retrospective registration). The resulting entry will look exactly like any other entry to the external users. The resulting LSID of a retrospective entry can then be copied and pasted into the reissued pdf, while the original pdf is wiped off. There is no difference between a prospective entry and a retrospective entry being shown by ZooBank. With current technology, it is totally possible to generate an LSID for a past date in ZooBank, display it as a past date and display that LSID and a past date on a newer pdf. A group of people could concertedly hack priority and make it look as it never happened. So no, your statement "The key point with a case like that described above is that ZooBank itself will record the date of registration as 04.01.2020" is incorrect. Internally, maybe, externally, we will see any date that was manually entered.

 

Also, thank you for pointing out your concern with the "backdating problem" that you mentioned. I think that "backdating problem" is what I described and what you described is "delayed publication", something that we already have dealt with over centuries of printed publication. Maybe we need an analysis on delayed publication in the digital context.

 

Cheers,

Charlie

On 04.01.2020, the publisher realizes the mistake, registers the publication in ZooBank, directly edits version 1, adds the ZooBank LSID, and reissues the paper but keeps the same date of 01.01.2020. I don't see much point in me entering into a specific thread relevant to much wider concerns about the current state of zoological nomenclature (it is just one issue amongst thousands), however I will answer the above question. The stated date on a publication does not have to be correct. There needs to be a date specified, but it is not required to be correct. The key point with a case like that described above is that ZooBank itself will record the date of registration as 04.01.2020 and so, all other things being equal, that will be the actual date of (electronic) publication, even if  the date on the publication is 01.01.2020.

 

However, a slightly more disturbing possibility is that something like this might happen: the date on the publication is 01.01.2020, the date of ZooBank registration is 04.01.2020 (and the stated date of publication on ZooBank is 01.01.2020 or 04.01.2020), but the publication didn't actually go online until 10.01.2020 and there is nothing on the publisher website to indicate the date that it actually went online. I'm not sure how the actual publication date could be determined down the track?? Anyone? We can call this a "backdating problem".

Art. 8.5.3.3. The following are examples of *inadmissible errors*: (...) A

publisher discovers errors in a work and *reissues it* to correct those

errors, *but* instead of registering the new edition, *uses the original

ZooBank number*; the revised edition is not available because it was not

separately registered.

 

This is a bit confusing and misleading. There may be dozens (hundreds?) of

"editions" of an unpublished work prior to the first edition of the work

that

fulfills the requirement for publication under the Code. This is true for

paper

publications as well (draft manuscripts, reviewer copies, galley proofs,

etc.).

The existence of these prior unpublished (in the sense of the Code) works

does

not in any way impact the availability of a work that is eventually

published in

accordance with the Code. From the perspective of the Code, these previous

unpublished editions do not exist, and therefore have no impact whatsoever

on

the existence of a later published work.

 

For example, a ZooBank LSID is created for a work intended to be published

electronically. That LSID appears within the galley proofs, which itself is

not

published in the sense of the Code. The work is later issued in a way that

is

Code compliant. The existence of the LSID within the galley proofs (un

unpublished work) does not therefore require that the published edition

must be

registered anew.

 

This is a simple example of a very complex problem that will be fixed in

the 5th

Edition; but the point is that the last example under Art. 8.5.3.3 (quoted

above) can be very misleading. In a situation such as the one that has been

described in this thread, we DO NOT WANT people to create a new registration

record in ZooBank. That would only apply for a truly different work (like a

corrigendum, which itself must contain all the elements necessary for

conferring

nomenclatural availability), but which itself is a completely different work

from a previously "published" (but technically non-Code-compliant) work.

 

But the fact that these examples exist underscores that the separation of

requirements in two separate places (i.e., both within the work itself and

within the ZooBank registration record) leads to myriad complexities, which

are

unintended consequences of the Amendment to the Code to allow for electronic

publication. I'll stay off my usual soapbox on this point (for now).

 

Aloha, Rich
